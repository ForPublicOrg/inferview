## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 30 minutes">
"Our replicas take minutes to start, and the autoscaler keeps arriving after the queue. How many replicas above current load would you keep running, and what does that cost?"
</Callout>

The service (scenario): a chat endpoint on one model, one replica per GPU node.

- A load test found that one replica holds 48 concurrent streams at the inter-token latency objective, and a request spends 12 s in the system on average.
- The autoscaler is an HPA on waiting requests per replica, scraped every 30 s, with the threshold required to hold for 60 s. From load exceeding capacity to the scale-up decision takes about 90 s.
- Measured cold start on this fleet: GPU node provisioning 150 s, image pull 240 s, weight load 90 s, engine start and warm-up 60 s.
- Weekday traffic from last month's logs, requests per second:

- Outside the morning ramp, the largest rise in any 10 minutes last quarter was 60 requests per second.

For comparison, published figures: one engineer's measurement on GKE put the image pull for a 28 GB vLLM image on a fresh node at 236 s (April 2026), and a published interview prompt assumes 5 to 10 minutes to load weights on a cold start.

Choose your assumptions and estimate the number of replicas you would keep running above what current load needs, all day. Say what it costs in replica-hours a day, which term dominates, and what would change your answer.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>One replica serves 48 / 12 = 4 requests a second. The autoscaler's reaction time is the decision plus the start: 90 + 150 + 240 + 90 + 60 = 630 s, 10.5 minutes, and nothing it orders helps before then. The steepest quarter-hour is 06:45 to 07:00, +80 requests a second, 5.33 a minute; over 10.5 minutes that is 56 requests a second, 14 replicas. The largest unscheduled rise, 60 in 10 minutes, all arrives inside the reaction time: 15 replicas. I would hold 15 replicas spare all day, schedule a floor that tracks the morning curve, and cache the image on node boot disks, which cuts the reaction time to 6.5 minutes and the spare to 10. At 15 the cost is 15 × 24 = 360 replica-hours a day. The dominant term is reaction time times the steepest rise; the assumption that flips the answer is whether a spike can land on top of the ramp.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Capacity per replica.</strong> Little's law: 48 streams in flight at 12 s each is 48 / 12 = 4 requests a second at the load-test limit. Running replicas at 85% of that, 3.4 a second, is a defensible choice that buys room for longer-than-tested requests; it raises every count below by about 18%.</p>
<p><strong>Reaction time, the first assumption.</strong> 630 s, or 10.5 minutes, if every scale-up provisions a node, pulls the image, loads weights and warms up. Anything that arrives faster than that has to be served by replicas already running. If you think the 240 s pull is avoidable, it is the one part the published GKE measurement also found dominant, and caching the image on the node's boot disk removes it: 6.5 minutes. Keeping nodes provisioned but empty removes the 150 s too, but with one replica per GPU node an empty node costs the same as a running replica, so it saves nothing here.</p>
<p><strong>The rise to protect against, the second assumption.</strong></p>

<p>Reasonable answers run from about 9 to about 30 replicas. The ramp is the same every weekday, so a scheduled floor that leads the curve by one reaction time takes it off the spare entirely, and the spare only has to cover the unscheduled rise. Stacking the two (29) is defensible if spikes have been seen during the ramp, and the hint asks exactly that; without that evidence it doubles the bill for an event nobody has observed.</p>
<p><strong>The dominant term and the flip.</strong> Spare is reaction time × steepest rise / capacity per replica. Halving the reaction time halves the spare; halving the capacity per replica doubles it. The assumption that flips the conclusion is whether spikes stack on the ramp: if they do, the spare doubles, and at 29 replicas the warm pool is larger than the whole 02:00 fleet of 80 / 4 = 20.</p>
<p><strong>Cost, and the constraint conflict.</strong> 15 spare replicas all day is 360 replica-hours a day. At 02:00 the fleet is 20 + 15 = 35 replicas, so the spare is 75% overhead; at the 500 request-a-second plateau it is 125 + 15 = 140, 12%. The conflict is cost against the TTFT objective: the night spare is the expensive part and protects against a spike that, on last quarter's data, came outside the ramp. Holding a smaller spare at night is a choice you can make if the 60-request rise never happened at night; write down that you made it.</p>
<p><strong>Where this estimate fails.</strong> It treats the cold start as a constant. When the cloud has no GPU stock in the zone, provisioning does not take 150 s, it fails, and the warm spare is the only capacity there is until stock returns. It also assumes requests keep their 12 s average; if a model update lengthens answers, capacity per replica drops and the same spare covers a smaller spike. Re-derive the spare whenever the load test is re-run.</p>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

### Choices that are not reasonable

- Zero spare, trusting the autoscaler: through the steepest ramp the shortfall grows at 5.33 requests a second per minute for 10.5 minutes, and the backlog by the time the first new replica serves is about 0.089 × 630² / 2 ≈ 17,600 requests.
- Holding the peak fleet all day: 500 / 4 = 125 replicas against a 02:00 need of 20, a spare of 105, for protection the scheduled floor gives for free on a predictable ramp.
- Counting an empty provisioned GPU node as cheaper than a warm replica, when the GPU is what you pay for.
- Sizing the spare in GPU utilisation percent; the autoscaler does not act on it, and it does not measure work done.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: finance asks you to cut the spare to 5 replicas at night. Which TTFT objective do you expect to miss, on which kind of night, and who signs that?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in a quarter of production data to drop the spare from 15 to 10 without caching the image?* No unscheduled rise above 40 requests a second in any 10 minutes, and a measured reaction time from the HPA's own events, not the scenario's 90 s.
- *Operating: the scale-down stabilization window is the HPA default of 5 minutes. Traffic dips for six minutes at 09:00 every day. What happens at 09:06, and what window do you set?*

## Common mistakes

- Measuring the cold start as weight load only, and leaving out node provisioning, image pull and warm-up.
- Holding spare for the morning ramp instead of scheduling a floor for a curve that repeats every weekday.
- Leaving out the autoscaler's own decision delay from the reaction time.
- Quoting the spare without its cost in replica-hours, so nobody can trade it against the objective.
