## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Critique, 30 minutes">
"This router design is approved by the feature team and goes out Monday. Review it: what is wrong, what would you require, and what can ship as it is?"
</Callout>

The design, constructed for this problem. The assistant answers questions about a customer's workspace (documents, tickets, code snippets) and runs on Claude Sonnet 5.5 today with automatic prompt caching.

```text
ROUTER v1                                        cost work item FIN-88

Goal
  Cut model spend on the workspace assistant by 60% this quarter
  without a quality regression.

Routing rule
  tokens = len(last_user_message) / 4
  if tokens < 400:  model = claude-haiku-5-5
  else:             model = claude-sonnet-5-5
  Evaluated on every turn.

Escalation
  If the small model's answer fails to parse as our response JSON,
  retry the same request on claude-sonnet-5-5 and return that answer.

Expected savings
  71% of requests have a last user message under 400 tokens.
  Haiku 5.5 costs 1/20 of Sonnet 5.5 ($0.10 vs $2 input, $0.50 vs $10 output).
  Savings = 71% x (1 - 1/20) = 67% of spend.

Quality evaluation
  200 requests sampled at random from last week's traffic, answered by both
  models. Claude Sonnet 5.5 judged each pair, with Haiku's answer shown as
  answer A: "Is answer A as good as or better than answer B?"
  Result: 191 of 200 (95.5%) yes. No meaningful quality difference.

Rollout
  100% of traffic on Monday behind flag router_v1.
  Dashboard: share of requests routed to Haiku, daily spend.
```

List the flaws, then open the checklist.

## Answer

The flaws, roughly in the order a reviewer finds them. The ones in the middle are the ones a first read misses.

**The rollout.** All traffic on day one, with a dashboard that shows the saving and nothing that shows quality. There is no holdout to compare against and no condition under which the flag goes off. Even a good router needs that.

**The evaluation proves less than it says.** 191 of 200 is 95.5%, and the 95% interval is about ± 1.96 × √(0.955 × 0.045 / 200) ≈ ± 2.9 points, so the data allows up to about 7% of answers being worse. The sample is all traffic, so about 29% of it is requests the router would send to Sonnet anyway; the question that matters, how Haiku does on the turns it would get, is answered by about 140 pairs, not 200. The judge always saw Haiku's answer first, so any position preference goes one way, and nobody checked the judge against people on this task.

**The escalation catches the wrong failure.** A parse failure is a format error. A wrong answer in valid JSON returns to the user. FrugalGPT escalates on a score of the query and the answer together, a model trained to predict whether the answer is correct, which is the part this design leaves out. Escalated requests also pay for both models and wait for both generations in series, and none of that is in the saving.

**The router measures the wrong thing.** `len(last_user_message)` ignores the history, the retrieved documents and any attachment. "And the second one?" after a 60,000-token conversation about a contract is a short prompt by this rule. Characters divided by four also undercounts code and many non-English scripts. Underneath both, nothing shows that length predicts difficulty for this product. The design needs a confusion matrix: the routed bucket against whether Haiku's answer was acceptable, on a sample of the routed traffic.

**The saving is computed on requests, not spend.** 71% of requests is not 71% of spend. Turns with short messages may carry long histories, or may be the cheap turns of the day; the design does not say which. If those turns are 35% of spend, the same arithmetic gives 33%, not 67%.

**The price ratio is not 1/20 on every line.** Input and output are 1/20. Cached input is not: Sonnet 5.5 reads its cache at $0.10 per million and Haiku 5.5 at $0.01, a tenth. And Haiku 5.5 prices prompts over 100,000 tokens at $0.50 and $2.50, a quarter of Sonnet's rates. A conversation-heavy product with caching saves well under 95% per routed request.

**Routing every turn defeats the cache.** A conversation that goes Sonnet, Haiku, Haiku, Sonnet builds a separate cache on each model, and the Sonnet cache is not refreshed while Haiku answers. If the Haiku turns take more than five minutes, the return to Sonnet rewrites the whole history: 40,000 tokens of history is a $0.10 write at $2.50 per million instead of a $0.004 read at $0.10, 25 times more, on the expensive turn. The answer's tone and format can also change from one turn to the next.

**Two judgement calls.** Length-based routing is acceptable if it is sticky per conversation, measured on a stratified sample of the routed bucket, and watched through a holdout. The parse-failure retry is acceptable if its rate and cost are counted in the saving and its latency fits the budget.

**What to require: two defensible designs.**

- *A sticky heuristic.* Decide the model on the first turn from the full prompt length and the feature (search answer, ticket summary, code question), and keep it for the conversation. Measure it on a stratified sample of the routed bucket, enough pairs to see the regression you care about: to bound a worse-answer rate near 5% within ± 2 points needs about 1.96² × 0.05 × 0.95 / 0.02² ≈ 460 pairs, judged in both orders, with a human-labelled subset to check the judge. Keep 5% of conversations on Sonnet 5.5 as a holdout. The cost: it leaves money on the table and misroutes hard questions that arrive in short first turns, for the whole conversation.
- *A cascade with a learned scorer,* in the FrugalGPT shape: Haiku answers first, a small scorer judges the answer, and low scores go to Sonnet. The cost: the scorer needs labelled examples from this product's own traffic, and FrugalGPT lists that and distribution shift as its limitations; escalated requests wait for two generations; and the scorer is a model with its own evaluation and drift to own. RouteLLM's routers, trained on preference data, avoid the serial call but bring the same labelling and drift questions.

The margin you accept is a number you choose and defend in the design: one to two points of worse answers on the routed bucket is a margin a few hundred pairs can bound; zero cannot be measured at any sample size, and five is a product decision, not an engineering one. The constraint conflict is the quarter's cost target against a quality regression you can only bound, not rule out.

I would ship the sticky heuristic with the holdout this quarter and collect labels for a scorer from the holdout's pairs. What can ship as written: the flag, the dashboard (with a quality line added), and the retry, once its rate is counted.

**Where the recommendation fails.** A sticky decision on the first turn is wrong for every conversation that starts easy and turns hard: "summarise this ticket", then "why does the customer's stack trace point at our SDK?". The holdout shows the average gap and hides that pattern, so the review sample needs conversations with a late hard turn, and the product needs a way to move a conversation up, such as a user-visible "use the stronger model" action, which then becomes a metric of its own.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the sticky design and the holdout cut the projected saving from 67% to an unknown number, and finance committed 60% for the quarter. What do you commit to in writing, and which risk do you accept to get closer?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in production to route per turn again?* Measured cache-write cost and quality on conversations that switch models, from a holdout that switches, showing both within the agreed margin.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants the learned scorer now, the other wants the heuristic for a quarter first. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Count the labelled examples you have from this product's traffic; if they are not enough to train and test a scorer on the routed distribution, the heuristic is how you get them.
- *The routed share drifts from 71% to 85% after a new feature launches. What does that tell you, and what do you check before celebrating?*

## Common mistakes

- Accepting a saving computed on request share.
- Treating the last message as the prompt.
- Reading 191 of 200 as "no difference" without the interval or the routed subset.
- Using a JSON parse check as a quality check.
- Missing what per-turn model switching does to the prompt cache.
- Rejecting routing outright instead of naming what can ship, which leaves the team to ship v1 unchanged.
