## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Debug, 30 minutes">
The question as candidate-prep lists put it: "p99 latency doubled after a deploy with no model change." Here are the deploy, the dashboard and one measurement. Which change explains the regression, what else in the deploy is wrong, and what do you ship tonight?
</Callout>

The endpoint streams chat answers from vLLM on six replicas. Most prompts are short; a few times a minute a user pastes a long document. The SLOs (scenario): p99 inter-token latency under 80 ms, p99 time to first token under 2 s. Since Tuesday's deploy, users report answers that freeze for a moment mid-stream. The change request says: raise engine limits for next month's launch, since the load test showed more throughput.

```diff
-image: serving-base:2026.10.1
+image: serving-base:2026.10.2
 engine:
   model: same weights, same revision
-  max_num_seqs: 128
+  max_num_seqs: 256
-  max_num_batched_tokens: 2048
+  max_num_batched_tokens: 16384
   enable_prefix_caching: true
   gpu_memory_utilization: 0.90
```

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The step budget. With chunked prefill, `max_num_batched_tokens` caps the tokens in one engine step, decodes first and prompt chunks after. At 2,048 a pasted document is fed in chunks of about 2,000 tokens and each step takes about 52 ms; at 16,384 most of it lands in one step, and the step table puts that at 120 to 215 ms. Every streaming user waits for that step before their next token, so p99 ITL doubles while p50 does not move, and TTFT improves because prefill gets about twice the tokens per second. The new `max_num_seqs` is a second defect that has not fired yet. Tonight I ship the budget back to 2,048 and `max_num_seqs` back to 128, knowing that puts p99 TTFT back at 1.9 s against a 2 s objective, and I write that down.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Why the budget, from the rows.</strong> ITL p50 moved by 1 ms and p99 doubled, so the regression is in rare steps, not every step. Throughput rose and TTFT fell, so the engine is not slower; it is spending some steps differently. The image change would move every step; prefix hits, preemptions, KV usage, replicas and waiting requests rule out caching, memory and capacity. That leaves the two limits, and only one of them changes what a single step contains. The prefill rate makes the TTFT gain concrete: a full step at the old budget carries about 2,000 prompt tokens in 52 ms, about 38,000 tokens a second; 16,000 tokens in 215 ms is about 74,000. The big budget doubles prefill speed and pays for it in the decode gaps of everyone sharing the step.</p>
<p><strong>The ranking.</strong> First, the step budget: it explains the symptom. Second, `max_num_seqs` from 128 to 256: the busiest minute held 112 running requests, under both caps, so it changed nothing this week, but the launch is when the batch grows past 128, and the step table shows the 110-sequence row already 8 to 11 ms slower than the 60-sequence row at every prompt size. Nobody has measured step times at 200 sequences, and that is what the launch will run. Third, the image: probably harmless, since p50 did not move, but unreviewed. Ask for its diff before closing the incident.</p>
<p><strong>What the evidence does not show, and what to collect.</strong> The dashboard shows correlation with a deploy. It does not show that the slow gaps coincide with long prompts. Collect `vllm:iteration_tokens_total` before and after (a step-size distribution with a new tail near 16,000 confirms it); per-request ITL p99 split by whether a prompt over, say, 6,000 tokens arrived during the request; and a replay of an hour of logged traffic against the old and new budget on one replica. Without them, ship the revert anyway: it is one line, it returns a configuration that met the ITL objective for weeks, and it is reversible.</p>
<p><strong>The value you choose, and the conflict.</strong> The ITL objective is 80 ms at p99. A full step must stay under it at the batch size you expect, with room for jitter. At 110 decoding sequences, 2,000 prompt tokens give 61 ms and 4,000 give 84 ms. Anything from 2,048 to about 3,000 is defensible (interpolating, about 72 ms at 3,000); 4,096 is not, because the busiest minute already breaks the objective with it. The TTFT objective pulls the other way: the smaller budget halves prefill speed, and p99 TTFT was 1.9 s against 2 s before this deploy. Latency between tokens and latency to the first token cannot both be bought with this one knob.</p>
<p><strong>Two defensible structural answers, each with a cost.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><em>Keep a large budget and cap per-request prefill.</em> vLLM's `long_prefill_token_threshold` limits the tokens one prompt takes per step, so short prompts still share a big budget and a pasted document is fed in small chunks. Cost: the cap is per request, so three long prompts arriving together can still fill the step, and long prompts take more steps to their first token. It needs its own replay test, and the option is read from the main-branch source, so check that the version you run has it.</li>
<li><em>Route long prompts to their own replicas.</em> The gateway sends prompts above a length threshold to a pool tuned for prefill throughput; the chat pool keeps a small budget. Cost: two pools to size and autoscale, a threshold to maintain, and the long-prompt pool idles between documents.</li>
</ul>
<p>I would revert tonight and test the per-request cap next week, because it changes one setting and keeps one pool; the separate pool becomes the answer if long documents grow past a share of traffic the replay says the cap cannot absorb.</p>
<p><strong>Where the recommendation fails.</strong> The revert restores last month's state, and last month's state had 0.1 s of TTFT margin. Any growth in long documents, or the launch traffic itself, breaches the TTFT objective with no deploy to blame. The step table was also measured at 60 and 110 sequences; it says nothing about 128 running sequences plus a 2,000-token chunk, which is where the launch puts the busiest minute. Before the launch the team needs a load test at launch concurrency with the real prompt-length mix, and a written note saying which of the two objectives gives way first if the budget cannot hold both.</p>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

**Why the wrong picks are tempting.** A larger batch is the textbook cause of slower decode steps, and `max_num_seqs` did double, but the running count never reached the old cap. Image changes are the usual suspect when nothing else is visible, and a kernel regression would show up as a uniform slowdown, which is not what the p50 row shows. Broken prefix caching is the most common latency regression after a prompt or tokenizer change; here the hit rate is flat. Preemption pauses look exactly like a stream freezing, but the counter is at zero.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: product says the launch needs the TTFT gain, and the revert takes p99 TTFT back to 1.9 s against a 2 s objective. Which objective do you let slip during the launch week, and who signs that in writing?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in a replay before you raise the budget again?* Per-request ITL p99 under 80 ms at launch concurrency, with the logged long-prompt mix and the per-request prefill cap on, across at least a day of traffic shapes.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants separate long-prompt replicas now, the other wants the per-request cap and one pool. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Agree on the share of long prompts each design must absorb, replay both at that share, and pick the one that holds both objectives at the lower GPU count.

## Common mistakes

- Reading mean latency, which barely moved, or one blended latency number that hides that TTFT improved while ITL regressed.
- Reverting the whole deploy without naming the line, so the next change request repeats it.
- Stopping at the cause and leaving `max_num_seqs` at 256 into a launch nobody has load-tested at that concurrency.
- Choosing the step budget from the vLLM docs' example values instead of from step times measured at the batch size the service runs.
