## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Debug, 30 minutes">
"Finance flagged it this morning: the model bill for the support assistant is three times what it was six weeks ago, and traffic is up 40%. Here is what we have. What happened, what else is wrong, and what do you do before month-end close on Friday?"
</Callout>

The assistant answers questions inside a multi-tenant support product. It runs on Claude Haiku 5.5 with automatic prompt caching, the same model for the whole period. The weekly usage export (synthetic, written for this problem; dollars at the provider's billed amounts):

```text
                          week of 2026-08-31            week of 2026-10-12
requests                        8,400,000                    11,760,000
                          tokens (B)    cost ($)       tokens (B)    cost ($)
cache read                    75.600      756.00          177.652     5,091.72
cache write (5 min)           12.600    1,575.00           23.158     4,004.75
input (uncached)              12.600    1,260.00           29.400     3,680.00
output                         3.360    1,680.00            4.741     3,036.50
total                                   5,271.00                     15,812.97
```

The product dashboard shows median input tokens per request (cache read, cache write and uncached input together) of 12,000 in the first week and 13,000 in the last.

Every change to the assistant's config in the six weeks, as one diff:

```diff
 model: claude-haiku-5-5
-retrieval_top_k: 8
+retrieval_top_k: 12            # recall complaints, SUP-2291
 chunk_tokens: 250
-history_max_tokens: 24000
+history_max_tokens: 150000
+history_summarise_above: 150000
-max_tokens: 1024
+max_tokens: 4096               # long answers were cut off
 system_prompt_template: |
-  You are the support assistant for this workspace.
+  You are the support assistant for {tenant_name}.
   {static_instructions}         # about 4,000 tokens, identical for every tenant
```

Which change explains most of the increase?

## Answer

**The cause.** Divide each cost line by its tokens. In the first week every line sits on Haiku 5.5's list price: $0.01 per million cache reads, $0.125 writes, $0.10 input, $0.50 output. In the last week cache reads cost $5,091.72 / 177,652 = $0.0287 per million, output $3,036.50 / 4,741 = $0.640, and cache writes and uncached input also sit above their list prices. Token volume cannot do that; only the price can. Haiku 5.5 bills a request whose prompt is over 100,000 tokens at five times the rates on every line, cache reads and output included, and a prompt's length counts cache reads and writes. If read tokens are billed at either $0.01 or $0.05, the share at $0.05 is (0.0287 − 0.01) / 0.04 ≈ 47%; for output, (0.640 − 0.50) / 2.00 ≈ 7%. Seven percent of output is close to the share of requests: about one request in sixteen now crosses the line, and those requests carry nearly half of all cached history.

`history_max_tokens` went from 24,000 to 150,000, with summarisation only above 150,000. Short sessions never reach either limit, which is why the median moved from 12,000 to 13,000 and the dashboard looked normal. Long sessions now carry up to 150,000 tokens of history and cross 100,000 on every turn after that point. The export's means show it where the median cannot: total input per request went from 100.8B / 8.4M = 12,000 to 230.2B / 11.76M ≈ 19,600.

**How the $10,542 weekly increase splits.** Traffic alone: $5,271 × 1.4 = $7,379, so $2,108. Applying the last week's short-request mix to every request at the under-100,000 prices gives $9,096, so the two secondary changes add about $1,717. Billing the long requests' extra history at those same prices gives $9,982, so the extra tokens add $885. The remaining $5,831, 55% of the increase, is the price tier. The history change accounts for $885 + $5,831 = $6,716 of it.

**The other changes, ranked.**

- *`retrieval_top_k` 8 to 12* ranks second: four more 250-token chunks is 1,000 uncached input tokens on every request, $1,176 a week at the base rate. It is a deliberate trade for recall that nobody priced, not a bug, and it belongs in the decision record for SUP-2291 with its cost.
- *`{tenant_name}` at the top of the system prompt* ranks third and is the cheapest to fix. The 4,000-token static block used to be one cached prefix shared by every tenant. Now each tenant has its own, and a tenant with less than one request every five minutes rewrites it at 12.5 times the read price. In this export it moved about 400 tokens a request from reads to writes, about $541 a week. Moving the tenant name below the static block, or into the first user turn, restores the shared prefix.
- *`max_tokens` 1,024 to 4,096* is not a cost change. Output is billed on tokens generated, and output per request is 4.741B / 11.76M ≈ 403 against 400 before. Its own risk is latency and a larger reservation in any limiter that reserves `max_tokens`.

**What the evidence does not show.** The export has no per-request lengths and no split by price tier, and the 47% is an inference from two prices, not a measurement. Collect: per-request usage records for a day in each week, with `input_tokens`, `cache_creation_input_tokens` and `cache_read_input_tokens` summed into prompt length, and the share above 100,000 per day against the date the history change shipped; cost by tenant, to see whether a few tenants with long sessions carry the increase; and the cache-write share by tenant before and after the template change.

**What to do before Friday, and the decision.** Two answers are defensible.

- *Cap the prompt below the tier.* Summarise history above a budget under 100,000 tokens, behind a flag, so it can be turned off. The cap is a number you choose: the application counts tokens with its own estimate, so it needs a margin. Something between 85,000 and 95,000 is reasonable; 99,000 is not, because an estimate a few percent low puts requests back over the line, and those requests are exactly the expensive ones. Cost: long sessions lose detail, and those are the users whose "it forgot what I said an hour ago" complaints led to the change. Each summarisation is also a model call.
- *Keep the long history and price it.* If the long sessions belong to enterprise tenants on a plan that can carry about $5,800 a week of tier premium, attribute it per tenant and put it in front of whoever owns that plan's margin. Moving those sessions to a model without a length tier does not help here: on Sonnet 5.5 the same long request costs 112,000 × $0.10 + 3,000 × $2.50 + 2,500 × $2 + 450 × $10 per million ≈ $0.028, against $0.00985 on Haiku 5.5's upper tier.

The constraint conflict is cost against answer quality for the sessions that matter most to the customers who complained. I would ship the cap at 90,000 behind the flag today, fix the tenant-name template in the same release, and take the per-tenant numbers to the product owner for the long-session decision. With the cap and the template fix, and nothing else changed, the week's bill comes down to roughly $9,000 to $10,000, about 1.8 times the first week against 1.4 times the traffic.

**Where the cap fails.** It fails in two ways. The summary drops what users come back for, an order number or an error message quoted an hour earlier, and the support team sees it as the old complaint returning. And the cap is measured by the application's token estimate: if that estimate runs low on some content, such as code or non-English text, requests still cross 100,000. The check is the provider's own usage fields: the share of requests with prompt length over 100,000 has to be zero the day after the release, not "lower".

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the product lead says the history change halved "the assistant forgot" complaints and refuses the cap. Finance wants the bill under twice the first week's by close. Which half do you give up, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see to remove the cap?* A summary that keeps the facts users refer back to, measured on a set of real long sessions replayed with and without it, and a per-tenant price that covers the tier for the tenants who keep full history.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants to revert the history change today, the other wants the per-request histogram first. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* The arithmetic on the export already shows a price change that only the tier explains; the histogram decides the cap's value, not whether to act. Ship the flag off, turn it on for the long-session cohort, and let the histogram set the number.
- *How would the gateway have caught this in the first week?* An alert on effective price per million tokens by line item for each feature, which moves the day a tier is crossed, rather than on total spend, which moves with traffic.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the median prompt length and concluding prompts did not grow.
- Explaining a price-per-token increase with token volume.
- Blaming `max_tokens` without checking output tokens per request.
- Treating the top_k change as a defect to revert rather than a priced trade-off to record.
- Capping at 99,999 tokens by the application's own count.
- Waiting for perfect per-request data before acting on arithmetic that already rules out every other cause.
