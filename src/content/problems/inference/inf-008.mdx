## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Tokens per pass went from 1.0 to 1.8. How much longer did each step get?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Why does the same change help overnight and hurt at peak?</p></details>

## Answer

The step became compute bound under load. Before, each step produced one token per sequence in 33 ms. After, each target pass yields 1.8 tokens per sequence, but TPOT is 42 ms per token, so each pass plus its draft steps takes about 1.8 x 42 = 76 ms, 2.3 times longer than before. A 1.8 times gain in tokens per pass cannot cover a 2.3 times longer step. At batch 4 overnight the step is bandwidth bound, the extra positions are nearly free, and TPOT drops from 14 to 8 ms, which is why the single-user test looked good.

The acceptance numbers agree with the formula. With alpha 0.45 and a draft length of 5, the expected tokens per pass is (1 - 0.45^6) / 0.55 = 1.80. Each pass verifies 6 positions for each of 96 sequences, 576 positions, yet only about 0.8 of the 5 drafted tokens per sequence are accepted on average (0.45 + 0.45^2 + ... + 0.45^5), so about 84% of the drafted tokens are thrown away.

Why the others tempt. A bigger draft model sounds like it would raise acceptance, but it raises the cost of every draft step at exactly the load where compute is short. The memory explanation is a real risk in general, but the batch and preemption rows rule it out here. The distribution and prefix-cache explanations would show up as longer outputs and slower TTFT, and neither row moved.

Fixes, in order: gate speculation on load (on below a batch-size threshold, off above it); shorten the draft to 2 or 3, which lowers wasted verification at this acceptance rate; try a cheaper or better-matched draft (n-gram for repetitive outputs, or a trained head such as EAGLE-3) and compare acceptance; and measure goodput, not just throughput, for each setting.

## Follow-ups

- *What draft length would you try at alpha 0.45?* With a draft costing 5% of the target, Leviathan's walltime formula peaks at a draft length of 3 for this alpha, at about 1.5 times, and that is the low-load best case.
- *How would you have caught this before production?* A load test that sweeps concurrency with and without speculation, plotting throughput and TPOT percentiles.
- *Which metric tells you speculation is worth keeping?* Accepted tokens per verified position, next to throughput, split by batch size.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the overnight improvement as proof that the peak regression is noise.
- Recommending a larger draft model to raise acceptance without accounting for its cost.
- Assuming speculative decoding changes the outputs.
- Looking only at tokens per pass and ignoring step time.
