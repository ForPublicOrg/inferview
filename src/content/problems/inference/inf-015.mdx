## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 50 minutes">
"Design an LLM inference API" with streaming and batch, three model sizes, and the follow-up that matters here: how do you autoscale and keep your SLOs when a new replica takes minutes to start?
</Callout>

The published prompt gives the load: 10 million calls a day, a peak of 1,000 requests per second, an average answer of 500 tokens at no less than 30 tokens per second, TTFT under 500 ms, and 7B, 70B and 405B models. Its follow-up puts a cold start at 5 to 10 minutes. What you learn by asking (scenario):

- Traffic by model: 7B 60%, 70B 35%, 405B 5%. Enterprise customers send 40% of requests and have a contract SLO; self-serve sends 60% and has none.
- A load test found the most concurrent streams one replica holds with p99 inter-token latency under 33 ms: 7B on 1 GPU, 128; 70B on 4 GPUs, 64; 405B on 8 GPUs, 24.
- A one-year reservation of 640 GPUs is signed. On-demand GPUs arrive in about 8 minutes when the cloud has stock; twice last quarter a request went unfilled for over an hour.
- The gateway (auth, billing, per-tenant limits) belongs to the platform team. You can configure routes and limits on it, not rewrite it.
- Five engineers, ten weeks to general availability. Finance wants the reserved GPUs at least 60% busy averaged over a day.

Write the SLOs, size the pools, specify the autoscaling policy and what happens to a spike that arrives faster than a replica can start, and say what you give up. Write the rollout plan for the autoscaler itself, with its rollback triggers.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what share of the day's requests arrives in the peak hour, and how far ahead the peak can be predicted.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask which tier's traffic the reservation has to protect, and what the contract says happens when the SLO is missed.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Three pools behind the existing gateway, one per model, each running continuous batching with paged KV. SLOs per tier, not one latency number: for enterprise, 99% of requests with TTFT under 500 ms and 99% with a mean inter-token gap under 33 ms, over a four-week window. Little's law sizes the peak: 1,000 requests a second times about 17.2 s in the system is 17,200 streams, which the measured concurrency turns into 749 GPUs at the load-test limit and 933 at 80% of it. The reservation covers the enterprise share of that peak, 373 GPUs, with room to spare; self-serve rides on on-demand capacity scheduled ahead of the daily ramp and is degraded or shed when the cloud has no stock. Pools scale on waiting requests and running batch size, never GPU utilisation, and scale in slowly because each replica costs 8 minutes to replace. Batch jobs fill the night, which is the only way the reservation gets near 60% busy.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The arithmetic, in front of the room.</strong> 10,000,000 / 86,400 = 116 requests a second on average, so the 1,000 peak is 8.6 times the mean. Time in the system at the floor rate is 0.5 + 500 / 30 = 17.2 s, so at peak 1,000 × 17.2 = 17,200 streams are in flight. Split by model and divided by measured concurrency:</p>

<p>The headroom is the quantity you choose. Running at the load-test limit means p99 ITL sits at 33 ms with no room for a longer-than-tested prompt mix; 80% buys room for bursts and for requests that hold more KV than the test's average. Anything from 70% to 90% is defensible if you say what it buys; 100% is not, and neither is sizing without a measured concurrency.</p>
<p><strong>SLOs and the error budget.</strong> SLIs as good over total requests, per tier: TTFT under 500 ms, per-request mean inter-token gap under 33 ms (the 30 tokens per second floor), and success. Enterprise SLO 99% on each latency SLI and 99.9% on success over a rolling four weeks; self-serve gets internal objectives and no promise. Enterprise sends 4 million requests a day, so the latency budget is 1% of 112 million requests over four weeks, 1.12 million slow requests. The written error budget policy: when enterprise has spent half its budget, engine and model upgrades stop until the burn rate falls; when it has spent all of it, self-serve is shed first during any shortfall.</p>
<p><strong>The decision: what the reservation covers.</strong> 640 reserved GPUs cannot hold the 933-GPU peak. Two defensible answers:</p>
<ul>
<li><em>Protect enterprise on the reservation, put self-serve on on-demand.</em> Enterprise at peak needs 40% of 933, about 373 GPUs, so the reservation covers it with 267 GPUs of warm spare. Self-serve needs about 560 more at peak; scheduled scale-ahead requests them 30 minutes before the daily ramp. Cost: when the cloud has no stock, as happened twice last quarter, self-serve 70B traffic falls back to the 7B pool with a shorter max_tokens, then to 429 with Retry-After. Self-serve users see a worse model at peak on those days.</li>
<li><em>Raise the reservation to the full peak.</em> No stock risk and no degraded mode. Cost: about 293 more reserved GPUs that sit idle most of the day; the average load is 116 / 1,000 = 11.6% of peak, so even with 640 GPUs the streaming traffic alone keeps them about 749 × 0.116 / 640 = 14% busy, against finance's 60%.</li>
</ul>
<p>I would take the first. It honours the contract with reserved capacity and leaves the risk where there is no promise. That is the constraint conflict in this design: finance's utilisation target against an SLO that needs idle headroom. The batch mode is how the two meet: jobs with a 24-hour window are drained into the pools' spare replicas at night and paused first when the waiting queue grows. Reaching 60% means about 384 of 640 GPUs busy on average, so batch has to fill roughly 300 GPUs' worth of the day; whether that demand exists is a question for product, not for the autoscaler.</p>
<p><strong>The autoscaling policy.</strong> Per pool, an HPA on `vllm:num_requests_waiting` per replica with a threshold found by load test (GKE suggests starting between 3 and 5), and on `vllm:num_requests_running` with a target just below the measured maximum. Scale-up stabilization stays at zero. Scale-down does not: the default 5-minute window removes replicas that cost 8 minutes to replace, so I would raise it to 30 minutes and remove at most 10% of a pool per step. A scheduled floor per pool follows the daily curve, because a reactive signal that fires when the queue forms is 8 minutes late by construction. KV usage and preemptions alert, they do not scale.</p>
<p><strong>A spike faster than a cold start.</strong> Between the queue forming and a new replica serving, the only capacity is what is already running. Enterprise has the 267 reserved GPUs of spare; self-serve has whatever on-demand has already arrived. The gateway's per-tenant limits do the rest: self-serve concurrency is capped per tenant, and past the cap it degrades, then sheds. Shortening the start helps every option: the image cached on the node and weights streamed from storage close to the GPUs attack the parts that repeat on every start.</p>
<p><strong>What you give up.</strong> A latency promise for self-serve; the 405B pool sized for its peak only on days the cloud has stock; finance's 60% unless batch demand appears; and autoscaling that reacts to surprise traffic, since only scheduled capacity is on time.</p>
<p><strong>Rollout plan for the autoscaler, as written.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Weeks 1 and 2: shadow mode. The policy computes desired replicas per pool every 30 s and logs them next to what the current static fleet does. Pass: the policy never asks for fewer replicas than were needed to keep the enterprise TTFT SLI above 99% in any hour.</li>
<li>Week 3: live on the 7B pool only. Rollback to the static fleet if the enterprise TTFT SLI on that pool falls below 99% for 30 minutes, if the pool changes direction more than four times in an hour, or if any pod stays pending for longer than 15 minutes without an alert having fired.</li>
<li>Weeks 4 to 6: the 70B pool, then 405B, with the same triggers. The static fleet stays defined for a one-command rollback until the four-week enterprise budget closes unspent.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Where this design fails.</strong> Every number above rests on one load test. If a model update makes answers 40% longer, time in the system goes from 17.2 s to 0.5 + 700 / 30 = 23.8 s, streams in flight rise by 38% at the same request rate, and enterprise now needs 373 × 1.38 = 516 of the 640 reserved GPUs, leaving 124 spare instead of 267, before anyone notices that the request rate never changed. The second failure is the scheduled scale-ahead on a day without stock: the queue signal fires, pods sit pending, and the autoscaler looks healthy while self-serve degrades for the whole peak. Alert on pending pods and unfilled capacity requests, not only on the queue, and re-run the load test on every model or engine change.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why not scale on KV cache usage?</em> It rises with longer sequences as well as with more of them, and it sits high on a healthy, well-packed replica; preemptions are the signal that it has run out.</li>
<li><em>Per-customer adapters on the 70B pool: what changes in the capacity table?</em> Each adapter load is a cold start inside a warm replica; concurrency per replica drops when many adapters are resident, so the load test has to run with the real adapter mix.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: finance says 14% busy is indefensible and wants the reservation cut from 640 to 400 GPUs. Enterprise at peak needs about 373. What do you accept in writing about the spare that remains, and which tier pays for the next stockout?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in production to move self-serve onto the reservation instead of on-demand?* Batch demand that keeps the reserved GPUs above the utilisation target at night, or a quarter without a stockout plus a measured cost of degraded self-serve sessions that exceeds the reservation price.
- *Operating: the 70B model's replacement gives answers 40% longer on your evals, and the current model's support ends in sixty days. Do you ship it on the current reservation, and which tier's objective do you degrade until the capacity arrives?*

## Common mistakes

- One latency SLO that mixes TTFT and inter-token latency, or one SLO for every tier.
- Autoscaling on GPU utilisation, which reads busy at very different loads.
- Keeping the default 5-minute scale-down window for replicas that take 8 minutes to replace.
- Capacity with no measured concurrency per replica behind it, or no headroom choice stated.
- Treating on-demand GPUs as always available, so the design has no degraded mode for a stockout.
- Building batch as a separate fleet instead of the work that fills the reservation at night.
