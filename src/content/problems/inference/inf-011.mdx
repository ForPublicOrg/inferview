## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As asked">
"You quantized your LLM, but accuracy dropped significantly. How do you minimize quantization loss?"
</Callout>

A team quantized a 70B model's weights to 4 bits to fit it on one GPU. Here is their quantization config and evaluation report.

```yaml
scheme: int4, weight-only
method: round-to-nearest, symmetric
scale granularity: one scale per weight tensor
calibration data: none
kv cache: bf16 (unchanged)
```

A weight inspection notes that in many MLP projection tensors, the largest absolute weight is about 40 times the 99th-percentile absolute weight.

Pick the root cause and the fix.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>A symmetric 4-bit format has 16 levels spread from minus the scale to plus the scale. What sets the scale here?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Why would short answers survive while long reasoning chains collapse?</p></details>

## Answer

With one scale per tensor, the scale must cover the largest weight in the whole tensor. If that weight is 40 times the 99th percentile, almost every weight lies within a small fraction of the range and gets rounded to one of a few levels near zero. The model loses most of its fine-grained information. Short factual answers degrade a little; long chains of reasoning, where small errors compound over many tokens, collapse, which is the pattern in the report.

The fixes attack that error directly. Use finer-grained scales so an outlier only stretches the range of its own group or block; GGUF's formats do this (Q8_0 stores one scale per 32 weights, and the K-quants' per-block scales are why Q4_K costs 4.5 bits per weight rather than 4). Use a calibrated method: GPTQ adjusts the remaining weights to compensate for each rounding error using second-order information from calibration data, and AWQ finds the small share of salient weight channels from activation statistics and protects them. Then re-run the same eval slices, because the loss is task specific: report per slice, not just an average. If 4 bits still fails the bar on the slices that matter, FP8 or a mixed scheme that keeps sensitive layers at higher precision is the fallback.

Why the others tempt. Mixing INT4 weights with a BF16 KV cache is normal, and quantizing the cache too would add error. Blaming the bit width is an understandable reaction, but published methods quantize large models to 3 and 4 bits; this config used the crudest available scheme. Temperature cannot be the cause under greedy decoding. The tokenizer is untouched, as the identical token counts show.

## Follow-ups

- *What calibration data would you use?* A few hundred samples resembling production prompts, including the languages and tasks you serve; calibration on generic web text can miss your domain.
- *How do you decide between INT4 and FP8?* Measure both on your evals and your hardware: FP8 halves bytes and runs natively on recent GPUs; INT4 halves them again but needs a good method and an efficient kernel.
- *Does quantization speed up prefill?* Weight-only INT4 mostly helps bandwidth-bound decode; prefill is compute bound and gains less unless the kernels compute in low precision.

## Common mistakes

- Judging a quantized model by perplexity or a short QA set alone.
- Quantizing with per-tensor scales and no calibration, then concluding 4 bits does not work.
- Reporting one averaged score that hides the collapse on reasoning tasks.
- Quantizing the KV cache in the same change, so you cannot tell which change caused the loss.
