## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As asked">
"We need to optimize inference for batch size 128. Should we use speculative decoding?"
</Callout>

You serve a dense model with continuous batching, and at peak the engine runs about 128 sequences per step. A colleague wants to turn on speculative decoding because it "doubled speed" in a single-user demo. Pick the best answer.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Speculative decoding spends extra compute to save target passes. When is that compute free?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>What part of each decode step grows with context length and never gets shared across the batch?</p></details>

## Answer

The correct answer is the conditional one. Speculative decoding works because a decode step at small batch is bandwidth bound: checking several draft tokens in one target pass costs little more than generating one. At batch 128 the weight read is already shared across 128 sequences, so the arithmetic per step is much higher. With short contexts the step can approach the compute limit, and verifying gamma + 1 positions per sequence then costs real time; every rejected draft token is wasted work, and throughput can fall, which is the failure the techinterview.org article describes. With long contexts, each sequence's KV cache is read separately every step, so the step stays bandwidth bound even at large batch, and MagicDec reports speedups up to 2.51 times for Llama3.1-8B at batch 32 to 256. The decision comes from measurement: acceptance rate, tokens per step, TPOT and goodput at your real batch sizes and context lengths, ideally with speculation gated on load.

Why the others tempt. "Always helps" comes from batch-1 demos and the true statement that outputs are unchanged. "Never above 32" is the overcorrection people learn after their first throughput regression. "Longer drafts amortise better" misreads the formula: the expected accepted length saturates at 1 / (1 - alpha), while draft cost keeps growing. "The guarantee breaks at large batch" confuses the sampling guarantee with numerical noise from different batch shapes.

## Follow-ups

- *What would you measure first?* Compute utilisation and model bandwidth utilisation at batch 128 without speculation; if the step is far from compute bound, speculation has room.
- *Which draft would you try first at large batch?* The cheapest one with decent acceptance: n-gram or suffix drafting for repetitive outputs, or a trained head such as EAGLE-3 with a short draft length.
- *Could you turn it on only some of the time?* Yes: enable it at low load and disable it above a batch-size threshold, since the low-load periods are where latency gains are largest.

## Common mistakes

- Answering yes or no without naming the variable that decides it.
- Arguing from the batch-1 demo result.
- Recommending longer drafts to "amortise" at high load.
- Saying speculation changes outputs.
