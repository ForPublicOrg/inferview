## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As asked">
"We need to optimize inference for batch size 128. Should we use speculative decoding?"
</Callout>

A dense model serves chat with continuous batching on one tensor-parallel group. At peak the engine runs about 128 sequences per step, with a mean context of 1,200 tokens. KV cache usage at batch 128 is 41% and the scheduler has not preempted anything this month. Product wants lower per-token latency for interactive users; the GPU count is fixed for the quarter.

A colleague proposes an EAGLE-style draft head with a draft length of 3. Replayed traffic gives an acceptance rate of 0.7 per drafted token. The head costs about 2 ms per draft step at batch 128 and about 1 ms at batch 8.

What happens at peak if speculation is turned on for every request?

## Answer

The arithmetic. With alpha 0.7 and a draft length of 3, the expected tokens per target pass are (1 - 0.7^4) / 0.3 = 2.53. At batch 128 the target verifies 4 positions per sequence, 512 positions per round. With short contexts the per-position cost is dominated by the matmuls, so the round costs about what the 512-token step costs, 124 ms, plus 3 draft steps at 2 ms, 130 ms in total.

| Batch 128 | Per round | Tokens produced | Throughput | Per-token latency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Without speculation | 38 ms | 128 | 128 / 0.038 = 3,368 tok/s | 38 ms |
| With speculation | 130 ms | 128 × 2.53 = 324 | 324 / 0.130 = 2,492 tok/s | 130 / 2.53 = 51 ms |

Throughput falls by 26% and per-token latency rises at peak.

The same arithmetic at small batch shows where the gain is. At batch 8 the 32-position round costs 22 ms plus 3 ms of drafting, so per-token latency falls from 20 ms to 25 / 2.53 = 9.9 ms. At batch 32: a 128-position round of 38 ms plus 6 ms of drafting gives 17.4 ms per token against 22 ms. At batch 64: 66 + 6 = 72 ms per round gives 28.5 ms against 26 ms, a loss. The break-even sits between 32 and 64 sequences on this profile. The profile tells you why: from 8 to 64 tokens per step the time barely moves, so extra positions are nearly free, and from 128 upward it grows almost in proportion, so they are not.

**The decision.** Two answers are defensible, and the constraint conflict is latency for interactive users against peak capacity on GPUs you cannot add.

- *Gate speculation on batch size* (vLLM lists dynamic speculative decoding as a feature; a scheduler-side threshold works too). Off-peak users get roughly half the per-token latency and peak capacity is untouched. Cost: two performance regimes to monitor, a threshold to retune whenever the profile changes, and peak-hour users get no latency gain at all.
- *Leave it off.* One regime and no tuning. Cost: the off-peak latency gain is forgone, and that gain is the reason product asked.
- *Always on* is defensible only if the latency target binds at peak and someone pays for about 35% more GPUs to hold peak throughput (3,368 / 2,492 = 1.35). The fixed GPU count rules that out this quarter.

I would gate, with the threshold at 32 to start, because that is the last measured point that wins, and move it after measuring 48.

**How the gate fails.** Batch size is a proxy for "the step is compute bound". It stops being a good proxy when context length changes. MagicDec reports gains at batch 32 to 256 for moderate to long sequences, because each sequence's KV cache is read every step and the extra verified positions reuse that read. If the long-context share of traffic grows, the gate switches speculation off exactly where it would now help. A gate on measured step time per position, or on the profile re-run by context bucket, holds up better.

Why the others tempt:

- "2.5 times" is the batch-1 intuition: one weight read, several tokens. It is right where the step is bandwidth bound and wrong here.
- "Longer drafts amortise" misreads the formula. Tokens per pass saturate at 3.3 for alpha 0.7 while verified positions keep growing.
- "Outputs drift" confuses the sampling guarantee with batch-shape numerics.
- "Halves the batch" names a real cost of draft models in general. The KV and preemption numbers rule it out here.

## Follow-ups

- *The gate costs you an on-call runbook and a second latency regime. Product says the off-peak gain is 4% of tokens and not worth it. Which half of the work do you cut, and what do you write down as the forgone latency?*
- *What would you need to see in production to reverse the gate and run speculation at peak?* A profile, at the new traffic mix, in which a 4× position step costs less than 2.5 times a 1× step at batch 128. That most plausibly happens if mean context grows several-fold or the draft's acceptance rises.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants a batch-size gate, the other a per-request toggle based on each request's running acceptance rate. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Replay a day of traffic through both and compare throughput and p99 per-token latency at each hour, and check whether a per-request toggle shortens the round at all when the rest of the batch still drafts.
- *Finance asks what the 26% means in money. What do you need, and what is the answer in GPUs?* About 35% more GPUs for the same peak throughput, priced at your rate.

## Common mistakes

- Answering yes or no without reading where the step sits on the profile.
- Taking the acceptance rate from batch-1 replay and assuming the speedup carries to batch 128.
- Treating "throughput" and "per-token latency" as the same question; at peak both get worse here, off-peak only latency improves.
- Setting the gate once and never re-running the profile when context lengths change.
