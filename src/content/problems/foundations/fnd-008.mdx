## Question

Scenario: a claims-triage service runs a self-hosted model on vLLM. A 300-case regression eval runs every night at temperature 0 against the production fleet, which is serving live traffic at the time. Two weeks ago the platform team upgraded the engine. Since then the eval has not passed once, and the on-call engineer has pulled this from the eval logs.

```text
eval config     temperature=0  seed=1234  max_tokens=512  model=claims-v7 (unchanged since August)
goldens         recorded 2026-09-30
engine          upgraded 2026-10-14

night    failing cases            first differing token (string diff against golden)
10-20    41, 152, 88, 230         41: 3    152: 7   88: 214   230: 133
10-21    41, 152, 17              41: 3    152: 7   17: 96
10-22    41, 152, 230, 61, 199    41: 3    152: 7   230: 141  61: 188  199: 102
10-23    41, 152, 105             41: 3    152: 7   105: 167
```

Logprobs are not logged, and neither is batch size per request. Which cause explains the failures that move from night to night? Then rank the other defects you see and say what you would collect before changing anything.

## Answer

The log holds two failure populations, and they have different causes.

Cases 41 and 152 fail every night, diverge at tokens 3 and 7, and started failing with the upgrade. That is the engine change: different kernels or a different default path compute slightly different logits, and on these two cases an early choice was close enough to flip. The goldens are stale, which is a real defect, but it explains only these two.

The rotating cases (88, 230, 17, 61, 199, 105) diverge after 96 to 214 identical tokens and change from night to night on an unchanged model. A model that sampled at a small temperature would tend to diverge early and often; these behave like the Thinking Machines measurement, where 1,000 temperature-0 runs agreed for 102 tokens and split at token 103. Their account: the forward pass is run-to-run deterministic for a fixed batch, but common kernels are not batch-invariant, so the reduction order inside matmul, attention and normalisation changes with how many requests share the batch. On a fleet serving live traffic, that is random from the eval's point of view, and wherever two tokens are nearly tied, the low-bit difference flips the argmax.

The evidence is incomplete, and the log supports the ranking without proving it. Before changing anything I would collect three things. The top-two logprob gap at the first differing token for the rotating cases: a gap of thousandths confirms near-ties. A replay of two rotating cases alone on an idle replica, twenty times each, and again with synthetic concurrent load: identical alone and divergent under load confirms batch dependence. And the engine version behind each golden. Without the logprobs, rerunning each failing case ten times on the fleet and counting how often it matches gives the per-case flip rate, which is enough to separate rotating from constant failures.

The decision has two defensible answers. Run the eval on a small dedicated pool with batch-invariant kernels: a single diff then means something, at the cost of a pool whose throughput is lower (Thinking Machines measured 26 s against 42 s on one offline workload) and whose numerics no longer match what users get. Or keep the eval on the production fleet and design it for variance: several runs per case, gate on each case's pass rate, at the cost of several times the calls and a longer night. For this team I would take the second for the nightly gate, because the eval's job is to catch regressions in what users see, and re-baseline the goldens with a human review of cases 41 and 152 rather than accepting the new outputs blindly. The failure mode of that answer: a pass-rate gate hides a regression that moves a case from 95% to 85%, so the per-case rate has to be tracked over time, not only compared with a threshold.

Separately, a near-tie at a decision token in a claims-triage output (approve, refer, deny) is a product defect in its own right. If the logprobs show one, the fix is in the prompt or the model, not the serving stack.

Why the wrong choices are tempting. The seed option comes from thinking of temperature 0 as low-temperature sampling; there is nothing to seed. The clamped-temperature option comes from a real detail of vLLM, which raises small positive temperatures to a minimum, but 0 itself is greedy. The stale-golden option is true and is the first thing most people find, which is why it is easy to stop there.

## Follow-ups

- *The pass-rate gate needs five runs per case and the nightly window is already full. Which cases do you run once, which five times, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *A dedicated deterministic pool costs about a third of one replica's capacity. Product says the eval should measure exactly what users get. Two senior engineers disagree; how do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *What would you need to see in the logprob data to conclude the rotating failures are a model problem rather than a serving one, and what would you do then?*

## Common mistakes

- Re-recording the goldens after the upgrade and declaring the eval fixed, which hides the rotating failures for a week.
- Setting a seed and expecting it to help at temperature 0.
- Retrying a failing case until it passes, which turns a near-tie on a triage decision into a green build.
- Treating both failure populations as one cause because they appeared at the same time.
