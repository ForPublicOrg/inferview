## Question

Scenario. A support assistant runs on a hosted frontier model. Product wants three things: answers grounded in 3,000 help articles that change weekly; the house tone and a JSON block the ticketing system parses on every reply; and a lower cost per ticket, because volume has grown. The proposal on the table, from the product lead, is "fine-tune a model on the help articles", and the vendor named in the proposal is OpenAI. Your team is four engineers with 90 days. Your organisation has never run a fine-tuning job.

Write the decision: for each requirement, prompt, retrieval or fine-tune, with the data volume and capacity conditions under which each branch wins. Choose the number of filtered examples you would collect before the first fine-tuning experiment, and defend it. Say what you give up and what you put in writing.

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Is the fine-tuning route in the proposal open to an organisation in your position today, and until when?</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The articles are knowledge that changes weekly: retrieval, not weights. Tone and JSON are behaviour: a prompt plus schema-constrained output first, fine-tuning only if the eval shows the prompt cannot hold them. Cost is where fine-tuning earns a place, as distillation of a stable behaviour into a smaller model. But the proposal's route is closed: OpenAI's self-serve fine-tuning has been unavailable since 7 May 2026 to organisations that never ran it. So the fine-tuning branch means an open-weight model with LoRA, hosted by us or a provider that still trains, which a four-person team has to operate. I would ship retrieval and constrained output in the first 45 days and gate the distillation branch on a measured cost case.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Step zero.</b> An eval set from real tickets: the right article, an acceptable answer, the expected JSON, scored on grounding, schema validity and tone. Every branch below is "did this move the eval, and at what cost per ticket".</p>
<p><b>Articles: retrieval.</b> Weekly change makes weights the wrong store: each update would be a training run, citations would be impossible, and details the weights hold imperfectly come back wrong. Retrieval wins whenever the content changes faster than you can retrain and evaluate. Condition that would move part of it to weights: domain vocabulary the base model mangles and retrieval cannot fix, shown on the eval.</p>
<p><b>Tone and JSON: prompt first.</b> A system prompt with tone rules and examples, and the provider's structured-output mode so the block is constrained rather than hoped for. Fine-tuning wins here only if the eval shows drift that prompts cannot remove and the behaviour is stable enough to train once.</p>
<p><b>Cost: the fine-tuning branch.</b> Collect the frontier model's answers that pass the eval, and fine-tune a smaller open-weight model on them, the same pattern DeepSeek used to distil R1 into smaller models with an 804,745-row SFT set. LoRA on all layers is the default because LoRA Without Regret finds it matching full fine-tuning on small and medium datasets; once the dataset outgrows the adapter's capacity it falls behind, and full fine-tuning becomes the branch. QLoRA keeps the hardware small (the paper fine-tuned 65B on one 48 GB GPU). Keep the frontier model behind a router for tickets where the small model fails the schema or the confidence check.</p>
<p><b>How many examples.</b> The number is chosen, then tested. I would collect 2,000 filtered examples, enough to hold out 400 and still train a learning curve at 400, 800 and 1,600, stratified by ticket category so each has a held-out slice. Reasonable answers range from a few hundred to a few thousand if they come with the learning curve; unreasonable answers are the 3,000 articles themselves (documents are not prompt and answer pairs) or a target set without a held-out slice. If the curve is still rising at 1,600, collect more; if it is flat at 400, fine-tuning is not the lever.</p>
<p><b>The conflict.</b> Cost against operating load. The distilled model cuts cost per ticket but adds a model to host, evaluate and retrain whenever the format changes, for a team of four. The alternative cost levers (a cheaper hosted model with the same prompt, prefix caching, shorter outputs) take days and add nothing to operate.</p>
<p><b>What you give up.</b> In the first 45 days, the cost cut: retrieval and constrained output ship first, and the fine-tuning branch starts only if a cheaper hosted model fails the eval. If it does start, the team gives up some of the frontier model's quality on the routed share and takes on model operations.</p>
<p><b>Written down.</b> A decision memo to the product lead: why the articles go to retrieval, the corrected status of the proposed vendor route with its dates, the cost-per-ticket threshold that triggers the fine-tuning branch, the held-out score the small model must match, and the rollback (route all tickets to the frontier model) with its trigger. Failure mode of the recommendation: the distilled model copies the frontier model's systematic errors, because examples were filtered on schema validity rather than correctness; the memo requires correctness review of a sample of training examples, not only schema checks.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- The tickets contain personal data that cannot leave your infrastructure. Which branches survive, and what does the hosting change cost the team of four?
- The base open-weight model you distilled into is deprecated by its maintainer in a year. What is the retraining cost, and what in your process makes it a routine job?

## Common mistakes

- Fine-tuning on documents to teach facts, then fighting staleness and invented details.
- Recommending a vendor fine-tuning route without checking that it is open to your organisation.
- Fine-tuning for JSON shape when constrained output already guarantees it.
- Choosing an example count without a held-out slice that can say whether more data helps.
- Treating the distilled model as free once trained; it is a service with an owner.
