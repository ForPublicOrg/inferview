## Question

Scenario: a support platform receives one million tickets a day and needs each one labelled with one of 30 queues, with a confidence score that decides whether a person checks the label before routing. Today a hosted model labels every ticket and nothing checks it. The review team is 12 people who can each check 400 tickets a day, and it will not grow this year. Finance has approved at most twice today's model spend. A misrouted ticket costs a support agent a manual re-route and adds a day to resolution; a ticket in the wrong regulated queue (two of the 30 are for legal and fraud) is a compliance incident.

Design the confidence mechanism. The options on the table are thresholds on the label's first-token logprobs from the existing call, a second self-verification call that answers yes or no, and a small classifier trained on labels. Defend the cost, the review rate and what you give up, and write the rollout plan with the triggers that would roll it back.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Review capacity is 12 x 400 = 4,800 a day, 0.48% of volume, so the score's job is to rank, and the threshold is set by capacity, not by the score. I would use the label's first-token probability from the existing call as the default score because it is free, reserve review for the two regulated queues plus the lowest-scored remainder, and train a small classifier on the audit labels as a second score and a fallback for the day logprobs go away. A verification call on every ticket roughly doubles spend for a score we can only act on for 0.48% of tickets.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The number that frames everything: at 95% accuracy (a scenario value until the audit measures it), one million tickets carry 50,000 wrong labels a day and review can see 4,800. Even a perfect ranking catches under 10% of errors. Review is not the error control for the general queues; it is the control for the regulated ones, plus a sampling instrument for calibration. Misroutes in general queues are handled by cheap re-routing and by improving accuracy.</p>
<p>So the design spends review on the regulated queues first: every ticket labelled legal or fraud with a score below a threshold, and every ticket whose second-ranked label is legal or fraud with probability above a small floor. The remaining capacity goes to the lowest-scored tickets elsewhere and to a fixed random sample of a few hundred a day above the threshold, which is the only way to measure the error rate that review does not see.</p>
<p>The score: first-token probabilities over the 30 queue tokens, renormalised, read from the existing call. That needs the label to be a single constrained token per queue, and it needs the provider to return logprobs at the setting we use, which current reasoning settings on some hosted models do not. A small classifier trained on the audited labels gives a second score at near-zero marginal cost and survives a model change; it is weak on queues with few labels. The verification call is the Instacart pattern, and it earns its cost on extraction where no single token carries the answer; for a 30-way label, the label's own distribution already carries the information.</p>
</Fragment>

### The cost of each option, computed

Scenario token counts and prices, to be replaced with measured ones: 600 input and 10 output tokens per ticket today, and $1 per million input and $5 per million output tokens.

- Today: 1,000,000 x 600 = 600M input tokens, $600; 10M output tokens, $50. $650 a day.
- **Logprob thresholds:** the same call with logprobs requested. $650 a day, plus the engineering to make the label a single token per queue.
- **Self-verification on every ticket:** a second call with the ticket, the label and the queue definition, about 650 input and 2 output tokens. 650M input, $650; 2M output, $10. Total $1,310 a day, 2.02 times today, which breaks the finance limit by itself.
- **Verification only on candidates for review:** if the cheap score pre-selects the lowest 5% of tickets, 50,000 verification calls cost about $33 a day, and the verified score decides which 4,800 go to people.
- **Small classifier:** a fixed serving cost on CPU or one small GPU, no per-ticket token cost, and a labelling cost that review is already paying.

### Recommendation and what it gives up

First-token probabilities from the existing call as the primary score, verification on the 5% lowest-scored tickets to choose the 4,800 reviews, and a classifier trained on the audit labels running in shadow from day one. Review priority: regulated queues, then lowest verified score, plus a fixed random sample above the threshold for calibration.

What it gives up: errors in general queues above the threshold are not reviewed and are corrected by agents re-routing, at the cost of a day's delay per misroute; the classifier is unproven on rare queues; and the design depends on a provider feature that is already being withdrawn on some reasoning settings.

The failure mode of the recommendation is calibration drift: a model update or a new product launch shifts the scores, the threshold now selects a different population, and the 4,800 reviews are spent on the wrong tickets without anyone noticing. The random sample above the threshold is what detects it.

### Rollout plan

Week 1 to 2: shadow. Compute scores on all tickets, review only from the random sample, and build the per-queue reliability table. Week 3: route the regulated queues through review by score. Week 4: full routing by score. Roll back to today's behaviour plus full review of the regulated queues if any of these hold for two consecutive days: the observed error rate in the random sample above the threshold exceeds the planned rate by half; review volume exceeds 4,800; a regulated-queue misroute is found that scored above the threshold; or the provider stops returning logprobs for more than 1% of calls.

## Follow-ups

- *Finance asks you to drop the verification calls and the classifier and live with logprobs alone. What does each removal cost in errors on the regulated queues, and who signs that risk?*
- *Your support lead says the reviewers should check the 4,800 highest-impact tickets, not the 4,800 least confident. Two defensible priorities; how do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *What would you need to see in the random audit sample after three months to reverse the design and move to the classifier as the primary score?*

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the threshold from the score's value (0.9) instead of from review capacity.
- Assuming a well-ranked score is calibrated, so the planned error rate above the threshold is wrong from day one.
- Running a verification call on every ticket when the label's own first-token distribution carries the same information for a 30-way classification.
- Having no random sample above the threshold, so errors that review never sees are never measured.
- Building the design on logprobs without checking that the provider returns them at the setting in use.
