checklist:
  - Starts with an inventory of every caller by API key, service, platform and model ID, and treats each platform's retirement date separately
  - Fixes interface breaks first (removed sampling parameters, changed limits) and proves them with shadow traffic before any quality work
  - Runs the per-prompt eval and behaviour evals (format validity, length, refusals on a fixed borderline set) on both models, per route, with tolerances set by each route's owner
  - Measures cost per request on real traffic, including the tokenizer change and thinking tokens, against the spend limit
  - Plans batches around the change freeze, with the last batch finishing at least a week before the earliest retirement date
  - Gives each route three exits (migrate, migrate to another model, migrate degraded with a compensating check) and decides per route by the cost of its errors
  - States hold criteria with a date, and treats holding past retirement as impossible rather than as an option

## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Workflow, 40 minutes">
Scenario: your company runs 40 production prompts across 6 routes on `claude-sonnet-4-5-20250929`. Four routes call the Claude API; two call the same model through Amazon Bedrock under a separate team's account. The vendor has just announced retirement in 60 days with `claude-sonnet-5-5` as the replacement. Constraints: a platform team of three; a company-wide change freeze for 14 days in the middle of the window; one route drafts customer-facing compliance notices, and the compliance officer must approve any change to it; finance will not accept a monthly spend increase above 10%. Design the migration workflow: the steps, the state you track, the evals, the hold criteria, the approvals, and who decides when a route regresses.
</Callout>

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether the Bedrock routes share the Claude API's retirement date, and where you would find out.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask each route's owner what error on their route costs more than a slow migration.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask for last month's token counts per route, so the cost change can be measured rather than read off the price list.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Treat it as a per-route migration with a fixed calendar: inventory callers and per-platform dates in week 1, fix interface breaks and shadow in week 2, run per-route task and behaviour evals and one round of prompt fixes before the freeze, ship batches around the freeze, and finish at least a week before the earliest retirement. Each route ends in one of three states (migrated, migrated to another model, migrated degraded with a check), each degraded route is signed by its product owner, and the compliance route by the compliance officer. Holding is only allowed with a date before retirement and a named reason.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Calendar.</b> 60 days minus a 14-day freeze leaves 46 days outside the freeze, and the last batch should finish at least a week before retirement to leave room for one rollback-and-retry. Week 1: inventory. Export usage by API key and model, map keys to services and owners, and get the Bedrock routes' retirement date from Bedrock's own model table, because Anthropic's dates apply only to the platforms Anthropic operates. Week 2: interface fixes. Remove sampling parameters the replacement rejects, rerun the client against the new model on shadow traffic, and count 4xx errors to zero.</p>
<p><b>Evals, per route.</b> Run the 40 prompts' eval sets on both models, scored against tolerances each route owner sets in advance, plus behaviour evals: schema validity, output length distribution, refusal rate on a fixed borderline set. Prompts do not transfer reliably even within a model family (Applied LLMs reports a 10% drop on one team's intent classifier between two snapshots of the same model), so one round of prompt fixes is scheduled before the freeze, not after.</p>
<p><b>Cost, measured.</b> Sonnet 5.5 is two thirds of Sonnet 4.5's per-token price, and its tokenizer produces about 30% more tokens for the same text, so a request is about 0.87 times the cost before any change in thinking length. Measure tokens per request on shadow traffic per route; a route whose output grows enough to break the 10% limit is a finding for finance, not a surprise on the invoice.</p>
<p><b>State per route</b>, in one shared table: platform and retirement date, eval status against tolerance, behaviour eval status, measured cost change, batch, owner, decision (migrate, other model, degraded, hold until a stated date), sign-off reference, rollback triggers and the date the old model stops being reachable.</p>
<p><b>Two defensible ways to handle a route that still regresses after the prompt round.</b> Move it to a larger model such as Opus 5.5 at $4 / $20, which costs money against the 10% limit and adds a model to operate. Or move it degraded with a compensating check (a validator, sampled human review) that the owner signs, which keeps spend flat and accepts a known error rate. The compliance route defaults to the first unless the compliance officer signs the second; the others decide by the cost of their errors against their volume.</p>
<p><b>Batches and rollback.</b> Low-risk routes before the freeze; the compliance and regressing routes after it, each with two days of shadow and a 10% canary. Rollback triggers are written before each switch (for example, schema-valid rate below the pre-switch floor for a day, escalations above the pre-switch weekly range), and the old model stays reachable until two quiet weeks pass or retirement arrives.</p>
<p><b>What we give up:</b> a second model ID on any route that moves to Opus, and a signed, known error rate on any degraded route. <b>Failure mode:</b> the evals cover the regressions we know about; a behaviour change outside the eval set (a new refusal pattern on a rare request type) reaches users after the old model is gone. Escalation rate per route is the signal, and after retirement the only remedy is forward: a prompt fix or another model.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *How would this workflow change for a self-hosted open-weight model with no retirement date?*
- *What would you change before the next deprecation notice so the next migration takes half the time?*

## Common mistakes

- Planning one date for all routes when two platforms publish different retirement schedules.
- Scheduling prompt fixes after the freeze, leaving no time to react to what they reveal.
- Reading the cost change off the price list without the tokenizer and thinking changes.
- Leaving degraded routes without a named signer, so the decision defaults to whoever deploys.
