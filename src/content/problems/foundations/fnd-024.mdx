## Question

You run retrieval over 80 million chunks. The embedding model, open weights, was trained with a Matryoshka loss: it returns 1,024 dimensions and its card lists 768, 512 and 256 as supported truncations. Each search replica has 48 GB of RAM set aside for vectors; the HNSW graph and metadata are sized separately, and the replicas have local NVMe. These deployment figures are scenario inputs.

Choose a dimension and a storage format. Estimate the RAM your choice needs for the vectors, say how much retrieval quality you expect to lose and on what evidence, and say how you would measure the loss before shipping.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Bytes per vector dominates: the count is fixed, so the answer is dimensions times bytes per value. Float32 at 1,024 is 327.68 GB and is out. Int8 at 512 dimensions is 40.96 GB and fits; binary at 1,024 is 10.24 GB in RAM with an 81.92 GB int8 copy on disk for rescoring. I would pick binary with rescoring if disk reads are allowed, int8 at 512 otherwise, and measure recall@10 against exact float32 search on a sample before choosing.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The arithmetic for 80,000,000 vectors:</p>
<ul>
<li>float32, 1,024: 80,000,000 × 1,024 × 4 bytes = 327.68 GB.</li>
<li>int8, 1,024: 81.92 GB. int8, 768: 61.44 GB. Both over 48.</li>
<li>int8, 512: 40.96 GB. int8, 256: 20.48 GB.</li>
<li>binary, 1,024: 1,024 / 8 = 128 bytes per vector, 10.24 GB. binary, 512: 5.12 GB.</li>
</ul>
<p>Two configurations are defensible. Binary at full dimension, with an int8 copy on disk to rescore a shortlist, keeps all 1,024 dimensions; the published figure for one 1,024-dimension model is 96.45% of float32 NDCG@10 with rescoring, at the cost of a disk read per candidate in the rescoring step. Int8 at 512 keeps everything in RAM and needs no disk path; its loss is two lossy steps compounded, int8 (about 97% without rescoring for that same model) and truncation to half the dimensions, and no source in hand measures the combination.</p>
<p>The flip: if the rescoring copy must also be in RAM, binary plus int8 is 10.24 + 81.92 = 92.16 GB and does not fit, so int8 at 512 wins by default.</p>
</Fragment>

Accepted band: 5 to 48 GB of RAM for the vectors. Anything from binary at 512 dimensions (5.12 GB) to int8 at 512 (40.96 GB) is a reasonable choice, depending on the rescoring answer.

Unreasonable choices, and why:

- Float32 at any of the listed dimensions. Even at 256 it is 80,000,000 × 256 × 4 = 81.92 GB.
- Int8 at 768 (61.44 GB). It is over budget by 13.44 GB.
- Binary at 256 dimensions (2.56 GB). It stacks the two largest lossy steps at once to save memory nobody asked for, and no source here measures that combination.
- Filling the budget exactly. The vectors grow with the corpus, and a configuration that fits at 80 million with no headroom is the next capacity incident.

What the evidence supports, with its conditions. The quantisation figures come from one model, mxbai-embed-large-v1, on the MTEB retrieval subset: binary keeps 92.53% of float32 NDCG@10 without rescoring and 96.45% with it; int8 keeps 97% without and about 99% with a rescore multiplier of 4 to 5. The Matryoshka figure most often quoted, 98.37% at 64 of 768 dimensions, is a Spearman score on a sentence-similarity benchmark for a different model, so it says truncation is cheap for that model on that task and nothing about recall@10 on this corpus. OpenAI's claim that text-embedding-3-large at 256 dimensions beats ada-002 at 1,536 is the vendor's own MTEB average and compares two models, not one model at two sizes.

How to measure. Take a sample of the corpus that a single machine can search exactly, say 2 million chunks, and compute exact float32 top 10 at 1,024 dimensions for a few thousand real queries as the reference. Then measure, for each candidate configuration, recall@10 against that reference, plus recall@10 against human labels for a few hundred labelled queries. Run the candidates through the real ANN index, because graph search adds its own loss on top of quantisation. Re-normalise after truncation before quantising; int8 calibration ranges should come from a sample of the corpus, not from the queries.

The failure mode of the recommendation: binary quantisation thresholds each dimension at 0, which works for models whose dimensions are centred near 0. A model whose values are skewed loses more information per bit, and the 96.45% figure does not transfer. Check the per-dimension sign balance on a sample before trusting it.

## Follow-ups

- The labelled set has 300 queries, and the two configurations differ by 1.5 points of recall@10. Is that a difference you can act on, and what would you need to make it one?
- The model is replaced next year by one without Matryoshka training. Which of your choices survives the swap?
- You are told the HNSW graph needs as much RAM as the int8 vectors at 512 dimensions. Does your answer change?

## Common mistakes

- Multiplying published retention figures from different models and tasks and quoting the product as the expected loss.
- Truncating without re-normalising, then quantising the shorter, unnormalised vectors.
- Sizing the budget against today's count with no growth.
- Using GiB and GB interchangeably: 327.68 GB is 305.2 GiB.
