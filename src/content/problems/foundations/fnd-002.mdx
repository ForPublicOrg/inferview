## Question

"The capital of France is Paris." is 31 characters and 7 tokens under cl100k_base, the GPT-4 tokenizer. Its Hindi translation, फ्रांस की राजधानी पेरिस है।, is 27 characters.

You know one more fact from the tokens lesson: under cl100k, Hindi text runs at roughly one character per token, because the pre-tokenizer splits words at every vowel sign and many Devanagari sequences fall back to bytes.

Estimate how many times as many cl100k tokens the Hindi sentence needs as the English one. Then say what that ratio does to cost and to the context window, and what changes under o200k_base, the GPT-4o tokenizer.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Divide by the English count.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>About 27 tokens against 7, so roughly 4 times as many. Measured, it is exactly 27, a ratio of 3.9. Cost per request scales with tokens, so the same Hindi content costs about 4 times as much and fills the context window about 4 times faster. Under o200k the Hindi sentence is 10 tokens against 7, a ratio of 1.4, because o200k's pre-tokenizer keeps vowel signs inside words and its vocabulary has more Devanagari merges.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Worked estimate. 27 characters at about 1 character per token gives about 27 tokens; 27 / 7 ≈ 3.9. The accepted band is 3 to 5 times, because the one-character rule is approximate.</p>
<p>Measured with tiktoken 0.13.0: cl100k_base gives 7 and 27 tokens (ratio 3.86); o200k_base gives 7 and 10 (ratio 1.43). The lesson's longer fox sentence shows the same pattern: 15 against 71 in cl100k (4.7 times) and 15 against 28 in o200k.</p>
<p>Consequences. Billing is per token, so a request in Hindi costs about 4 times the English one on a cl100k model. Context windows are counted in tokens, so the same window holds about a quarter as much Hindi text, and any chunker that sizes by characters overflows first for Hindi users. Other scripts vary: on the same sentence, Tamil (பிரான்சின் தலைநகரம் பாரிஸ்.) is 38 cl100k tokens (5.4 times) and Japanese 11 (1.6 times), and Petrov et al. report gaps up to 15 times across languages.</p>
<p>What to do. Measure tokens per request by language on real traffic, prefer a model whose tokenizer handles the script (o200k cut this ratio from 3.9 to 1.4), shorten and cache the static prompt prefix, and budget context in tokens. Re-measure on every model upgrade: Anthropic says Claude 4.7 and later produce about 30% more tokens for the same text than earlier Claude models.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why is Japanese not as bad?</em> Common CJK characters and short sequences already have merges in cl100k, so the gap is script-specific, not "non-English" in general.</li>
<li><em>How would you price a multilingual product?</em> Per conversation or per character, so customers do not pay for your tokenizer's training data.</li>
<li><em>Would a bigger vocabulary always fix it?</em> Only if the extra merges cover the script; o200k helped Devanagari through both new merges and a regex change.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *How do you confirm the ratio in production?* Log input tokens per request with the language detected, and compare medians for the same intent.
- *What breaks first for Hindi users?* Truncation and chunking limits written in characters or tested only on English fixtures.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming four characters per token for every language.
- Treating the ratio as a constant across tokenizers; it moved from 3.9 to 1.4 between cl100k and o200k.
- Comparing price per token across vendors without counting tokens in each vendor's tokenizer.
