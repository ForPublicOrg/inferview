## Question

Your billing assistant sends the same notification in three languages. Measured with tiktoken 0.13.0:

| Message | UTF-8 bytes | cl100k_base tokens | o200k_base tokens |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| English: "Your invoice INV-20481 for October is ready. The total due is 4,250.00 INR, payable by 15 November. Reply to this message if any line item looks wrong." | 151 | 41 | 40 |
| Hindi: "अक्टूबर के लिए आपका चालान INV-20481 तैयार है। कुल देय राशि 4,250.00 INR है, जिसका भुगतान 15 नवंबर तक करना है। यदि कोई मद गलत लगे तो इस संदेश का उत्तर दें।" | 354 | 151 | 49 |
| Japanese: "10月分の請求書 INV-20481 の準備ができました。お支払い総額は 4,250.00 INR で、お支払い期限は11月15日です。明細に誤りがある場合は、このメッセージに返信してください。" | 229 | 87 | 63 |

The product runs on a cl100k model today. Finance sees Hindi-language accounts costing more per message and asks for one of three changes: translate Hindi and Japanese to English before the model and back afterwards, move to a model with a different tokenizer, or charge per language. Quantify the skew, then recommend one and state what it costs.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which of the three messages are model input and which are model output, since the two are priced differently.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask for the per-language ratio under the candidate model's tokenizer, not under the current one.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Under cl100k the Hindi message is 151 / 41 = 3.7 times the English tokens and the Japanese 87 / 41 = 2.1 times. Under o200k the ratios are 1.2 and 1.6. Moving to an o200k-family model removes most of the Hindi skew, and does less for Japanese, which becomes the most expensive language. Translation through English adds two calls whose output is priced at the output rate and costs more than it saves. Per-language pricing is a commercial decision that passes the skew to customers; take it only for the residual after the model change, and only if the residual matters to margin.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The skew comes from two places. cl100k's pre-tokenizer matches letters with <code>\p&#123;L&#125;</code> only, so every Devanagari vowel sign, a combining mark, splits a word before BPE runs; and its merges cover little Devanagari, so much of the text falls back toward its UTF-8 bytes. 151 tokens for 354 bytes is about 2.3 bytes per token, against 3.7 for the English. o200k added <code>\p&#123;M&#125;</code> to its letter classes and has more non-English merges, and the Hindi count falls from 151 to 49 with no change to the text.</p>
<p>Translation. Translating Hindi to English is a call that reads Hindi input and writes English output; translating the answer back writes Hindi output, the most expensive tokens in the system, at the output rate. On a cl100k model the round trip writes about 151 Hindi tokens plus 41 English tokens of output to save the difference in one input. It also adds two model calls of latency and a translation-quality failure mode in an invoice, where a wrong number or date is a support ticket.</p>
<p>Model change. The cost is a migration: re-running your eval set, re-tuning prompts, and re-measuring every language, because the ratios move in different directions (Japanese goes from 2.1 to 1.6, Hindi from 3.7 to 1.2). A newer model can also change the unit for everyone: Anthropic says its Claude 4.7 and later tokenizer produces about 30% more tokens for the same text, so a migration is a pricing event for English too.</p>
<p>Per-language pricing. It is honest about cost and simple to build, and it charges Hindi-speaking customers for a property of your vendor's tokenizer. The ratio also changes on every model migration, so the price list inherits a dependency on an internal choice. Pricing per message or per character, with the skew absorbed into margin, avoids that coupling.</p>
<p>Recommendation: move to the o200k-family model if it passes the eval, then decide whether the residual Japanese skew is worth a pricing change. The failure mode is assuming the measured ratios hold on your traffic: these are three sentences, and Petrov et al. report gaps up to 15 times across languages for the same text. Re-measure on a sample of real messages per language before and after.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *Your chunker for retrieved invoice history sizes chunks in characters. Which language overflows the context first, and by how much?* Compare bytes per token per language under the serving tokenizer.
- *How do you keep the per-language ratios current?* A scheduled job that counts tokens for a fixed multilingual probe set under every model ID you serve, alerting when a ratio moves.

## Common mistakes

- Assuming four characters per token for every script.
- Treating the ratio as a property of the language rather than of the language and the tokenizer together.
- Proposing translation without pricing the extra output tokens it generates.
- Comparing price per token across vendors without counting the same text under each vendor's tokenizer.
