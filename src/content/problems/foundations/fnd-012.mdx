## Question

"Our model has a 1M-token context window. Why not paste the whole 400-page contract into every request instead of building retrieval? And why does it still miss a clause that is clearly in the document?"

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Does an API accepting 1M tokens tell you how accurately the model uses token 500,000?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Where in the input was the clause, and how closely did the question's wording match it?</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A context window is how much the API accepts, not how well the model uses it. Published evaluations show accuracy dropping with input length and with position: information in the middle is used less reliably than at the edges, and questions that do not share words with the answer suffer most. Pasting everything also costs tokens and latency on every call. I would retrieve the relevant sections, put them and the instructions at the edges of a short prompt, and measure accuracy at the lengths I actually send.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Evidence. Lost in the Middle found accuracy highest when the relevant passage sits at the start or end of the input and lower in the middle. RULER tested 17 models that each claimed at least 32K tokens, and only half kept satisfactory performance at 32K. NoLiMa removed literal word overlap between the question and the answer: at 32K, 11 of 13 models fell below half their short-context score, GPT-4o among them, from 99.3% to 69.7%. Chroma's Context Rot study found 18 models degrading as input grew even on simple tasks, and on LongMemEval focused prompts of about 300 tokens beat full prompts of about 113,000.</p>
<p>Why the clause is missed. Contract questions rarely repeat the clause's wording ("can they end the deal early" against "termination for convenience"), which is the NoLiMa condition, and a clause on page 230 of 400 is in the middle. Distractors, such as similar clauses in other sections, make it worse.</p>
<p>Cost. Every call pays for every token. A whole contract in every request multiplies input cost and time to first token for a question that needs two pages. Prompt caching softens the price of a repeated prefix but not the accuracy problem.</p>
<p>What I would build. Section-aware retrieval (hybrid keyword and vector search, which handles the defined terms contracts use), a reranker, and a prompt that places the instructions and the retrieved sections at the start and end. Long context is still useful as a fallback for questions that span the whole document, and as a way to send more retrieved material than an 8K model could take. Then measure: a labelled set of questions with known answer locations, run at the prompt lengths I plan to ship.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>When is the full document the right choice?</em> When the question needs global structure (summarise all obligations) and the document fits comfortably; even then, check accuracy against a chunked map-reduce approach.</li>
<li><em>How would you test your own model's effective context?</em> Insert known facts at different depths and phrasings into real documents and plot accuracy by depth and length.</li>
<li><em>Does a newer model fix this?</em> Maybe partly. Re-run the same depth test on each new model rather than trusting the advertised window.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *Where do you put the question in a long prompt?* After the documents, with key instructions repeated near the end, then test the alternative on your eval.
- *How does this interact with cost per task?* Shorter prompts are cheaper and faster, so retrieval often wins on both quality and cost.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the advertised window as the usable window.
- Testing long context only with questions that share exact words with the answer, which overstates accuracy.
- Dropping retrieval entirely because the document fits.
