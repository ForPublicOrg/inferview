## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Concept, 25 minutes">
Scenario: the legal team's contract assistant runs on Claude Opus 5.5 with a 1M-token window, and it sends each 400-page master services agreement whole with every question. Asked "can the customer walk away early without paying a penalty?", it answered no. Page 231 contains a termination-for-convenience clause that says yes. The team now asks you two things: why did a model that accepted the whole contract miss it, and should the product keep sending the full document or move to retrieval? Volume is about 2,000 questions a day across 300 contracts; legal says a missed clause costs far more than a slow answer, and finance wants a number per question for each design.
</Callout>

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask for the accuracy of the current design on questions whose answers sit at different depths of the document, and on questions that do not reuse the clause's wording.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how often the same contract is queried within a few minutes, and what the provider charges for a repeated prefix.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what fraction of real questions need the whole document rather than a few sections.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A window is what the API accepts, not what the model uses reliably. The clause was mid-document and the question shared no words with it ("walk away early" against "termination for convenience"), the two conditions where published long-context evaluations show the largest drops. With prompt caching, the full document costs about $0.07 per question against about $0.03 for retrieval, so cost does not decide; accuracy on our questions does. I would make section retrieval with the definitions always included the default, keep a cached full-document mode for whole-contract questions, and gate both on a labelled set measured by clause depth and wording overlap. The retrieval design fails when the retriever misses the same paraphrase, so retriever recall is a tracked number.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Why it missed.</b> Lost in the Middle found performance highest when the relevant information is at the start or end of the input and significantly worse in the middle, even for long-context models. Page 231 of 400 is the middle. NoLiMa removed literal overlap between question and answer: 11 of 13 models claiming 128K or more fell below half their short-length baseline at 32K, and GPT-4o went from 99.3% to 69.7%. Legal questions are posed in business words and answered in defined terms, which is the NoLiMa condition. RULER adds that only half of 17 models claiming 32K or more held up at 32K. Our prompt is an order of magnitude longer than any of those test lengths, and a needle score on the model card says nothing about it; HELMET found needle results do not reliably predict downstream performance.</p>
<p><b>Size the prompt.</b> I choose 500 words per page, so 400 pages is 200,000 words. At about 555k words per million tokens on the current Claude tokenizer, that is about 360K tokens. Pages of dense tables or a lower word count per page move this by tens of percent; it does not move the conclusion below.</p>
<p><b>Price both designs</b> at Opus 5.5's $4 per million input tokens. Full document, uncached: 0.36 × $4 = $1.44 per question, $2,880 a day at 2,000 questions. Full document with the contract as a cached prefix: a hit costs $0.20 per million, so $0.072 per question, $144 a day, plus a $1.80 write each time a contract's 5-minute cache goes cold. Retrieval with 8K tokens of sections: 0.008 × $4 = $0.032 per question, $64 a day. Without caching, cost decides for retrieval; with caching, the gap is about $80 a day and accuracy decides.</p>
<p><b>Two defensible designs.</b> Cached full document: every clause is visible, cross-references and defined terms resolve, and there is no retriever to miss anything; the cost is the middle-of-context and paraphrase degradation measured above, plus time to first token on a 360K prompt and cache writes when contracts are queried rarely. Retrieval: a short prompt with the relevant sections at the edges, where the model uses them best; the cost is a retriever that can miss the clause for the same paraphrase reason, and questions that need the whole contract ("list every obligation that survives termination").</p>
<p><b>My recommendation.</b> Retrieval by default: section-aware chunks, hybrid keyword and vector search, the definitions section always included, and a query step that rewrites the question into contract vocabulary before search. A classifier routes whole-contract questions to the cached full-document mode. Both are gated by a labelled set of about 200 questions with known answer locations, stratified by depth and by lexical overlap, reporting answer accuracy and, for retrieval, whether the gold clause was retrieved.</p>
<p><b>Failure mode of the recommendation.</b> The retriever is the new single point of failure: when it misses the clause, the model answers confidently from the sections it was given. Retriever recall on the low-overlap slice is the number to watch, and a missed-gold-clause rate above legal's tolerance sends that question class to the full-document mode until recall improves.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *The contract is in English but a third of questions arrive in German. What happens to retrieval and to the full-document mode?*
- *Contracts get amended. How do the cache and the index stay correct when an amendment overrides clause 14.2?*

## Common mistakes

- Treating the advertised window as the usable window, or citing a needle-in-a-haystack score as evidence for it.
- Pricing the full document without prompt caching, which overstates its cost about twentyfold and makes the decision look easy.
- Measuring retrieval only on questions that share words with the answer, which overstates its recall on the questions that failed.
- Dropping the full-document path entirely, which leaves whole-contract questions with no correct design.
