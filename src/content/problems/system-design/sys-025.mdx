## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"We serve our chat product from our own fleet. Next year's plan says 20 million turns a day. What does the GPU compute cost per day, including peak and whatever reserve you think the availability target needs?"
</Callout>

What you get when you ask:

- Model: Llama 3.1 70B in BF16, one replica per node of eight H100 SXM GPUs.
- Traffic (scenario): 20,000,000 turns a day, 2,500 input tokens and 350 output tokens on average; the peak hour is 2.5 times the daily average.
- Latency target: P95 inter-token latency of 35 ms.
- Availability target: the product keeps its latency target through the loss of any one zone. The fleet spans three zones.
- Price (scenario): $2.50 per GPU-hour on a committed contract; use your own rate if you have one.
- A load test on one node at this traffic mix (scenario numbers):

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>At 96 concurrent, each turn spends 0.50 + 349 × 0.027 = 9.92 s in the system, so a node completes 9.67 turns a second. Peak is 578.7 turns a second: 59.8, so 60 nodes. Carrying the full peak on two of three zones needs 30 per zone, 90 nodes: <b>about $43,200 a day</b> at $2.50 per GPU-hour, against $28,800 for 60 nodes with no reserve. The reserve is a third of the bill, and it is the decision to argue about.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Operating point.</b> 96 is the highest row under a 35 ms P95 ITL. 128 gives more throughput per node (10.5 turns a second) and breaks the target at 41 ms. 160 completes no more than 128: the node is saturated, which is the shape NVIDIA's metrics describe. Running at 64 for margin is defensible and costs 22 percent more nodes.</p>
<p><b>Peak.</b> 20,000,000 / 86,400 × 2.5 = 578.7 turns a second. 578.7 / 9.67 = 59.8, so 60 nodes, 20 per zone. The average day needs 24 nodes, so a fixed fleet sized for peak idles most of the day.</p>
<p><b>Memory is not binding.</b> 96 turns of 2,850 tokens at 327,680 bytes per token hold 90 GB of KV cache, far below what a node has left after 141 GB of weights.</p>
<p><b>Reserve, two defensible answers.</b> Full zone reserve: with one of three zones gone, the other two carry 578.7 turns a second, so each zone needs 30 nodes, 90 in total. 90 × 8 × 24 × $2.50 = $43,200 a day, $6.17 per million output tokens. Or N+2 with a degraded mode: 62 nodes, $29,760 a day, and on a zone loss at peak the product sheds or queues a third of traffic, or spills it to a hosted model whose limits and eval were set up in advance. The first buys the availability target outright; the second saves about $13,400 a day and turns the target into "keep latency for admitted traffic", which product and the availability owner have to sign.</p>
<p><b>Dominant term and the flip.</b> Output length and the operating point set throughput per node; the reserve multiplies the result by 1.5. The assumption that flips the reserve decision is the zone-loss history: rare, short losses at off-peak hours favour N+2 with spillover; losses that have coincided with peak, or a regulator's availability requirement, favour the full reserve.</p>
<p><b>Failure mode of the full reserve.</b> It does not cover correlated failures across zones, such as a bad model or engine rollout, which take out every zone at once; the reserve has to be paired with staged rollouts by zone. And idle reserve gets borrowed for batch jobs, so it needs preemption that returns it within the failover time, tested, or it is not a reserve.</p>
</Fragment>

The band, $27,000 to $55,000 a day, admits operating at 64 or 96, either reserve design, and rounding of node counts. Answers near $11,500 sized from the daily average. Answers that ran at 128 or 160 broke the stated latency target to save nodes; answers that added the zone reserve on top of a separate N+2 counted the same failure twice.

## Follow-ups

- *Could the fleet follow the daily curve instead of sitting at peak?* Only with capacity you can release and reacquire daily; on a committed contract the idle hours are already paid for, so the saving appears only at the next contract.
- *How would you check the plan before the contract is signed?* Re-run the load test with next year's traffic mix (input and output length distributions, not averages) and a zone-failover drill at peak in the current fleet.
- *What does a hosted API cost for the same turns?* Price it per turn from the provider's list with your cache hit rate, and compare like with like: a different model is a quality decision, not just a cost line.

## Common mistakes

- Reading throughput from the row with the most tokens per second instead of the row that meets the latency target.
- Sizing from average traffic.
- Adding a percentage "for headroom" without naming the failure it absorbs.
- Treating a reserve that batch jobs use as available without tested preemption.
