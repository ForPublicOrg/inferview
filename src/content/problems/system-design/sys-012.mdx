## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The topic, as a prep guide lists it">
Design a multi-tenant chat backend: conversation state, per-tenant limits, and billing for token-based pricing.
</Callout>

Scenario: 400 business tenants embed your chat product and pay per token at your list price, a margin over the provider's. Finance already invoices through Stripe Billing Meters and will not change billing systems this year. One tenant, a hospital group, has a zero-retention agreement. Two engineers, ten weeks. The busiest tenant produces about a third of all traffic at peak.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask finance what an invoice line must show, and whether tenants receive token counts by price category or one blended number.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask for the provider's usage object from one real cached request and read every field.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask how long after month end invoices are finalised, and who is allowed to issue a credit note.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Conversations live in our database, keyed by tenant, and every request is rebuilt from it with a byte-stable prefix so the provider's cache does the work; that is also the only option the zero-retention tenant allows. Every model call writes a ledger row with all usage fields, model, price version and our request ID, in the same transaction as the assistant message. An hourly job reconciles ledger totals against the provider's usage export, then sends one meter event per tenant, price category and hour with a deterministic identifier. Per-tenant token buckets in our gateway, carved from the organization's limits, keep the busiest tenant from consuming everyone's capacity. What I give up is real-time usage on the tenant dashboard, which lags by the reconciliation delay.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>State.</b> Messages table keyed by tenant and conversation, with a rolling summary for long conversations so the prompt stays bounded. Provider-held state would save code but not money (OpenAI bills all previous input in a <code>previous_response_id</code> chain) and is not a record past 30 days.</p>
<p><b>Usage and price.</b> On the Claude API the input side is three fields at three prices. One deep turn of 40,000 cache-read, 2,000 cache-write, 300 uncached input and 500 output tokens costs $0.0186 on Sonnet 5 list prices; billing from <code>input_tokens</code> alone gives $0.0056, and billing all input at the base rate gives $0.0896. The ledger stores each field; the price table is keyed by model and effective date.</p>
<p><b>Limits.</b> A token bucket per tenant on uncached input and output, sized so the guaranteed shares sum to less than the organization limit, with the remainder shared. Cache reads do not count toward the input-token limit on most current Claude models, so a cache regression cuts capacity as well as margin; a deploy that changes the prefix is a capacity event.</p>
<p><b>Metering.</b> Stripe allows one concurrent meter event call per customer per meter, and the busiest tenant would hit that first if events were sent per request. Hourly aggregates per tenant and category avoid it. Adjustments can cancel only events sent in the last 24 hours, so reconciliation runs before an hour is sent, and later corrections become negative quantities while the invoice is open.</p>
<p><b>Failure mode of this design.</b> Reconciliation depends on the provider's export, which can be late. The aggregator then either holds hours, and the dashboard lags further, or sends unreconciled hours. I would hold up to six hours, then send and flag them, and accept that a flagged hour may need a negative correction later.</p>
</Fragment>

### The numbers you choose

The reconciliation hold (six hours above; anything under the 24-hour cancellation window is defensible, and a hold close to 24 hours leaves no time to cancel a bad send), the guaranteed share per tenant (the busiest tenant's third of peak traffic is its demand, not its guarantee), and the aggregation interval (hourly fits Stripe's per-customer concurrency; per request does not for the largest tenant).

### Two defensible metering designs

**Reconcile, then send.** Invoices are right the first time and corrections are rare. It costs freshness: usage on the dashboard lags, and threshold invoices see only what was sent.

**Send, then correct.** Usage is visible within minutes and spend alerts are timely. It costs correction traffic and a hard deadline: any error found after 24 hours can only be fixed with negative quantities, and after finalisation only with a credit note issued by a person. Reasonable for a product whose tenants watch spend closely and whose ledger rarely disagrees with the provider.

### What you give up

Real-time usage, under the first design; invoice accuracy without manual correction, under the second. Under both, one engineer-month goes to reconciliation tooling instead of product.

### Decision memo, in four lines

Decision: own-store conversation state, ledger per provider call, hourly reconciled meter events. Why: auditability and the zero-retention tenant. Risk accepted: dashboards lag up to six hours, and a provider export outage delays invoicing data. Review when: a tenant needs real-time spend control, or finance adopts a billing system with longer correction windows.

## Follow-ups

- *A stream fails halfway and the provider reports usage for it. Does the tenant pay, and where is that decided?*
- *A tenant asks to bring their own provider key. Which parts of this design remain?*
- *The provider ships a model with a cheaper cache-read multiplier. Which tables change, and on which date?*

## Common mistakes

- Billing from `input_tokens` alone, which under-bills cached traffic most for the tenants with the best caching.
- Sending a meter event per request from the request path, with no identifier and no aggregation.
- Treating the provider's stored responses as the conversation record.
- Retrying the spend-cap 429 like any other rate limit.
- Per-tenant limits that sum to more than the organization limit with no shared pool and no priority.
