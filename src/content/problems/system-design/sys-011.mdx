## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Design a content moderation system for user posts using AI."
</Callout>

Constraints from a community whiteboard version of this prompt: 1 million posts a day, a decision within 500 ms before a post becomes visible, and 10 languages. Posts are text with optional images. There is a written content policy and a small team of human reviewers.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>What fraction of posts actually needs an expensive model? Design so only that fraction pays for one.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>A 500 ms gate does not mean every check finishes in 500 ms. What can a post do while a slower check runs?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Classifier scores move when the model behind them is upgraded. How do you notice?</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A cascade. A fast classifier scores every post inside the 500 ms gate and decides the clear cases: publish or block. Posts in the uncertain band publish with limited reach (or stay visible only to the author) while a heavier model, which can read the written policy, decides asynchronously; what it cannot decide goes to human reviewers. Thresholds are set per category and per language from labelled data, pinned to a model version and re-checked on every upgrade, and every decision is logged with the model and threshold that made it, so appeals and audits work.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Scope. Which harms matter most (the policy's categories), what a wrong block costs versus a wrong publish for each, which languages, and whether images are in scope. Those answers set different thresholds per category.</p>
<p>Load. 1,000,000 posts a day is about 11.6 a second on average; with an assumed peak three times the average, about 35 a second. Both stages are sized from that and from the escalation rate, which is an assumption until measured. If 5 percent go to the heavy stage, it handles about 50,000 posts a day and under 2 a second at peak.</p>
<p>Stage one, synchronous. A trained classifier that covers text and images: a hosted moderation endpoint (OpenAI's is free and returns per-category scores across 13 categories) or a self-hosted model such as Llama Guard 4 if data cannot leave. Neither choice is trusted in a language until it has been measured on labelled posts in that language. Per-category thresholds split posts into publish, block and uncertain. This must fit in the 500 ms gate with the network included.</p>
<p>Stage two, asynchronous. A heavier model reads the written policy and the post, and returns a decision with a reason. Policy-as-input models such as gpt-oss-safeguard make new policies quick to deploy, but OpenAI's own report says classifiers trained on tens of thousands of labelled examples can still do better, and that the reasoner is compute-intensive, which is why it belongs off the synchronous path. Uncertain posts stay at limited reach until it answers.</p>
<p>Stage three, humans. What stage two cannot decide, appeals, and a random sample of automated decisions for quality control. Reviewer labels feed back into training and threshold tuning.</p>
<p>Calibration. Thresholds are chosen per category and language on a labelled set to hit agreed precision and recall targets. OpenAI warns that scores may need recalibration as its model is upgraded in place, so pin versions where you can, monitor score distributions, and rerun the labelled set on every change.</p>
<p>Operations. Every decision logs the post ID, model versions, scores, thresholds and outcome. Block rate and appeal-overturn rate per category and language are the dashboards; a sudden shift in either is an incident.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>A coordinated attack floods the uncertain band.</em> Stage two backs up. Size it for bad days, keep uncertain posts at limited reach while queued, and add rate limits per account.</li>
<li><em>One language has much worse precision.</em> Collect labelled data in that language, tune its thresholds separately, and consider a language-specific model for stage one.</li>
<li><em>Some categories need current knowledge (elections, defamation).</em> Llama Guard's card says these need more complex systems; route them to stage two or humans with up-to-date context rather than trusting a static classifier.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

### Rubric check

**Problem navigation:** asked for per-category error costs before setting thresholds. **Solution design:** cascade with a limited-reach state that makes the 500 ms gate achievable. **Technical excellence:** per-category, per-language thresholds, recalibration on upgrades, the trade-off between policy-as-input reasoning and trained classifiers. **Communication:** stated the escalation rate as an assumption and showed what it drives.

## Follow-ups

- *How would you measure the system?* Precision and recall per category and language on a fresh labelled sample each month, appeal-overturn rates, and time to decision for escalated posts.
- *Why not one big model on everything?* Cost and latency on a million posts a day, and the cascade lets each stage do what it is good at. Anthropic's production classifiers use the same cheap-screen-then-escalate shape at about 1 percent compute overhead.
- *A new policy category arrives tomorrow.* Write it into the stage-two policy prompt now, label examples from its decisions, and train it into stage one once there is enough data.

## Common mistakes

- One global threshold for all categories and languages.
- Running the expensive model synchronously on every post.
- No appeal path, so false positives are never measured.
- Treating classifier scores as stable across model upgrades.
