## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 45 minutes">
Design a chat product over SEC filings. An analyst asks a question that may span many companies and years; the system plans, reads the relevant filings with several agents in parallel, and streams an answer with citations. The analyst can cancel at any time. Each question has a token budget the system must not exceed.
</Callout>

Scenario inputs, chosen for the exercise: a typical question touches 40 annual reports. After parsing, one annual report is about 80,000 tokens (measure this on your corpus; filings vary widely). Each customer's plan sets a per-question budget of 1,500,000 input tokens. The product runs on one provider organization on the Build tier, with 5,000,000 uncached input tokens a minute for the model it uses. Analysts expect the first cited fact within 20 seconds.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether the budget is a hard ceiling the customer is billed against or a target, and what the analyst sees when it is reached.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Compute what reading every touched filing in full would cost, before deciding how the agents read.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask the provider what happens to billing and rate-limit accounting for a request the client disconnects from mid-generation.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A manager orchestrator plans the question into per-filing sub-tasks, retrieves sections rather than whole filings, and runs readers in parallel under a budget ledger that reserves each call's maximum input and output before it starts and settles to actual usage after. Full reading is 40 × 80,000 = 3.2 million tokens, more than twice the budget and 64 percent of the organization's per-minute input limit, so retrieval narrows each filing to its relevant sections first. Facts stream as each reader finishes; cancellation is a per-turn token every call checks, and in-flight streams are closed and settled.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The arithmetic that sets the design.</b> 40 filings × 80,000 tokens = 3,200,000 input tokens to read everything once: 2.1 times the 1,500,000 budget, and 64 percent of the organization's 5,000,000-token minute if sent at once by one analyst. So readers cannot get whole filings. A retrieval step over pre-chunked, pre-indexed filings (section-aware: risk factors, MD&amp;A, segment notes) selects the sections the plan names. With, say, 12,000 tokens of sections per filing, 40 readers spend 480,000 input tokens, leaving room for planning, a second pass and synthesis.</p>
<p><b>The quantity I choose: parallelism.</b> All 40 readers at once gets the first facts fastest and puts 480,000 tokens in one burst against a shared organization limit, alongside every other analyst. Eight concurrent readers per question keeps one question's burst near 100,000 tokens and still finishes 40 readers in five waves. I choose eight with a per-tenant concurrency cap in the gateway; sixteen is also reasonable for a smaller customer base. One per question is not: the full answer then takes 40 reader latencies end to end.</p>
<p><b>Budget enforcement, the main decision.</b> Two defensible answers. Reservation: before each call, the ledger reserves input tokens (counted before sending) plus the call's <code>max_tokens</code>, refusing the call if the reservation exceeds the remainder; after the call, it releases the unused part. The budget can never be exceeded, at the cost of refusing calls that would have fit, because <code>max_tokens</code> is a ceiling, not a forecast. Post-hoc metering: sum usage after each call and stop scheduling when the total passes the limit. Simpler and never refuses early, but eight concurrent readers can overshoot by up to eight calls' worth. Because the customer is billed against the budget (hint 1), I choose reservation and set each reader's <code>max_tokens</code> from the measured distribution of reader outputs, so the reserve is tight. The provider's own limits work the same way: input is estimated at the start and adjusted, and output counts as it is generated.</p>
<p><b>Orchestration.</b> The manager pattern from OpenAI's guide: one orchestrator owns the turn's state, delegates readers and synthesises. Readers return structured findings (claim, value, filing, section, page) rather than prose, so synthesis cites from data and a reader's output can be checked against its section text.</p>
<p><b>Streaming.</b> Events on one stream per turn: plan, reader started, finding (with citation), synthesis tokens, done. Findings stream as each reader finishes, which meets the 20-second target on the first wave; synthesis streams last.</p>
<p><b>Cancellation.</b> A cancel sets the turn's state; every scheduler loop and reader checks it before each call, and in-flight model streams are closed. Readers already finished keep their findings, so a cancelled turn still shows what it found. The ledger settles from the usage reported for each call, including partial ones; what the provider bills for a stream closed mid-generation is the question in hint 3, and the ledger records what the provider reports rather than assuming zero.</p>
<p><b>Conflict and failure mode.</b> Recall against cost and latency: section retrieval can miss the footnote that answers the question. The design surfaces that: a reader that finds nothing reports "not found in sections read", and the analyst can spend more budget on a full read of one filing. The reservation design's own failure mode is starvation late in a turn, when a large synthesis call cannot reserve enough; synthesis gets a reserved share of the budget from the start.</p>
<p><b>What you give up.</b> Exhaustive reading by default, some calls refused that would have fit, and answers that admit gaps instead of filling them.</p>
</Fragment>

<li><em>Two questions from one customer arrive together and each wants eight readers.</em> The per-tenant cap queues the second; its first event shows the queue position.</li>
</ul>

### The written artefact

A budget policy: how reservations are computed, the reserved share for synthesis, what the analyst sees at 80 and 100 percent of the budget, and how cancelled turns are settled and billed. Rollout trigger to revisit: if more than the agreed share of answers report "not found in sections read", the retrieval step is the bottleneck and the section index is the next investment.

### Check your answer

- The full-read cost is computed against the budget and the organization's limit.
- Parallelism is a number you chose, with its cost to other analysts.
- Budget enforcement prevents overshoot under concurrency, or states the overshoot.
- Cancellation reaches in-flight calls and keeps completed findings.
- Citations come from structured findings, not from the synthesis model's memory.
- The answer says what happens when retrieval misses the relevant section.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: reservation refuses calls that would have fit, and analysts see "budget reached" with budget left. What do you change, and what overshoot do you accept in writing?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to measure to switch readers from retrieved sections to whole filings?*
- *Operating: the provider halves your rate limit during an incident. Which questions slow down, and how does the stream tell the analyst?*

## Common mistakes

- Sending whole filings to every reader without computing the cost.
- Metering the budget after the fact with many calls in flight.
- Cancelling the user's stream while sub-calls keep running and spending.
- Letting the synthesis model cite from memory instead of from reader findings.
- No per-tenant cap, so one large question starves everyone on the organization's limit.
