## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Your platform exposes a shared agent capability (a code-search tool backed by one index service and one model-provider account) to six internal products. One product starts calling it from a background job that fans out to hundreds of parallel agent runs, and everyone else's agents time out. How do you stop one product's misuse of a shared capability from degrading every other product, without blocking legitimate heavy use?"
</Callout>

Operating constraints (scenario): the index service cannot be re-architected this half; the heavy product's job is tied to a launch its director has committed to; the platform team is three engineers.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Which resource saturates first under the fan-out: index service concurrency, provider tokens per minute, or harness workers?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>What latency budget does each of the six products have, and which of them are interactive?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Is "misuse" here a bug, a design the platform never anticipated, or a prompt change that made agents call the tool in a loop? What does the call graph per run show?</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Treat the capability as a multi-tenant service with its own admission control: a caller identity on every call, per-product quotas on each scarce resource (index concurrency, provider tokens), separate lanes for interactive and batch traffic with reserved capacity for interactive, and per-run budgets enforced by the harness so a looping agent fails itself, not the platform. Quotas come from measured demand, and heavy batch use gets a lane rather than a ban. The cost is lower peak throughput for the heavy product and a quota process that someone has to own.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Find the scarce resource.</strong> The tool call fans into two shared things: the index service and the provider account. Hundreds of parallel runs exhaust whichever saturates first. I would ask for concurrency and tokens per minute per product for the incident window before choosing limits.</p>
<p><strong>Identity and accounting.</strong> Every call carries the calling product and run id, attached by the harness, not the model. Without it the platform cannot tell misuse from growth. Usage is metered per product on each resource.</p>
<p><strong>Admission control: the decision.</strong> Two defensible designs. (a) Hard per-product quotas on concurrency and tokens: simple, predictable isolation; the cost is idle capacity when other products are quiet, and quota negotiations on every launch. (b) Shared pool with weighted fair queuing plus reserved capacity for interactive lanes: higher utilisation, and the heavy product gets spare capacity when it exists; the cost is that interactive latency now depends on the scheduler's correctness, and fairness is harder to explain to six directors. I would ship (b) with a hard floor for interactive lanes, because the index service cannot grow this half and idle reserved capacity is the scarcest thing to waste. The floor is the quantity to defend: size it from the measured peak concurrency of the interactive products plus headroom you choose. If interactive peak is 40 concurrent index calls (scenario) out of a service ceiling of 100 and you choose 25 percent headroom, the floor is 50 and batch gets at most 50 at peak and all 100 when interactive traffic is idle. Headroom of zero fails at the first interactive spike; a floor above the service ceiling starves batch entirely.</p>
<p><strong>Per-run budgets.</strong> The harness caps tool calls, tokens and wall time per run, by product. A prompt change that makes an agent call search in a loop exhausts its own budget and fails with a clear error, rather than draining the shared pool. This is the defence against misuse that is a bug rather than load.</p>
<p><strong>Constraint conflict.</strong> The heavy product's launch date against everyone else's latency. The batch lane runs at whatever capacity the interactive floor leaves, so the background job completes later but completes. I give up the heavy product's launch-day throughput, and I write down the expected completion time of its job under the new lane so its director can decide whether to buy dedicated capacity.</p>
<p><strong>Failure mode of the recommended answer.</strong> Weighted fair queuing isolates per product, not per tenant inside a product. If the misuse moves inside an interactive product (one large customer's agents looping), the interactive lane degrades for that product's other customers. Per-run budgets catch loops; they do not catch one legitimate heavy customer, so the next step is per-customer weights inside each product.</p>
<p><strong>Written artefact: rollout plan.</strong> Week 1: identity on every call, metering, per-run budgets in log-only mode. Week 2: budgets enforced; batch lane live for the heavy product. Week 3: interactive floor enforced. Rollback trigger: interactive p95 tool latency above the agreed budget for a sustained window (both the threshold and the window are set with the products, not chosen by the platform alone), or error rate from budget rejections above the rate seen in log-only mode.</p>
</Fragment>

### Rubric

- Asks which shared resource saturates first before choosing a limit
- Attaches caller identity in the harness, not through the model, and meters per product
- Compares hard quotas with a fair-share pool and names the cost of each
- Reserves capacity for interactive lanes and gives batch use a lane rather than a ban
- Enforces per-run budgets so a looping agent fails itself
- States what the heavy product gives up and writes a rollout plan with rollback triggers
- Names per-customer contention inside one product as the remaining gap

## Follow-ups

- *Can the shared capability publish its own regression gate?* Yes: a change to the tool's description or schema changes how every product's agent calls it, so tool changes go through the same gate as harness changes, with each product's scenarios.
- *Who sets quotas when products disagree?* A published formula from measured demand and agreed latency budgets, reviewed on a fixed schedule, so the decision is not re-litigated at every incident.

## Common mistakes

- Rate-limiting by API key when all six products share one key to the provider.
- Banning the heavy product rather than giving it a lane, which moves the problem into a shadow integration.
- Relying on the model to respect a "call search at most N times" instruction instead of enforcing a budget in the harness.
