checklist:
  - Stated a time plan in the first two minutes and finished within two minutes of 45 on the recording
  - Asked or stated as assumptions the scoping answers that changed the design (who sees output, what a miss costs, what data may meet what), and the design visibly used them
  - "After card 1, partitioned retrieval, caches and logs by matter, enforced below the model, and named what partitioning costs (no cross-matter search, smaller per-matter caches)"
  - "After card 2, made every flag carry a playbook rule ID and the clause span, checked in code, and said what happens to flags the model cannot tie to a rule"
  - "After card 3, computed the cost of at least two options against the cap in front of the listener, chose one, and stated what it gives up"
  - Named a failure mode of the chosen budget option inside the answer, with the measurement that would catch it
  - "After card 4, answered with an eval and a decision rather than a defence: what a miss rate is measured on, and the condition under which the tool is pulled"
  - Revised earlier decisions when a later card invalidated them, out loud, instead of bolting the change on
  - Kept the lead between cards, choosing the next topic without waiting for a prompt from the listener
  - Ended with a written artefact on the board or page, such as a rollout plan with rollback triggers or a risk note with an owner
  - Judged a design with no human review of flags acceptable if the tool only ranks clauses for a lawyer who reads every contract anyway, and said why that changes the cost of a miss

## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Practice format, constructed for this problem">
Run this as a timed 45-minute round. A colleague reads the prompt and plays the interviewer; alone, record yourself and open each card at its minute without reading ahead. Afterwards, tick the probe list against the recording, not against memory.
</Callout>

The prompt the interviewer reads: "Design a contract-review assistant for a law firm. 400 lawyers. It reads a contract and flags clauses that deviate from the firm's playbook of preferred positions. You lead."

Open each card only at its minute.

<details class="hint"><summary>Card 1, minute 10</summary><p>"The firm acts for clients who are in conflict with each other. Material from one matter may never be seen by people or systems working on a conflicting matter. How does your design hold that?"</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Card 2, minute 20</summary><p>"The firm's insurer now requires that every flag can be traced to the playbook rule that produced it. A flag with no rule is a liability."</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Card 3, minute 30</summary><p>"Budget. You planned 1,500 contracts a week at $6 of model spend each. Spend is now capped at $3,000 a week. About a third of contracts arrive on the counterparty's paper; the rest are on the firm's templates with edits. A small model costs about $0.80 a contract to screen every clause."</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Card 4, minute 38</summary><p>"A senior partner says the tool missed an uncapped indemnity last week. She wants it pulled from her practice group. What do you do?"</p></details>

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Before the first card, ask what a missed deviation costs compared with a false flag, and who reads the flags.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>When the budget card arrives, ask what share of clauses on the firm's own templates the playbook flags today.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Lead with a plan: scope for five minutes, request path for ten, deep dives on retrieval of playbook rules and on evaluation, then failure and rollout. Card 1 partitions every store by matter and enforces the partition in the retrieval service and cache keys, not in the prompt. Card 2 turns flags into structured output (rule ID, clause span, explanation) with a code check that the rule exists and the span is in the document; flags without a rule are dropped and counted. Card 3 has two answers that fit $3,000: screen every clause with the small model and send only flagged clauses to the large one, or review only counterparty-paper contracts with the large model. Card 4 is answered with the miss rate on a labelled set for that clause type and a condition for pulling the tool.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Before the cards.</strong> The scoping answers that move this design: lawyers read the flags, so a false flag costs a minute and a miss costs whatever the clause costs the client; contracts are confidential client material; the playbook is a few hundred rules owned by the knowledge-management team. The request path: upload into a matter, clause segmentation, retrieval of the playbook rules relevant to each clause type, a model call per clause or clause group, structured flags, a review screen.</p>
<p><strong>Card 1.</strong> Ethical walls are access control, and the design enforces them where the model cannot reach around them: each matter's documents, embeddings, prefix cache entries and logs are keyed by matter, the retrieval service filters by the caller's matter permissions from the firm's conflicts system, and the playbook (firm-wide, no client material) is the only shared index. The cost: no cross-matter search ("how did we negotiate this clause before?"), which lawyers will ask for, and smaller caches.</p>
<p><strong>Card 2.</strong> The model returns flags as structured records, and code checks that the rule ID exists in the current playbook version and the span is in the document. A deviation the model sees with no rule to cite is not a flag; it goes to a separate "unmatched" list that the knowledge-management team reviews weekly, which is also how the playbook grows. The design changes made earlier still hold, but the prompt now carries rule IDs, so retrieval of rules becomes the component to evaluate.</p>
<p><strong>Card 3: two defensible answers.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><em>Screen then escalate.</em> The small model screens every clause at $0.80 a contract; the large model sees only screened-positive clauses. If the screen flags a fifth of the clauses, large-model spend is about $6 × 0.2 = $1.20, so $2.00 a contract and 1,500 × $2.00 = $3,000 a week. Every contract gets reviewed. Cost: the screen's misses are never seen by the large model, and the screen's recall has to be measured per clause type before anyone relies on it. If the screen flags more than a fifth, the plan exceeds the cap.</li>
<li><em>Review counterparty paper only.</em> 500 contracts a week at $6 is $3,000. Counterparty paper is where most deviations are. Cost: edits made to the firm's own templates are not reviewed at all, and an uncapped indemnity inserted into firm paper passes silently.</li>
</ul>
<p>I choose screen-then-escalate, because it keeps coverage of every contract and its weakness is measurable, while the other option's weakness is a blind spot by construction. The conflict is coverage against cost, under a cap set by someone else. Failure mode of the choice: the screen is weakest on exactly the unusual clauses that matter most, so I measure its recall on a labelled set weighted toward high-severity rules, and route high-severity clause types (indemnity, limitation of liability) to the large model regardless of the screen. That routing costs money, so the screen's threshold is tuned to stay inside the cap with it.</p>
<p><strong>Card 4.</strong> One miss is a data point, and also a test case: it goes into the eval set the same day. The question to answer is the miss rate for uncapped indemnities on a labelled set from her practice area, under the current pipeline, and whether the miss came from the screen, retrieval of the rule, or the large model. The decision: if the rate on that clause type exceeds what the practice group agrees is tolerable, indemnity clauses in her group route to mandatory human review and the tool stays as a ranking aid; she owns that threshold. Pulling the tool entirely is the outcome if the miss rate cannot be measured within two weeks.</p>
<p><strong>Artefact.</strong> A rollout plan: one practice group for four weeks with every flag reviewed and misses logged, expansion only if the high-severity miss rate stays under the agreed threshold, rollback to ranking-only on any high-severity miss without a corresponding eval case.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Pushback: the screen's measured recall on indemnity clauses is lower than the large model's, and routing them all to the large model breaks the cap. Which clause types lose large-model review, and who signs that?</em></li>
<li><em>Reversal: what would the first month's data have to show for you to switch to reviewing counterparty paper only?</em></li>
<li><em>The knowledge-management team and the partners disagree on whether unmatched deviations should be shown to lawyers. How do you decide without deciding by rank?</em></li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

The 20% screen rate in the worked answer is an assumption, not a card value; a reader who asks for it (Hint 2) and plans around the answer is doing the work the card asks for. Any option that fits the cap with its arithmetic shown is acceptable; an option that keeps the original plan and "optimises prompts" to reach a third of the cost is not, because nothing in it says where two thirds of the spend goes.

## Follow-ups

- *Lawyers ask for cross-matter search after launch. What has to be true of the conflicts system before you build it?*
- *The playbook changes monthly. How do you re-run old contracts, and do you?*
- *How do you staff the weekly review of unmatched deviations, and what happens when it falls behind?*

## Common mistakes

- Treating each card as a bolt-on and not revising decisions the card invalidated, such as a shared cache after the ethical-walls card.
- Enforcing ethical walls with a prompt instruction.
- Answering the budget card with "use a cheaper model" and no arithmetic against the cap.
- Defending the tool against the partner's report instead of measuring the clause type she named.
- Letting the interviewer pick the topic after every card, so the round is led from the other side of the table.
