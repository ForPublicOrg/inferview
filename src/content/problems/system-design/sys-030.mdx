## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 45 minutes">
Design a customer-service agent for an online electronics retailer that follows the returns and refunds policy and can refund money. The deterministic limits must live outside the model.
</Callout>

Scenario, not a real retailer. The policy is a 14-page document owned by the operations team, who change it most weeks: promotional exceptions, new product categories, holiday return windows. Legal requires that two customers in the same situation get the same outcome and that every refund can be traced to the rule that allowed it. Customers can also act on their own orders in the web app during the conversation (cancel, change address, start a return). Engineering is three people; operations will not learn to write code.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask operations for the last ten policy changes and sort them by what they changed: a number, a condition, or a new kind of case.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how the agent finds out that the customer changed the order in another tab.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask legal what counts as "the same situation": which order fields, and whether the conversation itself can change the outcome.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The policy becomes data: a rules table with typed conditions over order fields (category, days since delivery, condition, promotion) and outcomes (refund amount, restocking fee, approval needed), edited by operations in a form with test cases, versioned and evaluated by an engine the agent calls. The agent's job is to collect facts and explain; <code>get_eligibility</code> returns the outcome and the rule ID, and <code>issue_refund</code> accepts only that outcome. Policy changes ship through a suite of generated cases, and the conversation eval is dual-control because customers act in the app mid-conversation.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The decision: how prose becomes limits.</b> Three defensible answers. (a) Engineers translate each change into code. Exact and testable; costs a week of lag per change with three engineers, and operations will route around it with manual credits. (b) Rules as data that operations edit, with an engine evaluating them. Same-situation-same-outcome by construction and traceable by rule ID; costs building the editor and accepting that some policy cannot be expressed as conditions on order fields. (c) The prose document in the prompt plus a model-based checker on each refund. No translation step; it fails legal's requirement, because two runs of the same case can differ and "the rule that allowed it" is a model's paraphrase. I choose (b), with (a) for the cases (b) cannot express, and anything neither covers goes to a person.</p>
<p><b>The quantity I choose: what the table can express.</b> Conditions are comparisons on typed order fields and a promotion code; outcomes are an amount formula from a fixed list (full, minus fee, store credit) and an approval flag. The sort from hint 1 decides it: numbers and conditions fit the table, while a new kind of case (a recall) is engineering work. If more than a third of changes are new kinds of case, (a) with faster review beats building (b).</p>
<p><b>Shipping a policy change.</b> Each rules version gets a generated test suite: for every rule, cases just inside and just outside each boundary, plus cases that match two rules to expose priority conflicts. Operations sees the outcome diff between the current and proposed versions on a sample of last month's real orders before publishing. Versions are immutable; the eligibility result records the version, so a refund issued under last week's rules is traceable after this week's change.</p>
<p><b>The agent.</b> Tools: <code>get_order</code>, <code>get_eligibility(order_id, facts)</code> where facts are the customer's claims the rules need (damaged on arrival, unopened), <code>issue_refund(order_id, outcome_id, key)</code>, <code>handoff</code>. The model never supplies an amount; it supplies an outcome ID the engine issued. Claims the model collected are recorded as claims, and the rules decide which need evidence (photos, a return scan) before money moves. Refunds marked for approval pause, and the side effect carries a key per logical action because approval does not guarantee single execution.</p>
<p><b>The customer acts too.</b> The customer may cancel or start a return in the web app while chatting. Eligibility is re-read immediately before <code>issue_refund</code>, and the refund call checks an order version so a stale decision fails with a typed error. The eval follows tau2-bench's dual-control setting, where the simulated user also changes the environment; the paper reports significant drops when agents move into it, and a no-user eval would overstate what ships.</p>
<p><b>Conflict and failure mode.</b> Policy agility against consistency and traceability. The weak point of the design is the facts the model collects: "it arrived damaged" is the customer's word, and the rules can only require evidence where operations thought to. A policy that refunds on the claim alone will be found by customers. The rollout watches refund rate per rule against the rate before the agent.</p>
<p><b>What you give up.</b> Policy that cannot be written as conditions goes to humans; operations gets an editor with guard rails instead of a document; three engineers spend their first quarter on an engine and an editor rather than the agent.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Operations publishes a rules version with a mistake that refunds a whole category in full. What stops it, and how fast do you roll back?</em> The outcome diff on last month's orders shows the jump before publishing; after publishing, a rate alarm per rule and a one-click revert to the previous immutable version.</li>
<li><em>Why not let the model read the rules table and decide?</em> Then the outcome depends on the model's reading; the engine makes it a function of the order.</li>
</ul>

### The written artefact

A policy-change runbook: who edits, which generated tests must pass, the outcome-diff review on historical orders, who publishes, and the revert path. Rollback trigger for any rules version: refund rate for any rule more than the threshold operations sets above its four-week average within 24 hours, or a legal complaint that cites inconsistent outcomes.

### Check your answer

- The model never supplies a refund amount.
- The policy representation is chosen against the three alternatives, with costs.
- Policy changes are versioned, tested at rule boundaries and traceable per refund.
- Customer actions mid-conversation are handled with a re-read and a version check.
- The eval includes the customer acting, not only talking.
- Customer claims are separated from verified facts.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the editor and engine take the whole quarter. What do you ship in the first month, and what risk does legal accept in writing for it?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in the change log to abandon the rules table and go back to engineers translating policy?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants an off-the-shelf rules engine, the other a 300-line evaluator. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*

## Common mistakes

- Putting the policy document in the prompt and calling the refund tool's amount limit "outside the model" when the model supplies the amount.
- Letting the rules decide on claims the model collected without marking which need evidence.
- Evaluating the agent only with customers who talk and never act.
- Mutable policy versions, so last week's refunds cannot be explained.
