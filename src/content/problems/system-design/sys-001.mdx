## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt, as a public interview guide reports it">
"Design a support assistant. How do you know it's helping and not making things worse?"
</Callout>

The case, constructed for this problem:

- A subscription software company with a help centre of a few thousand articles and about 25,000 support contacts a month, handled by human agents in an incumbent ticketing system.
- The ticketing system cannot be changed this year: no new fields, no new workflows, no webhooks beyond the ones it has. It accepts tickets through its existing email intake and its public ticket-creation API, and it exports tickets nightly.
- The assistant lives in the web app's help panel. Launch is answer-only; refunds and plan changes stay with humans.

Design the assistant, the handoff into the ticketing system, and the measurement that answers the second sentence of the prompt. Choose the comparison you would run and its size. State what you give up because the ticketing system cannot change.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what the ticketing system's creation API accepts, and whether any free-text field survives into the nightly export.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how the company would identify the same customer on a ticket and in the web app today.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Request last quarter's contacts by topic, with the share that support resolved in one reply.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A read-only assistant: retrieval over versioned help articles, answers that cite an article version, a check that every citation was retrieved, and a handoff that creates a ticket through the existing API with a summary and the conversation ID written into the ticket body, since no new field is allowed. "Helping" is resolution without a ticket; "not worse" is a ticket from the same account within seven days, and escalation after an answer, both compared with a holdout of accounts that keep today's help panel. The ticketing constraint costs measurement precision: re-contacts are matched by account and time window from the nightly export, not linked by ID.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Request path.</strong> The web app authenticates the user, so the assistant knows the account and plan. The query is rewritten with the conversation, hybrid retrieval runs over help articles with their version IDs, a reranker orders the passages, the model answers with citations, and a code check rejects any citation not in the retrieved set. A topic classifier marks billing disputes, security and account-ownership questions as never-answer, which go straight to handoff.</p>
<p><strong>Handoff into a system that cannot change.</strong> Two workable options. Create the ticket through the public API with the summary and the conversation ID in the body: the human sees context at once, and the ID survives into the export as text, which a nightly job can parse. Or send the customer to the existing email intake with a prefilled message: no API integration to maintain, but the customer can edit or drop the ID, and the summary is whatever they keep. I choose the API, because the ID is what makes the measurement work; the cost is an integration the support tooling team did not ask for and a parser that breaks if agents edit ticket bodies.</p>
<p><strong>Measurement.</strong> The success metric is the share of help-panel conversations that end without a ticket from that account within seven days. The guardrails are tickets within seven days on the same topic after an assistant answer, and handoffs after an answer. The comparison is a holdout of accounts, assigned by a stable hash of the account ID, that see the current help panel. The size is my choice: at 25,000 contacts a month, a 10% holdout gives about 2,500 contacts a month on the control side and 22,500 on the treatment side. For a rate near 50% the standard error of the difference is √(0.25/2,500 + 0.25/22,500) ≈ 1.1 points, so a change of about 3 points is readable within a month. A 2% holdout (500 a month) gives √(0.25/500 + 0.25/24,500) ≈ 2.3 points and needs about five months to reach the same precision. I accept 10% of customers on the old experience for one quarter.</p>
<p><strong>Offline evals.</strong> 20 to 50 real tickets from last quarter with known resolutions to start, including tickets whose right outcome is a handoff, grown from every failure triaged after launch; each case run several times so pass^k shows consistency, not one lucky run.</p>
<p><strong>What I give up.</strong> Exact re-contact linking: matching by account and time window misses customers who write in from another email address and over-counts customers with two unrelated problems. The holdout makes that bias roughly equal on both sides, so the comparison holds even where the absolute rate is off.</p>
<p><strong>Where this fails.</strong> The conversation ID lives in a free-text body. If support agents routinely edit or forward tickets, the ID disappears from a share of exports, and that share may differ by topic. A weekly check of the share of handoff tickets whose ID parses is the alarm; below an agreed level, the re-contact guardrail stops being trusted for that week.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Resolution without a ticket went up 8 points and the same-topic ticket rate went up 3 points against the holdout. Do you keep it?</em></li>
<li><em>The support tooling team refuses to let anything write ticket bodies through the API. What do you measure instead, and what precision do you lose?</em></li>
<li><em>When would you add plan changes, and what has to be true of the read-only version first?</em></li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

The 10% holdout and the seven-day window are chosen quantities. A holdout between 5% and 20% is reasonable at this volume; below about 2% the control side is too small to read within a quarter, and above about 25% the assistant reaches too few customers to learn from. A window from three to fourteen days is defensible; one day misses customers who try the answer before coming back, and thirty days mixes in new problems.

## Follow-ups

- *The help centre is out of date on a third of billing topics. Whose incident is a wrong answer from a stale article, and what changes in the design?*
- *What would you log per conversation, given that the ticketing system keeps nothing you add?*
- *Six months in, the support director asks to remove the holdout because the assistant "obviously works". What do you keep instead?*

## Common mistakes

- Treating "add a field to the ticket" as free when the prompt says the system cannot change, so the measurement rests on a field that will not exist.
- Measuring "helping" by thumbs-up rate, which is sparse and comes mostly from satisfied users.
- No comparison group, so a seasonal drop in tickets is credited to the assistant.
- Treating handoff as failure in the success metric, which rewards answering questions that should go to a human.
- An eval set of questions the assistant should answer, with no cases whose correct outcome is the handoff.
