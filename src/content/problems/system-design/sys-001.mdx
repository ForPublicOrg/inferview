## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt, as a public interview guide reports it">
"Design a support assistant. How do you know it's helping and not making things worse?"
</Callout>

You have 40 minutes. The company sells a subscription software product, has a help centre of a few thousand articles and a ticketing system staffed by human agents. Nothing else is specified; ask for what you need. The interviewer will spend the last ten minutes on the second sentence of the prompt.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Before drawing anything, find out whether the assistant only answers or can also act on accounts, and who sees its mistakes.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>"Helping" needs a success metric and "not making things worse" needs a guardrail metric and a comparison group.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>An eval plan has an offline part you run on every change and an online part you watch after launch.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Read-only assistant first: hybrid retrieval over versioned help articles, an answer with citations, and a handoff to a human with a summary when confidence is low or the user asks. "Helping" is resolution without a human; "not worse" is re-contact on the same issue and escalation after a bad answer, measured against a holdout that keeps the old flow. Before launch, a set of real tickets with expected outcomes runs on every change, several times each, and a calibrated judge scores sampled conversations after launch.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Scoping. I ask: answer-only or actions (refunds, plan changes)? Which channels? Which languages? What does a wrong answer cost here? What is the current resolution path? I assume answer-only at launch with actions later, chat in the web app, English first.</p>
<p>Architecture. Auth and account lookup, conversation store, query rewrite using the conversation, hybrid retrieval (BM25 plus vectors) over help articles with their version IDs, rerank, answer with citations, an output check that every citation was in the retrieved set, then reply or hand off. The handoff is a component: a trigger (low retrieval score, a policy topic, a user request, two failed turns), a summary written for the human, and the conversation attached to the ticket.</p>
<p>Offline evals. Start with about twenty real tickets with known resolutions, the way Anthropic started its research-agent evals, and grow the set from every failure triaged after launch. Score retrieval (did the right article come back) separately from the answer (correct, grounded, right tone). Run each case several times and report pass^k, because the same question asked twice should get the same right answer.</p>
<p>Online. Success metric: share of conversations resolved without a human. Guardrails: re-contact on the same issue within a fixed window, escalations that follow an assistant answer, thumbs-down rate, and cost per resolved conversation. Compare against a holdout of users who keep the current flow, because ticket volume moves for reasons unrelated to the assistant. A judge model scores a daily sample for groundedness, checked against human labels before anyone trusts it.</p>
<p>Making things worse looks like: fewer tickets but more re-contacts, confident answers about features that do not exist, or users who give up. Each has a metric above, and each has an alert.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Resolution rate went up and CSAT went down. What happened?</em> Probably deflection: users stopped asking, not got answers. Check re-contact and churn for the treatment group before celebrating.</li>
<li><em>The help centre is out of date. Now what?</em> Retrieval returns stale articles confidently. Log the cited article on every answer, surface the most-cited articles with poor outcomes to the docs team, and treat the help centre as part of the system.</li>
<li><em>When would you add actions?</em> After the read-only version holds its guardrails, one action at a time, each behind a policy tool and an approval step.</li>
</ul>

### A design that scores well

The rubric this track uses has four parts; this is what a strong answer shows in each.

**Problem navigation.** The action boundary and the cost of a wrong answer were asked about in the first five minutes, and the answers changed the design (read-only at launch).

**Solution design.** The request path is complete: auth, state, retrieval with versions, answer with citations, citation check, handoff. The handoff has a trigger and a summary, not just an arrow to "human".

**Technical excellence.** At least one deep dive at working depth, for example: why citations point at article versions, how the eval set is seeded and grown, or why pass^k rather than a single success rate.

**Communication.** The answer to "how do you know" was a metric, a guardrail and a comparison group, said in that order.

## Follow-ups

- *How big is the offline set at launch and in six months?* Small at launch (tens of real cases), then every triaged failure is added. A set that never grows stops catching new failures.
- *How do you test the assistant with a user who also acts, such as one who changes their plan mid-conversation?* That is the dual-control setting of tau2-bench; simulate such users in the eval set, because agents score noticeably lower there.
- *What would you log?* The prompt version, retrieved article IDs and versions, model ID, latency per stage, token usage, the handoff decision and the final outcome, all keyed by conversation.

## Common mistakes

- Starting with the vector database before asking whether the assistant can act.
- Answering "how do you know it's helping" with "user feedback". Thumbs are sparse and biased; they are one signal among several.
- No comparison group, so a seasonal drop in tickets is credited to the assistant.
- Treating escalation as failure. A fast, well-summarised handoff is a success for questions the assistant should not answer.
