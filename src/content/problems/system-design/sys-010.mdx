## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Design a voice assistant for our retail support line. The first audio of every reply must start in under a second."
</Callout>

The assistant answers order and delivery questions over the phone and in the browser, and looks orders up through an internal API whose P50 is 400 ms and P95 1.2 s (scenario values). Legal requires that the assistant never promises a refund; refunds go to a person. Three engineers, eight weeks to a pilot on 5 percent of calls. The product lead took "under a second" from a vendor deck.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether "under a second" is a P50 or a P95, and from which event (the caller stops speaking, or the endpointer decides they stopped) to which (first audio sent, or first audio heard).</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask for per-stage timings from any existing voice system the company runs, measured on phone audio rather than a headset.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask legal whether a refund promise that is spoken and then corrected counts as a promise.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>I treat "under a second" as one vendor's target (Exotel's page sets about 800 ms) and not as a standard: the Pipecat maintainers' June 2026 primer recommends 1,500 ms and measures a typical stack at about 1.3 s, and ElevenLabs' illustrative budget is about 680 ms at P50 and 1,560 ms at P95 when every stage streams into the next. So I would commit to a P50 under a second and a P95 under 1.6 s for turns without a tool call, and say that tool turns are slower. The architecture is a chained pipeline (streaming speech-to-text, a text model, streaming text-to-speech) because legal's refund rule needs the text checked before it is spoken; that costs latency against a speech-to-speech session, and I recover it by overlapping stages and starting speech on the first checked clause. What I give up is the lowest possible latency and the paralinguistic quality of a native audio model.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The budget.</b> Endpointing, transcript finalisation, network, model time to first token, a policy check on the first clause, TTS first audio, playout buffer. With ElevenLabs' P50 figures that is 680 ms before the policy check; a check on the first clause with a small classifier adds its own latency to every turn, and I would measure it before promising a number. The Pipecat measurement shows where real stacks spend time: 300 ms in transcription and endpointing and 650 ms in model time to first byte, out of 1,293 ms.</p>
<p><b>Tool turns.</b> An order lookup at P95 1.2 s cannot fit a sub-second turn. I prefetch the caller's recent orders when the call connects (the phone number or login identifies them), so most order questions are answered from context, and for the rest the assistant says a fixed short line while the lookup runs.</p>
<p><b>Turn detection.</b> A fixed silence threshold either cuts callers off or adds hundreds of milliseconds to every turn. Layered detection (a short VAD trigger, a small audio model that classifies end of turn, and the text model's own judgement) is what the Pipecat primer recommends; it trades a little compute per turn for fewer false endpoints.</p>
<p><b>Barge-in.</b> When the caller speaks over the assistant, playback stops and the conversation record is truncated to what was actually played, so the model does not believe it said things the caller never heard.</p>
<p><b>Cost.</b> On a speech-to-speech model the history is sent again with each response. At gpt-realtime-2.1 audio prices, with user audio at 1 token per 100 ms and assistant audio at 1 per 50 ms, a 10-turn call with 5 s of caller and 10 s of assistant audio per turn costs about $0.50; 20 turns cost about $1.81, 3.6 times as much for twice the turns; a 90 percent cache hit brings 20 turns to about $0.46 (computed below). A chained pipeline sends text history, which is far fewer tokens, so it is cheaper per minute as well as checkable.</p>
<p><b>Failure mode of this design.</b> The clause-level policy check is the weakest point. A refund promise can span two clauses ("I can do that for you. The refund will..."), and a check on the first clause passes it. I would check a sliding window of the last two clauses and hold playback when the classifier is uncertain, accepting that held turns are slow; the hold rate is a launch metric.</p>

### The numbers you were asked to choose

The latency target is yours to set. A P50 under a second for turns without tools is defensible from the published budgets; a P95 under a second is not, on any stack in the sources. Anything stated without a percentile, or measured from the endpointer's decision rather than the caller's last word, is not a target.

The session cost, computed from OpenAI's audio token rates and gpt-realtime-2.1 prices:

```python
P_IN, P_CACHED, P_OUT = 32.0, 0.40, 64.0   # USD per 1M audio tokens, checked 2026-10-28

def session_cost(turns, user_s, agent_s, cache_hit=0.0):
    history, total = 0.0, 0.0
    for _ in range(turns):
        user_tok, agent_tok = user_s * 10, agent_s * 20   # 1 per 100 ms in, 1 per 50 ms out
        reused = history * (cache_hit * P_CACHED + (1 - cache_hit) * P_IN)
        total += (reused + user_tok * P_IN + agent_tok * P_OUT) / 1e6
        history += user_tok + agent_tok
    return total

for turns, hit in [(10, 0.0), (20, 0.0), (20, 0.9)]:
    minutes = turns * 15 / 60
    c = session_cost(turns, 5, 10, hit)
    print(f"{turns} turns, {minutes:.1f} min, cache hit {hit:.0%}: ${c:.3f} (${c / minutes:.3f}/min)")
```

```text
20 turns, 5.0 min, cache hit 0%: $1.808 ($0.362/min)
20 turns, 5.0 min, cache hit 90%: $0.457 ($0.091/min)
```

### Two defensible architectures

**Chained pipeline.** Text exists before speech, so the refund rule can be enforced, vendors can be swapped per stage, and the transcript is free. It costs extra hops and a policy check on the critical path, and it loses tone and interruption handling that a native audio model does in one place.

**Speech-to-speech session.** Fewest hops, the lowest latency, natural prosody and native interruption handling. It costs checkability: moderation runs on the transcript stream after the audio is generated, so a forbidden sentence can be partly spoken before playback stops. It is the right answer if legal accepts "stopped mid-sentence and corrected" as compliant.

### What you give up

With the chained pipeline: the last few hundred milliseconds of latency, and voice quality on emotional calls. With the target as stated: tool turns, which will not meet it, and that has to be said before the pilot rather than discovered in it.

### Rollout plan

Pilot on 5 percent of calls, assigned by a stable hash of the caller ID. Rollback triggers, each checked daily: P95 time to first audio above 1.6 s on non-tool turns for two days; any refund promise found in a daily sample of 200 transcripts; transfer-to-person rate more than 5 points above the control line. Each trigger has an owner and the rollback is a routing flag, not a deploy.

## Follow-ups

- *Callers in a noisy environment trigger false endpoints. Which number do you change first, and what does it cost every other caller?*
- *The internal order API's P95 doubles during a sale. What does the assistant say, and when does it hand off?*
- *Recording every call for evals needs consent and a retention period. Who sets the period, and what breaks in your eval plan if it is 30 days?*

## Common mistakes

- Quoting 800 ms as an industry standard rather than one vendor's target, with no percentile.
- Adding stage P95s and calling the sum the P95; tails rarely line up, so it is a pessimistic bound.
- Choosing speech-to-speech without saying how a forbidden sentence is stopped before it is heard.
- Leaving the tool call on the critical path with no prefetch and no spoken filler.
- Pricing a call per minute as if the history were not resent each turn.
