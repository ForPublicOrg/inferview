checklist:
  - Compares treatment with the holdout over the same weeks before reading the before-and-after numbers, and computes the launch's effect on resolution and on re-contact from the arms
  - Converts both metrics into one unit the support team uses (human contacts or agent-hours per week) and asks for the handle-time ratio of a re-contact to a fresh ticket instead of assuming 1
  - Designs the labelling sample with the 16-hour budget, allocating it across topics and failure types, and computes what a clean result can and cannot rule out
  - Separates harm (a wrong statement the customer acted on) from annoyance (an incomplete answer), and refuses to net harm against resolutions elsewhere
  - Lays out at least three options (full rollback, rollback by topic, reduced exposure, fix forward), each with its cost in contacts or customers affected
  - Picks one with a written reversal condition and threshold, and states what it gives up
  - Names one owner for the decision and the person who accepts the remaining risk, and says why they differ
  - Handles the product lead's quarterly-review deadline without letting it set the decision, for example by reporting the guardrail beside the headline metric
  - Includes remediation for customers who received a wrong statement, with the log fields needed to find them
  - Writes the rollback as steps with state (traffic share, routing rule, who flips it, how long a full rollback takes) and the trigger that fires each step
  - States a failure mode of the chosen option, such as a topic classifier that misroutes, and the measurement that would catch it
  - Judges keeping the new version for unregulated topics acceptable if the labelled sample shows no wrong policy statements there and the holdout stays in place

## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Staff design round, 40 minutes">
"Two weeks after launch, the support assistant's resolution rate is up and so are re-contacts. Decide whether to roll back. Show the analysis, the process, and who owns the call."
</Callout>

The case, constructed for this problem:

- A consumer subscription service. The assistant handles about 60,000 conversations a week in the app; unresolved ones become tickets for a human team.
- Version 2 launched 14 days ago: a new retrieval index over the help centre plus billing policy documents, and a new prompt. 5% of accounts, assigned by a stable hash of the account ID, stayed on version 1 as a holdout.
- Last 14 days, treatment against holdout: resolved without a ticket 55% against 45%; a ticket from the same account within 7 days after a resolved conversation, 14% of resolved against 9%.
- Support operations counts re-contacts by matching account and time window in the ticket export. They say re-contact tickets are angrier and take longer; nobody has measured how much longer.
- Two support leads can label conversations for one day each, 16 hours in total.
- The product lead reports the launch at the quarterly business review in 10 days and wants to keep version 2. The support director wants it rolled back today.

Design the decision workflow: what you compute first, the labelling plan, the options with their costs, the decision and who signs it, the rollback mechanics if any, and what happens to customers who were told something wrong. Write the decision memo's last paragraph.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask support operations for the median handle time of re-contact tickets and of first-contact tickets over the same two weeks.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request re-contact rates split by the topic of the original conversation, for both arms.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what the assistant logs per conversation: retrieved document IDs, the answer as sent, the prompt and index versions.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Do not roll back everything today, and do not wait for the business review. The holdout shows the launch caused both changes. Per 1,000 conversations the new version resolves 550 and sends 77 back (14% of 550) against 450 and 40.5 (9% of 450) on the holdout, so human tickets fall from 590.5 to 527: the launch saves work unless a re-contact costs about 2.7 times a fresh ticket to handle. The open question is harm, so the 16 labelling hours go to re-contacts from the topics where the rate rose most, with billing and cancellation oversampled. Billing and cancellation route back to version 1 from tomorrow while the sample is labelled, because those are the topics where a wrong statement is acted on. The support director owns the decision to keep version 2 elsewhere and signs the remaining risk; the product lead reports both metrics at the review.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Step 1: effect, from the arms.</strong> The holdout ran over the same 14 days, so the season, the price change and the release calendar are on both sides. Per 1,000 conversations: treatment resolves 550, of which 77 re-contact; holdout resolves 450, of which 40.5. Tickets reaching humans: 450 + 77 = 527 against 550 + 40.5 = 590.5. The launch removes about 64 tickets per 1,000 conversations, about 3,800 a week at 60,000 conversations, at equal handle time.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: one unit.</strong> Re-contacts are 77 of the 527. If a re-contact takes w times a fresh ticket, treatment load is 450 + 77w against 550 + 40.5w for the holdout. They are equal at w = 100 / 36.5 ≈ 2.7. Support's claim that re-contacts take longer is therefore a question about whether they take nearly three times longer, which the ticket export can answer today.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: the labelling plan.</strong> Sixteen hours at about four minutes a conversation, reading the conversation and the re-contact ticket, is about 240 cases. The choice is the allocation. I put 120 into billing and cancellation re-contacts, 80 into the two other topics with the largest rise, and 40 into a random sample of the remaining re-contacts, each labelled as unrelated new issue, incomplete answer, wrong answer, or wrong policy statement the customer acted on. What a clean result means: zero wrong policy statements in 120 billing cases puts the 95% upper bound on that rate at about 3 / 120 = 2.5% of billing re-contacts, which rules out a widespread problem and not a rare one.</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: options and costs.</strong> Full rollback: re-contacts return to the holdout rate, and the 3,800 tickets a week saved come back to the human team. Rollback by topic: keeps most of the saving; costs a routing rule and a topic classifier that misroutes some conversations. Reduced exposure, 50% of accounts: halves both effects and keeps the comparison sharp; costs a week of slower learning on the rest. Fix forward: keeps everything, accepts 77 re-contacts per 1,000 until the fix lands.</p>
<p><strong>Step 5: decision and owners.</strong> Billing and cancellation route to version 1 from tomorrow, before the sample is in, because wrong statements there are acted on and cannot be netted against resolutions. Everything else stays on version 2 pending the sample. The assistant team lead owns the decision and the routing change; the support director accepts in writing the remaining re-contacts on non-billing topics, because the cost lands on their queue; the product lead reports resolution and re-contact side by side at the review, with the topic rollback as part of the result.</p>
<p><strong>Step 6: rollback mechanics.</strong> Version routing is a flag keyed by topic and account hash, flipped by the on-call engineer on the assistant team; a full rollback is the same flag for all topics and takes effect on the next conversation, with no redeploy. Triggers: any wrong policy statement in the non-billing sample triggers a full rollback that day; a re-contact rate for the rerouted topics that has not returned within two points of the holdout after seven days triggers a widened topic list.</p>
<p><strong>Step 7: remediation.</strong> For each wrong policy statement found, query the logs for conversations that retrieved the same document version and produced the same claim, and contact those customers through support with the correct terms. That needs the retrieved document IDs and the answer as sent on every conversation; if the logs do not have them, the memo says so and the remediation becomes a broader notice.</p>
<p><strong>Where this fails.</strong> The topic classifier routes by the first message. Billing questions that start as something else ("my account looks wrong") reach version 2, so the rerouted topics will not fully return to the holdout rate, and a re-contact rate that stays high on them is the signal. The second failure is the sample: 240 cases can miss a harm that occurs in under 1% of re-contacts, and the memo states that bound rather than "no harm found".</p>
<p><strong>The memo's last paragraph.</strong> "Version 2 stays live for all topics except billing and cancellation, which return to version 1 on 29 October. Owner: assistant team lead. The support director accepts the remaining non-billing re-contact rate until 12 November. We reverse to a full rollback if the labelled sample contains any wrong policy statement outside billing, or if re-contact handle time exceeds 2.5 times first-contact handle time."</p>
</Fragment>

Reasonable choices: a sample allocation that oversamples the topics with the most consequential wrong answers and keeps some random cases is right; an allocation spread evenly across all topics spends most of the budget where harm is unlikely. A trigger threshold for handle time between 2 and 2.7 times is defensible against the 2.7 break-even; a threshold above 2.7 means the trigger fires only after the launch has stopped saving work.

## Follow-ups

- *There was no holdout. What do you do in the first week, and what does the decision rest on until then?*
- *The labelled sample finds that a third of billing re-contacts quote a policy document that was superseded last month. Whose incident is it, and what changes in the pipeline?*
- *What would you change in the launch process so that the next version ships with this analysis already wired?*

## Common mistakes

- Reading the before-and-after numbers when a holdout exists over the same weeks.
- Adding resolutions and subtracting re-contacts as if both cost the same, without asking for handle time.
- Spending the labelling budget uniformly, so the high-consequence topics get a handful of cases.
- Reporting a clean sample as "no harm" instead of an upper bound.
- Letting the business review date set the decision, or naming the product lead as the person who accepts a risk that lands on the support queue.
- Writing "roll back if things get worse" with no metric, threshold or person who flips the flag.
