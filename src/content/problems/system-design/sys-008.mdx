## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 45 minutes">
Design an agent that handles customer support for a large music streaming service: billing questions, plan changes, playback problems and refunds. Then: you have run it for six months, the model it uses is being retired, and the replacement doubles the escalation rate in shadow traffic. What do you do?
</Callout>

Scenario, not the company's real systems: plans are individual, duo, family and student. Card and PayPal payments are refunded by the service; payments made through mobile app stores can only be refunded by the store. Today's agent handles 30,000 conversations a day and escalates 7 percent of them to a human team sized for that load. The retirement notice gives 60 days.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask for the list of actions human agents take today and which of them move money or cannot be undone.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how a payment's origin (card or app store) is visible to the systems the agent can call.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Before deciding about the new model, ask for the escalations broken down by the reason recorded at handoff.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>One agent loop over typed tools, with policy enforced in code: an eligibility tool returns the refundable amount and the rule, and the refund tool rejects anything else. Refunds up to an auto-approve threshold run with an idempotency key; above it, or out of policy, the run pauses for a supervisor; app-store payments get an explanation tool, not a refund tool. Every handoff carries a reason code. For the upgrade, I split the doubled escalations by reason before deciding, and ship per intent where both end-state pass^k and escalation hold, with the rest moved by the retirement date and the staffing cost written down.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Action tiers.</b> Following OpenAI's rating by write access, reversibility and financial impact: read (explain a charge, show plan, diagnose playback), reversible writes (resend receipt, reset password, re-sync downloads), money within policy (refund up to eligibility), out of policy (goodwill credits, refunds on older invoices), and account-structure changes (remove a family member, merge accounts), which hand off. The family-plan rule sits in the account tool: it returns the requester's role, and plan-change tools refuse when the requester is not the owner.</p>
<p><b>Policy in tools.</b> <code>get_refund_eligibility(invoice_id)</code> computes from billing rules and returns amount, rule and payment origin. For app-store origins it returns zero with the rule "refund through the store", and the agent's only tool is one that explains how. <code>issue_refund</code> rejects anything above eligibility with a typed error. The prompt describes policy so the agent asks the right questions; the executor enforces it.</p>
<p><b>The threshold, chosen and defended.</b> The auto-approve threshold for eligible refunds is the quantity I choose. One defensible answer is "one month of the customer's current plan": a single disputed charge is the common case to ask support to confirm from its data, and anything larger is a pattern a supervisor should see. The other is zero, every refund approved: safe, and it sends every refund conversation to the human queue, which the team is not sized for. I take one month, measure the refund reversal rate on the canary, and lower it if reversals rise.</p>
<p><b>Approvals and idempotency.</b> Out-of-policy requests become a pending action with the evidence attached, and the run waits on a checkpoint. LangGraph restarts the interrupted node from its beginning on resume, so the refund is in its own node after the approval, with a key from conversation, invoice and action.</p>
<p><b>Evaluation.</b> Tasks built from past conversations with checkable end states (refund row, plan state, ticket status), including tasks whose correct end state is a handoff, run several times each and reported as pass^k. The tau-bench paper reports pass^8 under 25 percent in retail for the agents it tested, which is why one run per task is not a gate.</p>
<p><b>The upgrade.</b> 30,000 × 7% = 2,100 escalations a day today; doubled, 4,200, which the team cannot absorb. The total says nothing about whether the new model is worse. Split by reason code: policy-required (the old model may have been resolving cases it should not; check the executor's rejection log), failure-threshold (worse at some intents; the eval should show which), and model-chose (more cautious than policy; fix with per-intent instructions and examples). Two defensible plans. Ship everywhere and staff up: correct behaviour, a cost leadership must sign. Ship per intent: intents that hold on both measures move now, the others are tuned through the notice period. I choose per intent with a fixed date, three weeks before retirement, after which the remainder ships untuned and staffing absorbs it.</p>
<p><b>Constraint conflict and failure mode.</b> Autonomy against safety on refunds, and a hard deadline against a measured regression. The plan's weak point is the reason code: the model writes it at handoff, so a model that escalates for the wrong reason may also label it wrongly. In the first week of each intent's rollout, a sample of handoffs is labelled by the human team and compared with the codes.</p>
<p><b>What you give up.</b> Two models in production for several weeks, an approval queue that adds hours to large refunds, and a refund path that cannot help app-store customers beyond an explanation.</p>
</Fragment>

### The written artefact

A rollout note with one row per intent: old and new end-state pass^k, old and new escalation rate by reason code, ship date, and owner. Rollback triggers: any intent's pass^k below the old model's on the nightly suite, refund reversals in the canary slice above the six-month baseline, or a handoff-label disagreement rate in the weekly sample above the rate the team agreed. A risk note signed by support leadership states the expected extra escalations per day for intents that ship untuned.

### Check your answer

- Refund limits are enforced by a tool, not by the prompt.
- App-store payments have a path that does not pretend to refund.
- The auto-approve threshold is a number you chose and defended.
- The refund side effect cannot run twice on resume.
- Releases are gated on repeated trials with end-state checks, including correct handoffs.
- The doubled escalation rate is split by reason before any decision.
- The plan meets the retirement date and names who absorbs the cost.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: your eval suite takes a day to run with eight trials per task. Which tasks drop to fewer trials, and what does that hide?*
- *Reversal: what evidence would make you remove the auto-approve threshold entirely?*
- *Operating: support leadership will fund extra staff for four weeks only. How does that change the per-intent schedule?*

## Common mistakes

- Writing the refund policy into the prompt and trusting the model to follow it.
- Treating the doubled escalation rate as a regression without asking which escalations grew.
- Measuring the new model on single runs per task.
- Placing the refund before the approval in the same resumable step.
- Ignoring the retirement deadline and planning as if the old model stays.
