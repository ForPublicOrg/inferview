## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, as a prep site reports it">
"Design an insurance-claims agent that ingests claims and outputs an approval decision using RAG. Cover infrastructure and storage, and how you control token cost."
</Callout>

What you learn by asking (scenario):

- A motor insurer. 4,000 claims a day. A claim bundle averages 42 pages: a claim form, repair invoices, photos, sometimes a police report or a medical letter. About half the PDFs have a text layer; the rest are scans or phone photos.
- Adjusters approve every claim today, taking about a day per claim end to end. Claimants are promised a decision within five working days.
- Finance caps model spend at $0.50 a claim. The head of claims wants windscreen claims under a fixed amount paid without an adjuster within a year. Compliance requires every decision to be explainable to the regulator.
- The claims system of record cannot be replaced; it accepts a decision and a reason code through an API.

Cover the steps, what the model does and does not decide, storage, the cost per claim, and how you would move toward the automatic windscreen payments. State what you give up and write the rollout plan for that automation with its rollback triggers.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A workflow, not an agent: store files by content hash, classify and send only pages without a text layer to vision, extract once in batch into a typed claim record with page citations, validate with rules, route low-confidence fields to reviewers, retrieve the policy clauses in force on the loss date, draft a recommendation with citations, apply a rules engine, and an adjuster decides. About $0.26 a claim at the scenario's sizes, dominated by the one-time page cost. Windscreen automation starts as a shadow, then a narrow auto-pay band with audits and written rollback triggers. What I give up: same-hour drafts, and some accuracy on the fields routed to the cheaper model.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>What the model decides.</strong> Nothing final at launch. The model extracts, classifies and drafts; rules apply coverage limits, deductibles and exclusions; an adjuster decides and the decision goes into the system of record with a reason code. "Agent" in the prompt is the wrong shape: the steps, approvers and evidence are fixed, so a workflow engine owns the order, the retries and the state, and the model is a step.</p>
<p><strong>Steps.</strong> Intake stores each file under its content hash, which is its version. A classifier labels file types and text-layer presence. Text-native pages go to text extraction; scans and photos go to vision. One extraction pass fills the claim record, every field carrying hash, page and region. Rules validate: invoice lines sum to totals, loss date inside the policy period, the policy covers the peril. Fields under a confidence threshold, scored as Instacart does by the probability of "yes" in a self-verification prompt, go to a reviewer screen beside the source page, as in Uber's review UI. Policy clauses are retrieved by product, version and effective date on the loss date. The model drafts with citations; the rules engine runs; the adjuster signs.</p>
<p><strong>Cost per claim, computed.</strong> On Claude, about 7,000 tokens per 3 pages in full visual mode makes the 42-page bundle 98,000 tokens. Extraction on Sonnet 5.5: 98,000 × $2 / 1M + 3,000 × $10 / 1M = $0.226, or $0.113 through the Batch API. Drafting from the record plus eight 800-token clauses: about $0.029. Five review turns of 12,000 tokens carrying the record: $0.12. About $0.26 in total, inside the cap with room for reruns. The design that re-sends the bundle on ten review turns spends $1.96 on input alone. Routing text-native pages away from vision lowers the extraction term further, and Gemini's 258 tokens per page (10,836 for the bundle) is the alternative family to price on the same documents before choosing.</p>
<p><strong>The decision: one large model or routed models.</strong> Two answers are defensible. <em>One large vision model for every field</em>: simplest, one prompt to evaluate, the best accuracy on line items, and Uber's experience that smaller fine-tuned models hallucinated line data argues for it. Cost: the full page price on every claim and no headroom when the cap tightens. <em>Route by field type</em>: dates, policy numbers and plate numbers to a small model, line items and damage descriptions to the large one, chosen by per-field accuracy on a labelled set. Instacart measured a cheaper model at parity on simple attributes for 70% less and a 60% accuracy drop on difficult ones, which is the shape of the risk. Cost: two models to evaluate on every change, and a routing table that goes stale. I would start with one model, because $0.26 is under the cap, and build the labelled set that makes routing a measured decision when the cap or volume changes.</p>
<p><strong>Storage.</strong> Object storage by hash for files, never overwritten; a relational store for claim records, fields, corrections and decisions; a vector and BM25 index over policy clauses with version metadata; the workflow engine's history as the step log.</p>
<p><strong>Windscreen automation, the rollout plan as sent.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Stage 1, eight weeks: shadow. The system produces an auto-pay decision for every windscreen claim under the amount; adjusters decide as today; we log agreement.</li>
<li>Stage 2: auto-pay where draft, rules and extraction confidence all agree, for 10% of eligible claims by stable hash of claim ID, with 1 in 20 audited after payment.</li>
<li>Rollback to adjusters if: audited overpayments exceed the rate adjusters have on the same audit; any payment exceeds the band; a new fraud pattern is confirmed in an auto-paid claim; extraction correction rate on any document source jumps week over week.</li>
<li>The agreement bar for Stage 2 is set by the head of claims and compliance before Stage 1 ends, from last year's adjuster-to-adjuster agreement on the same claim type.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>What I give up.</strong> Batch extraction adds hours, so a claim submitted in the morning is drafted later that day, not in minutes. A single model costs more per claim than a routed design would. Auto-pay waits at least eight weeks for shadow evidence.</p>
<p><strong>Where this fails.</strong> Confidence thresholds are calibrated on past documents. A new invoice template from a large repair chain can be extracted confidently and wrongly, and the field-level router will not send it to a reviewer. The guard is the per-source correction rate and the post-payment audit, and the auto-pay rollback trigger names that rate directly.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Pushback: finance halves the cap to $0.25 and the routed design takes four engineer-weeks to evaluate. Which do you do: route, switch family, or cut review turns, and what does compliance sign?</em></li>
<li><em>Reversal: what would you need to see in the shadow period to drop auto-pay plans entirely?</em> Agreement below adjuster-to-adjuster agreement on windscreen claims after fixing the top disagreement causes, or disagreements concentrated in a fraud-relevant pattern.</li>
<li><em>The regulator asks why claim 1234 was denied two years ago, and the drafting model has since been retired by its vendor. What do you show?</em> The stored record: document hashes, cited fields, clause IDs and policy version, rules-engine output, the stored draft and the adjuster's decision. Replay is from stored outputs, not re-execution.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>

### Checklist

- Treats it as a workflow with model steps and keeps the final decision with rules and a person at launch.
- Prices a page by model family and computes the cost per claim from stated sizes.
- Sends each page to a model once, extracts into a cited record, and uses batch for extraction.
- Routes only low-confidence fields to reviewers, with the threshold set from a labelled sample.
- Chooses between one model and routed models with the cost of each.
- Retrieves clauses for the policy version in force on the loss date.
- Stores files by content hash and a decision record that can be shown to a regulator.
- Gives an automation rollout with a shadow stage, an audit rate and written rollback triggers.
- Names a failure mode of the chosen design and the metric that catches it.

## Follow-ups

- *A claimant uploads a corrected invoice after the adjuster approved. What happens to the record and the decision?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree on whether the drafting model should see photos of the damage. How do you settle it without deciding by rank?*
- *Cost per claim doubles in a week with no change in volume. What do you check first?*

## Common mistakes

- An agent that decides and pays, with no rules layer and no person.
- Re-sending raw PDFs on every turn of a review conversation.
- Quoting page-token figures without naming the model family.
- Retrieving from all policy wording instead of the version in force on the loss date.
- Storing files by name, so a corrected upload overwrites the evidence for a past decision.
