## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt, as a prep site lists it">
Design an insurance-claims agent that ingests claims and outputs an approval decision using RAG. Cover infrastructure and storage choices, and how you control LLM token cost.
</Callout>

A claim arrives as a bundle: a claim form (PDF), photos, invoices, sometimes a scanned police report or a medical letter. The policy wording for each product is a few dozen pages. Adjusters review the agent's output. The prep site that reports this prompt attributes it to Scale AI's loop.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Who makes the final approval decision, and what must be on record when they do?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Work out what a 40-page PDF costs to send to a model, then how many times your design sends it.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Separate what is extracted once per claim from what is retrieved per question.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A pipeline, not a chat: ingest and classify each file, extract structured fields once into a claim record, retrieve the relevant policy clauses by product and coverage, have the model draft a recommendation that cites fields and clauses, run deterministic rules on hard limits, and route to an adjuster who approves. Token cost is controlled by extracting once and never re-sending raw PDFs, retrieving clauses rather than whole policies, routing easy documents to cheaper models, and using batch pricing for anything not time-critical.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Scope. I'd confirm the agent recommends and a person decides, at least at launch; that decisions must be explainable to a regulator; and the volume and turnaround target. I assume adjusters approve every decision and some low-value claim types may later be auto-approved under rules.</p>
<p>Ingestion. Files land in object storage with the claim ID; a classifier labels each (form, invoice, photo, letter); text-native PDFs are parsed directly, scans go through OCR or a vision model; every extracted field keeps a pointer to the page and region it came from.</p>
<p>The cost arithmetic. Claude bills a PDF page as 1,500 to 3,000 text tokens plus an image; a 40-page claim file is 60,000 to 120,000 text tokens, $0.12 to $0.24 at $2 per million on one pass. A review conversation that re-sends it on ten turns costs ten times that. Gemini counts 258 tokens a page, 10,320 for the same file, so the provider choice for the extraction step is itself a cost lever. After extraction, the claim record (fields plus citations) is a few thousand tokens, and that is what later turns carry.</p>
<p>Policy retrieval. Policy documents are chunked by clause with the product, version and section as metadata, plus a short contextual prefix per chunk. A claim retrieves only clauses for its product version and coverage type. If one product's whole policy is under about 200,000 tokens, it can sit in a cached prompt instead, which Anthropic suggests for small knowledge bases.</p>
<p>Decision. The model drafts: recommended outcome, reasons, each tied to a claim field or a clause ID. A rules engine then applies hard limits (coverage caps, exclusions, deductibles) that the model must not override. The adjuster sees the draft, the citations and the rule results, and approves or edits. Every decision stores the inputs, the prompt version, the model and the adjuster's action.</p>
<p>Infrastructure. Object storage for files, a relational store for claim records and decisions, a vector plus keyword index for policy clauses, a queue between stages, and batch processing for overnight backlogs at half price.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>How do you evaluate it?</em> Historical claims with known outcomes, scored on extraction accuracy per field, clause retrieval recall, and agreement with the final human decision, broken down by claim type.</li>
<li><em>When could a claim be auto-approved?</em> For a narrow, low-value type where the recommendation and the rules agree and historical agreement with adjusters is high, with random audits.</li>
<li><em>An invoice is fake.</em> Fraud signals belong to a separate model trained on outcomes, feeding the adjuster view; the drafting model should not be the fraud detector.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

## Follow-ups

- *A regulator asks why claim 1234 was denied.* Show the stored decision record: extracted fields with page references, the clauses retrieved with their policy version, the model's draft, the rule results and the adjuster's action.
- *Policy wording changes next quarter.* New policy versions are new documents; claims retrieve the version in force on the loss date, which is why the version is chunk metadata.
- *Cost per claim doubled.* Check whether raw PDFs crept back into the conversation context, and whether extraction is re-running on retries without a cache keyed by file hash.

## Common mistakes

- Letting the model make the final decision with no rules layer and no human.
- Re-sending raw documents every turn instead of a structured claim record.
- Retrieving from all policies instead of the product and version in force.
- No citations from decision to page and clause, which makes the system unauditable.
