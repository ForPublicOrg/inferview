## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Our candidate-ranking model is high-risk under Annex III from December 2027. Legal sent us Article 15(5) and asked engineering what it means for us. Walk me through which of its measures become engineering work on this system, which you would argue are out of scope, and what evidence you would put in the technical file for each."
</Callout>

The system (scenario):

- Recruiters at customer companies upload applications as PDFs. The service extracts text, retrieves the job description and the customer's rubric, and asks a model for a score from 1 to 10 and a rationale.
- The model is an open-weight base model downloaded from a public hub, with a LoRA adapter the team trains each quarter on shortlisting decisions exported from customers' applicant tracking systems.
- The scoring prompt changes about weekly; the adapter quarterly; the base model about once a year.
- Applicants write the PDFs. Customer recruiters choose which past decisions are exported for training.

For each attack class in 15(5), say whether it applies, the engineering task, and the evidence. Choose how often each test runs and defend the choice.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask who can write text that reaches the model at inference time, and who can write records that reach the adapter's training set.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask where the base weights and the tokenizer come from, and whether the build pins them by revision and hash.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask the conformity assessment body what it accepts as a test record: a CI run, or a dated report signed by a named person.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>All five classes apply here, because this system trains on data outsiders influence, downloads its base weights, takes attacker-written input and holds other people's personal data. Each becomes a control plus a test with a stated frequency: provenance and anomaly checks on the training export (data poisoning), pinned and verified weights (model poisoning), invisible-text handling and an adversarial suite on every prompt or adapter change (evasion), canary and cross-tenant extraction tests (confidentiality), and invariance tests for known flaws. The paragraph says prevent, detect, respond, resolve and control, so prevention alone is not enough: each class also needs a signal in the Article 12 logs and a runbook entry. The evidence is Annex IV 2(h) for the measures and 2(g) for dated, signed test reports, with test frequency written into the quality system under Article 17(1)(d).</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Why this is engineering work at all.</strong> Article 16(a) makes the provider responsible for Section 2, and Article 15 is in Section 2. "Where appropriate" and "appropriate to the relevant circumstances and the risks" make the scope a judgement, so the technical file has to say which classes were assessed and why each was in or out. An unexplained absence reads as an omission.</p>

<p><strong>The quantity: how often and how big.</strong> I tie frequency to change class rather than to the calendar. Prompt change, weekly: the adversarial and invariance suites, because the prompt is what evasion attacks target. Adapter change, quarterly: everything, including the poisoning and canary checks. Base model change: everything, plus a fresh bill of materials. For the adversarial suite's size: to claim an attack success rate under 5 percent at 95 percent confidence with zero successes, n must satisfy 0.95<sup>n</sup> ≤ 0.05, so n ≥ ln 0.05 / ln 0.95 = 58.4, which is 59 cases per attack family. Under 1 percent needs 299. I would take 59 per family on the weekly run and 299 on the quarterly one. Either is defensible; the file must say which and what it does and does not show.</p>
<p><strong>The constraint conflict.</strong> Annex IV 2(g) wants test reports dated and signed by responsible persons, and the prompt ships weekly. A human signature on every weekly run either stalls releases or turns into a rubber stamp. The resolution I would propose: the suite is approved and signed when it changes, each run is recorded automatically against the release, and a named owner signs the quarterly report covering every run in the quarter. Whether the notified body accepts that is a question to ask before December 2027, not after.</p>
<p><strong>What I would argue is narrow, not out of scope.</strong> Model poisoning of the base model is mostly the upstream publisher's risk, but the provider chose the component, so pinning and verifying it is still the provider's work. If the team stopped fine-tuning and used a hosted model, the data poisoning surface would shrink to the retrieval data, and the file would say that and why.</p>
<p><strong>Failure mode of this answer.</strong> A fixed adversarial suite measures the attacks someone thought of. Once applicants learn that invisible text is stripped, they move to visible text that reads like a qualification. A passing suite is evidence of a regression gate, not of resilience; the post-market monitoring plan (Annex IV item 9) has to watch score distributions per tenant for shifts the suite would not catch.</p>

<li><em>A customer insists that its recruiters' decisions are clean and refuses the per-tenant cap. What do you do?</em> Keep the cap or train a tenant-specific adapter on that tenant's data alone; the cap is what stops one tenant's labels from steering every other tenant's scores.</li>
<li><em>What would make you drop the canary extraction test?</em> No fine-tuning on personal data, so nothing personal is in the weights to extract.</li>

## Follow-ups

- *The notified body says your weekly runs do not count because nobody signed them. Which runs do you sign, and what does that cost in release cadence?*
- *An applicant's PDF scores 10 and the logs show it contained 600 words of invisible text. Which steps of "prevent, detect, respond, resolve and control" ran, and which did not?*
- *Legal asks whether the per-tenant cap means the model is less accurate for your largest customer. How would you measure it, and who decides if it is?*
- *What changes in the technical file if the base model's publisher ships a new revision with a security fix two weeks before a customer audit?*

## Common mistakes

- Treating Article 15(5) as a list of attacks to defend against, without the detect, respond and resolve parts or the evidence a reviewer reads.
- Declaring model poisoning the publisher's problem because the weights came from a hub.
- Running the adversarial suite only when the model changes, while the prompt, which the attacks target, changes weekly.
- Writing the tests and leaving their frequency unstated, when Article 17(1)(d) asks for it.
