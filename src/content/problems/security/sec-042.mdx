## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design, 40 minutes">
"Our fine-tuned model is the product. How would you detect someone stealing it through the API, and what would you do when you think you have?"
</Callout>

The system (scenario):

- A public API serving a model fine-tuned on the company's own labelled data, with a hidden size of 4,096. It returns the top 5 logprobs on request and accepts a `logit_bias` map.
- Two of the three largest customers use logprobs as confidence scores for classification, and one also uses `logit_bias` to force a choice among label tokens. Sales says removing either feature costs those renewals.
- Accounts are self-serve with a card; a startup programme gives free credits after a short form.
- Three engineers own the API, and the abuse budget for this half is one of them.

Design the detection, say which features you change, and say what happens to an account you flag.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what the attacker wants to leave with: weights or a model that behaves like yours. Request a list of which API parameters each of those needs.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request a week of traffic from the two customers who use logprobs, so you can see what heavy legitimate use of those features looks like.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask how many accounts the startup programme approved last month, and how many of them share a payment instrument or a sign-up network.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Two attacks, two detectors. Parameter extraction needs logprobs and logit bias in large volume with few distinct prompts and many distinct bias maps, so it is visible per request and per account; the cheapest strong control is to refuse <code>logit_bias</code> and logprobs on the same request, which the extraction paper says makes the attack about ten times more expensive and which neither key customer needs. Functional replication needs only ordinary outputs at volume, so no single request is suspicious and the detector works on patterns across accounts: capability concentration, template repetition, requests for step-by-step reasoning, shared payment and synchronised timing. A flag leads to review and throttling, not a silent ban, because the false positives are your best customers.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Separate the threats.</strong> OWASP lists model extraction and functional replication as different risks, and they leave different traces. Extraction recovers parameters: Carlini et al. recovered the embedding projection of OpenAI's ada and babbage for under $20 using top-K logprobs with logit bias. Replication trains a copy on your outputs: Anthropic's report describes more than 16 million exchanges through about 24,000 fraudulent accounts, aimed at a few capabilities.</p>
<p><strong>Extraction: change the API and watch the feature that remains.</strong> The paper's own defences are removing logit bias, replacing it with a block-list, adding noise, or forbidding logit bias and logprobs on the same request. The two customers do not need the combination: one reads logprobs on unbiased requests, the other forces label tokens and reads the chosen token. Forbid the combination, which breaks neither, and log every <code>logit_bias</code> request with the number of distinct biased tokens and a hash of the prompt. The paper's logprob-free attack still works through binary search over biases, so the per-account signal is many requests that share one prompt and differ only in the bias map, a shape no classification workload has, because a classifier biases the same handful of label tokens every time.</p>
<p>Choose a threshold from the cost of the attack. In Table 3 the query count for the dimension attack doubles from ada (hidden 1,024, under 2 million) to babbage (2,048, under 4 million). Extrapolating linearly, labelled as an extrapolation, a 4,096-wide model needs on the order of 8 million queries, and the combination ban multiplies that. A per-account cap of 10,000 biased requests a day, counting only bias maps outside the label tokens an account has declared, forces about 8,000,000 / 10,000 = 800 account-days for one extraction; the label-forcing customer declares its label tokens once and never meets the cap. Anything from 5,000 to 20,000 is defensible: much lower and developers experimenting with <code>logit_bias</code> hit it, much higher and the number of accounts one extraction needs falls toward what a patient attacker can register in a week. That is the point of the cap: it does not stop the attack, it converts it into many accounts, which is the pattern the second detector looks for.</p>
<p><strong>Replication: features across accounts, not rules on requests.</strong> Anthropic's report puts the distinguishing signal in the pattern, since a single prompt can look benign. Per account: share of traffic on one narrow task, number of distinct prompt templates against volume, share of prompts that ask for step-by-step reasoning, and how the output is consumed (full outputs at maximum length, never streamed to a user interface). Across accounts: shared payment instruments, sign-up details, network ranges and synchronised timing, and clusters of accounts whose template sets overlap. Data-centre origin, which the question's source offers as a signal, flags most legitimate API traffic.</p>
<p><strong>The decision with two defensible answers: what to do with a flag.</strong> Option A, ban on detection: it stops the account now, it tells the attacker which signal fired, and a false positive costs a paying customer and a public complaint. Option B, degrade quietly: throttle to a low rate, drop logprobs, and queue a human review with a decision in two working days; the attacker keeps a trickle and may notice the change, and a false positive gets slower service rather than a lockout. With one engineer and the largest customers inside the false-positive zone, I take B for paying accounts with history and A for accounts from the startup programme that match a cluster.</p>
<p><strong>The constraint conflict.</strong> Revenue against protection: the features that make extraction cheap are features two renewals depend on, and the accounts that look most like distillation (high volume, one task, templated prompts) are the evaluation and data-labelling customers you want most.</p>
<p><strong>Failure modes of this design.</strong> A patient attacker stays under every per-account threshold and mixes extraction traffic with ordinary requests, as the proxy networks in Anthropic's report did, so the cross-account clustering carries the weight, and it is the part one engineer will build last. Earlier detection defences for extraction were bypassed, as the paper notes, so treat detection as raising cost. And watermarking appears in OWASP's list without a named technique; it can support a claim after the fact, it does not detect anything during the attack.</p>
<p><strong>What goes in writing.</strong> A one-page decision memo: the combination ban with the two customers' sign-off, the thresholds and the cost arithmetic behind them, the flag-to-action table, the review owner and the two-day decision time. What you give up: logprobs on biased requests for every customer, and some slowed legitimate accounts each month.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why not add noise to logprobs instead of banning the combination?</em> Noise strong enough to hurt extraction also degrades the confidence scores the customer pays for; the ban costs neither customer anything.</li>
<li><em>A flagged account turns out to be an evaluation company benchmarking you for a prospect. What changes?</em> An allowlist with a named contact and a declared workload, reviewed quarterly, so its traffic stops training the false-positive rate upward.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: building cross-account clustering takes the abuse engineer the whole half. Which half of the design do you cut, and what do you accept in writing?* Ship the combination ban and the per-account bias-map signal, which take weeks, and write down that replication through many accounts is undetected until clustering ships.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see to remove `logit_bias` entirely, despite the renewal?* Evidence that bias-only extraction is running against you below the per-account cap across a cluster, at a cost to the business above the renewal.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants every flag to be a ban, the other wants humans to review each one. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Measure the share of last quarter's flags that review confirmed; the ban rule applies to the segments where that share is high, review to the rest.
- *A model with your behaviour appears on a public leaderboard. What evidence do you collect before saying anything?* Prompts where your model has distinctive, fine-tuned outputs, run against both, plus your logs of which accounts sent those prompts at volume.

## Common mistakes

- Treating extraction and replication as one problem with one detector.
- Removing logprobs outright and breaking the customers who use them, when banning the combination with logit bias does the work.
- Detecting on per-request rules when a single distillation prompt looks benign.
- Using data-centre origin as the signal for an API whose legitimate callers are mostly servers.
- Presenting a watermark as a detection control.
