## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"We are building an accounts-payable agent for small businesses. It reads the shared invoices inbox, works out what is owed, and pays suppliers through our banking partner. Design its permission model: what it can do on its own, what needs a person, and how authority flows from the business owner to the bank."
</Callout>

The constraints are a scenario given by the interviewer:

- The banking partner's API cannot change this year. It issues OAuth tokens with one scope, `payments:write`, with no amount or payee limits, valid for 60 minutes.
- Six engineers and ten weeks to a beta with twenty businesses.
- The pilot customer's last quarter, from their books: 1,200 invoices; 840 under £1,000, 300 from £1,000 to £10,000, and 60 above £10,000.
- The head of product wants recurring suppliers paid with no clicks. The risk team wants every payment confirmed by the account holder. Compliance requires that payments above an amount the bank sets are authorised by the account holder on a device the agent cannot drive.

Write the design, choose the numbers it needs, and end with a decision memo with a rollout plan and rollback triggers.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The agent never holds the bank's token. It reads email and proposes payment intents that name a supplier by its id in a verified supplier list, never by bank details from the email. A payments service we own holds the bank token, applies a deterministic policy, and either pays or asks the owner to approve the exact intent on their phone. Auto-pay is limited to verified suppliers with unchanged details, under a threshold, within daily and per-supplier caps; everything else, and every change of bank details, goes to a person through a channel the agent cannot write to. I give up zero-click for new suppliers and accept a few approvals a day per business.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Split the agent.</strong> The inbox is untrusted input, the books are private data, and paying is a state change: all three of the Rule of Two's properties. So no single session has all three. The reader reads email and the supplier list and emits structured intents; it has no payment tool. The payments service changes state and sees the books; it never reads email text, only the intent's fields.</p>
<p><strong>The intent.</strong> Supplier id, amount, currency, invoice number, due date, and a hash of the source email. No account numbers. If the invoice shows bank details that differ from the supplier record, the reader flags a payee change and the intent cannot be paid until that change is verified.</p>
<p><strong>Authority.</strong> The owner authorises our app once with the bank; the payments service stores that token and refreshes it. The agent runtime gets a token for the payments service only, scoped to <code>intents:create</code>, issued by token exchange with the owner as subject and the agent as actor, so the payments service's log separates what the owner did from what the agent proposed. The bank's coarse <code>payments:write</code> scope never leaves the payments service, which is where amount and payee limits live, because the bank cannot enforce them.</p>
<p><strong>Policy, outside the model.</strong> Auto-pay if all hold: the supplier has been verified with the same bank details for at least the waiting period, the amount is under the threshold, the invoice matches an open order or a recurring pattern for that supplier, it is not a duplicate of a paid invoice, and the day's auto-paid total and the supplier's total stay under their caps. Anything else returns an approval requirement, the scope-challenge pattern MCP uses for step-up.</p>
<p><strong>Approval.</strong> A push to the owner's phone app, rendered from the intent's fields (supplier legal name, last four digits of the account on file, amount, invoice number), never from text the model wrote. The approval signs the intent's hash, so the payment that runs is the one that was shown. Above the bank's threshold, the bank's own authentication runs on the owner's device.</p>
<p><strong>Payee changes.</strong> Never accepted from email. A change is confirmed by calling the supplier on a number already on file, by a person, and starts the waiting period again.</p>
</Fragment>

### The numbers you choose

**Auto-pay threshold.** With the pilot's quarter, a £1,000 threshold auto-pays 840 of 1,200 invoices, 70%, and sends 360 to approval. Over about 60 working days that is 360 ÷ 60 = 6 approvals a day. A £10,000 threshold sends only the 60 large invoices, one a day, but any single fraudulent payment can now be ten times larger. Hint 3 decides it: if the owner answers six a day without reading them, the threshold is too low to be safe in practice.

**Daily auto-pay cap.** This bounds the worst day, not the average one: if a compromised supplier mailbox feeds the agent plausible invoices, the most that leaves before the owner's evening review is the cap. Pick it from the pilot's daily auto-payable spend; a cap below the median day pushes ordinary days into approvals, a cap far above the worst day bounds nothing.

**Per-supplier cap and waiting period.** The per-supplier daily total stops an attacker splitting one large invoice into many under the threshold. The waiting period after a verified bank-detail change is the window in which the real supplier notices they have not been paid; choose it from the suppliers' payment terms, not from a convention.

Unreasonable choices: a threshold set from what the head of product wants the demo to show; caps per invoice only; a waiting period of zero for "trusted" suppliers, which are exactly the ones whose mailboxes attackers take over.

### Two defensible positions

- **Every payment approved by the owner** (the risk team's position). The agent drafts; the owner taps. Cost: about 20 approvals a working day at the pilot's volume (1,200 ÷ 60), the fatigue that makes approval meaningless, and a product that saves typing but not attention.
- **Auto-pay inside a policy, approval outside it** (the design above). Cost: a bounded loss per day when a real supplier's mailbox is compromised, engineering for verification and caps, and a policy engine that becomes the security boundary and needs its own tests.

The conflict is autonomy against loss: the head of product's zero-click is the risk team's nightmare. I take the second position for suppliers with a payment history and the first for everything else, and the beta starts with the first position for all suppliers.

### What you give up

New suppliers and changed bank details always cost a person's time. The agent cannot pay from the email alone, even when the email is genuine and urgent. The payments service, not the bank, is where limits are enforced, so a bug there is a loss we own.

### Decision memo

**Decision.** The agent proposes; a deterministic payments service decides. Auto-pay only for verified suppliers within threshold and caps; owner approval of the exact intent otherwise; bank-detail changes only through an out-of-band call.

**Rollout.** Weeks 1 to 4: reader, intents and the payments service with approval for every payment. Weeks 5 to 8: supplier verification and the payee-change flow. Weeks 9 to 10: beta with twenty businesses, approval-only. After two weeks of beta data, auto-pay for verified suppliers under the threshold, one business at a time.

**Rollback triggers.** Any auto-paid payment later disputed as fraud: auto-pay off for every business until the cause is found. Approval response times that cause late-payment fees: raise the threshold for that business with the owner's written agreement, or return to manual payment. Reader-flagged payee changes that turn out to be genuine, above the rate the owners will tolerate: review the reader, never the verification.

### Where this design fails

The verified supplier list is now the thing to attack. If a supplier's real mailbox is compromised, invoices arrive from the right sender with the right bank details and an inflated amount or a duplicate number, and every check above passes for invoices under the threshold until the caps are hit. The caps bound the loss per day; they do not prevent it. And the supplier list is maintained by people: an onboarding form, a support agent who updates details over the phone. An attacker who social-engineers that process gets a "verified" payee, and the waiting period is the only control left. The Rule of Two split has a limit too, and Meta's write-up says meeting the rule is not sufficient on its own: the amounts and invoice numbers in the intent still come from untrusted email, so the payments service's policy, not the split, is what limits them.

## Follow-ups

- *The owner approves 95% of requests within ten seconds. Is approval still a control, and what do you change?*
- *A supplier emails that their bank has changed and the next payment is urgent. Walk through what the owner sees and what the agent can do.*
- *Two senior engineers disagree on whether the reader may see the books at all. How do you decide without deciding by rank, and what does the reader lose without them?*

## Common mistakes

- Giving the agent the bank token and writing the limits into the system prompt.
- Letting the payee's bank details travel from the email into the payment.
- Showing the owner a model-written summary in the approval prompt instead of the fields that will be paid.
- Caps per invoice with no daily or per-supplier total, so splitting defeats them.
- Requiring approval for everything and calling the result safe, without asking whether anyone reads the approvals.
