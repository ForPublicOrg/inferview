## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design, 45 minutes">
"An attacker just got our support agent to do something it should never have done. You are the incident commander. Walk me through the first hour, and then the week."
</Callout>

The system and the alert (scenario):

- An online retailer's support agent. Tools: `lookup_order`, `issue_refund` (automatic up to €150, a human approves above that), `send_email` (any recipient), and `add_note`, which writes a note to the customer's memory that later sessions read. Retrieval covers the help centre and resolved tickets, which are re-indexed every night at 02:00.
- The agent closes about 60 percent of 9,000 tickets a day; 40 human agents handle the rest. Sessions run on a durable workflow engine and resume from checkpoints, and each session reads its tool list when it starts.
- 14:05. Finance flags 37 refunds in two hours on orders whose owners never opened a ticket. An analyst finds a ticket from yesterday whose attached PDF contains instructions to refund listed order numbers and to email order details to an outside address. Traces show 11 emails to that address.
- Most customers are in the EU. Content logs are kept for 7 days.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which of the agent's stores could still be carrying the attacker's text into sessions that start tomorrow.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask when a running session reads the tool list, and how many sessions are paused at a checkpoint right now.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Request a count of sessions since 02:00 yesterday whose traces record a retrieval of the poisoned ticket, and ask whether retrievals are logged with document ids at all.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>In the first hour: declare the incident and take command; put the agent in read-only mode by refusing every state-changing tool at the tool gateway, on every call, so resumed sessions are covered too; snapshot the index, the memory store and the traces and put the logs on hold before anyone purges anything; quarantine the poisoned ticket and the notes written by sessions that retrieved it; block the outside address; stop pending refunds at the payment provider; and start the 72-hour clock with the data protection officer. During the week: scope from traces, fix the three controls that failed (refund ownership, free recipients, customer text indexed as trusted), add deterministic regression tests, restore tools one at a time behind written rollback triggers, notify in phases, and review against measured detection and containment times.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Minutes 0 to 15: command and containment.</strong> The incident commander owns the decision to take the model or its tools offline. The containment is read-only mode: <code>issue_refund</code>, <code>send_email</code> and <code>add_note</code> return a refusal from the tool gateway, checked on each call. A kill switch in the tool list a session reads at start does nothing for sessions resuming from a checkpoint, and those are exactly the ones that may be mid-attack. Lookups and answers keep running, so the 60 percent of volume degrades to "answered, action queued for a human" rather than to nothing. Block the outside address at the mail gateway and ask the payment provider to hold refunds not yet settled.</p>
<p><strong>Minutes 15 to 40: evidence before eradication.</strong> CoSAI's order is forensic copies first, then containment that does not destroy them. Snapshot the vector index and the memory store, export the traces and raw model outputs (the same input will not reproduce them), and extend the 7-day content retention to a legal hold for everything since the poisoned ticket arrived. Then quarantine, not delete: drop the ticket from retrieval and from tonight's 02:00 re-index, and flag every note written by a session that retrieved it. Deleting them now would remove the only record of what the attacker wrote into memory.</p>
<p><strong>Minutes 40 to 60: credentials, clock, comms.</strong> Rotate what the model could have seen or a tool could have returned. If the orchestrator holds the payment and mail tokens outside the context window, the injection could use them but not read them, and read-only mode already removes the use; rotating them now breaks the human queue for no gain. The 11 emails carried order details to an unknown party, which is a personal data breach, so the data protection officer hears now: Article 33 gives 72 hours from awareness, and the first notice may be partial. Support leads get a status note; customers wait for the scope.</p>
<p><strong>The decision with two defensible answers.</strong> Option A, stop the agent entirely: containment is certain, and 5,400 tickets a day land on 40 people who already have 3,600. Option B, read-only mode: most volume keeps flowing, and you bet that the state-changing tools are the only sinks. That bet has a known hole: answers still reach customers, so poisoned text can put a phishing link in a reply. I take B with one more cut: the renderer shows links as plain text until the scope is known. If the traces show the attacker using any read-only path to get data out, I switch to A.</p>
<p><strong>The week.</strong> Day 1 to 2, scope. The scope query is every session that retrieved the poisoned ticket, every <code>send_email</code> to an address that is not the ticket owner's, every refund to an order its requester does not own, and every session that read a flagged note. The window is your choice: from the 02:00 re-index that made the ticket retrievable is the narrowest defensible start; if retrievals are not logged with document ids, the window becomes every session since 02:00 and the notification grows with it. Day 2 to 3, root cause: three failures, not one. Refunds had no ownership check in code. Recipients were free. And customer-supplied text from resolved tickets was indexed into the same trust level as the help centre, which turned a one-off injection into a stored one. Day 3 to 5, eradication: refund only to orders owned by the authenticated requester, email only to the ticket's verified address, resolved tickets indexed without attachments and tagged untrusted, the index rebuilt from the help centre plus a cleaned ticket set. Each fix gets a deterministic test with a scripted model emitting the forbidden call. Day 5 to 7, recovery: re-enable <code>send_email</code> first, since its fix holds by construction, then refunds, each behind a rollback trigger written before it goes live (any refund to a non-owned order, any email to a non-owner address, reverts to read-only without a meeting). The follow-up notices to the authority and affected customers go out as the scope firms up.</p>
<p><strong>The constraint conflict.</strong> Speed against evidence, and the disclosure clock against an incomplete scope: every minute spent snapshotting is a minute the attack can continue, and every hour spent narrowing the scope is an hour off the 72.</p>
<p><strong>Failure modes of this plan.</strong> Read-only mode covers the tools you know are state-changing; a lookup tool with a side effect, such as one that sends a verification code, is a sink nobody listed. Quarantining the ticket does not stop the next attacker from filing a new one before the ingestion fix ships, so the attachment exclusion goes in on day 1 as a config change, ahead of the rebuild. And an AI tool used to triage thousands of transcripts reads the attacker's text too, so it runs without tools and its conclusions are checked against the raw traces.</p>
<p><strong>What goes in writing.</strong> A timeline from first poisoned input to containment, the scope query and its window, the decision log with times and names, the rollback triggers, and the review's measures: time from first bad refund to the finance alert, time from alert to read-only, sessions affected, euros refunded and recovered, records sent out. What you give up: a day of refunds and outbound mail handled by people, and links in replies for as long as the scope is open.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Finance caught this, two hours in. What would have caught it in ten minutes?</em> An alert on the ratio of refunds whose order owner differs from the requester, which should be zero by design and is cheap to compute per call.</li>
<li><em>Why not purge the customer memory notes immediately?</em> They are the record of what the attacker made the agent persist, and the scope of who read them depends on keeping them until the snapshot is verified.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: support says read-only mode costs them a full day of overtime and asks you to restore refunds tonight with the ownership check deployed as a hotfix. What do you accept, and in writing?* Accept refunds with the hotfix only after its deterministic test passes in CI and with the rollback trigger live, and write down that the stored-injection path stays open until the re-index fix ships.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in the traces to move from read-only to a full stop?* Any data leaving through a path read-only mode does not close, such as a reply that carries another customer's order details, or a tool call outside the four known tools.
- *Legal wants to wait for the full scope before notifying the authority, because a partial notice looks worse. Two senior people disagree; how do you decide without deciding by rank?* Article 33(4) allows phased information and 33(1) requires reasons for a late notice, so put the two notices side by side: a partial one now, and a full one at hour 90 with a written reason.
- *The vendor's model is retired in sixty days and the replacement follows injected instructions more readily on your adaptive suite. Does the incident change the migration?* The fixes from the week hold by construction and do not move with the model; the migration is gated on the adaptive suite for the remaining rate controls.

## Common mistakes

- Purging memory and deleting the poisoned document in the first minutes, which destroys the scope.
- Implementing the kill switch in the tool list a session loads at start, so resumed sessions keep their old tools.
- Rotating every credential by reflex and breaking the human workflow, while the stored injection stays in the index.
- Treating the poisoned ticket as a one-off and missing that the nightly re-index made it a stored injection.
- Waiting for the full scope before starting the 72-hour clock.
