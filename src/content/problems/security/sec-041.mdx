## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 30 minutes">
"Set per-tenant rate limits and spend caps for our API from the traffic we see. Then tell me what one stolen tenant key costs us in a day, and which abuse your limits still miss."
</Callout>

Published inputs (vendor documentation, checked 2026-10-28): the API runs on Claude Sonnet 5.5 at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. The organisation is on the Scale tier: 10,000,000 input tokens and 2,000,000 output tokens per minute for that model, and a $200,000 monthly spend cap, after which every request is refused until the first of the next month.

Traffic model (scenario):

- A contract-review API with 150 paying tenants and a self-serve free trial.
- A median tenant sends 3,000 requests a day, all inside a 10-hour working window. Each request carries 4,000 uncached input tokens and returns 600 output tokens. The five largest tenants send ten times the median; the other 145 behave like the median.
- Traffic runs about 22 working days a month.

Estimate:

1. A per-tenant token bucket for input and output tokens per minute, for the median tenant. You choose the headroom over the mean.
2. With those buckets and no spend cap, the most one stolen median-tenant key can spend in 24 hours. Give this number in USD per day; it is the graded answer.
3. A daily spend cap per tenant, and whether the buckets fit under the organisation's limits.
4. The abuse pattern that your limits do not see.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The median tenant averages 5 requests a minute in its working window: 20,000 input and 3,000 output tokens per minute. With buckets at four times that, 80,000 and 12,000, a stolen key that runs flat out for 24 hours spends 1,440 × (80,000 × $2 + 12,000 × $10) per million = $403 a day, against a normal day of $42. The dominant term is the attacker running all day at the bucket ceiling instead of 10 hours at the mean, which is what turns a 4× bucket into a 9.6× bill. Buckets bound the rate, not the bill, so a daily cap in dollars of about $100 does the wallet work. None of this sees an attacker who spreads load across many tenants, each under its limits.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>1. The bucket.</strong> 3,000 requests over 600 minutes is 5 per minute, so 5 × 4,000 = 20,000 input tokens and 5 × 600 = 3,000 output tokens per minute on average. The headroom is yours to choose: 2 to 6 times the mean is reasonable for a business API with month-end peaks. Below about 1.5 the bucket throttles legitimate peaks and support tickets start; above about 10 it no longer constrains anyone. Take 4: refill at 80,000 input and 12,000 output tokens per minute, with a capacity of a few minutes of refill so a batch of large contracts can burst. Limit tokens, not requests: a request limit lets the caller pick the size of each request, and OWASP lists exactly that variable-length flood.</p>
<p><strong>2. One stolen key, buckets only.</strong> Cost per minute at the ceiling: 80,000 × $2 / 10^6 = $0.16 for input and 12,000 × $10 / 10^6 = $0.12 for output, $0.28 a minute. Over 1,440 minutes that is $403 a day, against a normal day of 3,000 × (4,000 × $2 + 600 × $10) / 10^6 = $42. With headroom 2 the figure is $202; with 6 it is $605; the accepted band, 190 to 650, covers that range and the one-time burst capacity. Unreasonable answers: a figure near $42, which assumes the attacker keeps office hours and average sizes; or one computed from a request-only limit, which has no ceiling in tokens.</p>
<p>The dominant term is time at the ceiling: 24 hours against 10, times the headroom, gives 2.4 × 4 = 9.6 times a normal day. Input is 57 percent of a normal request's cost ($0.008 of $0.014), but an attacker fills both buckets, so at the ceiling the split is set by the buckets, not by the traffic.</p>
<p><strong>3. Spend cap and the organisation's limits.</strong> A daily cap per tenant in dollars, 2 to 3 times the normal day, $84 to $126, bounds a stolen key regardless of request shape. The cost is a hard stop for a legitimate tenant on its busiest day, which is the decision with two defensible answers. A hard cap that refuses requests protects the wallet and causes an outage for that tenant; a soft cap that alerts and throttles to the mean keeps the tenant up and leaves the wallet exposed until someone reads the alert. I take the soft cap for paying tenants with a hard cap at 5 times the normal day, and a hard cap for free trials.</p>
<p>Now the organisation. Average load: 145 × 20,000 + 5 × 200,000 = 3.9 million input tokens per minute and 145 × 3,000 + 5 × 30,000 = 585,000 output tokens per minute, 39 and 29 percent of the tier. The buckets at 4× add up to 15.6 million and 2.34 million, 1.56 and 1.17 times the tier, which is fine while peaks do not coincide. The monthly figure is tighter than the per-minute one: 145 × $42 + 5 × $420 = $8,190 a day, about $180,000 over 22 days, against the $200,000 cap. That leaves about $19,800, or 49 days of one stolen key at the bucket ceiling, before every tenant is refused until the first of the month. Denial of wallet against one tenant becomes denial of service for all of them, so the organisation-level cap needs its own alert well before the vendor's.</p>
<p><strong>4. What the limits miss.</strong> Every limit here is per tenant, and the assumption that flips the conclusion is that one actor equals one tenant. With a self-serve trial, an attacker opens many accounts and keeps each one under its bucket and cap. Anthropic's distillation report describes about 24,000 fraudulent accounts and proxy networks that mixed extraction traffic with ordinary requests, and its espionage report describes tasks split until each looked innocent. Per-tenant limits never see either; what sees them is cross-account analysis (shared payment instruments, synchronised timing, near-identical prompt templates), a global budget for all trials together, and verification before the trial reaches paid limits.</p>
<p><strong>Failure modes of the recommended limits.</strong> A daily cap resets at midnight, so an undetected stolen key spends the cap every day until someone notices; the alert on a tenant at its cap two days running matters more than the cap. And tenant-level buckets say nothing about a tenant's own end users: one abusive user inside a legitimate tenant spends that tenant's budget and locks out its other users.</p>
</Fragment>

<li><em>Why charge cached input differently in your buckets?</em> The vendor does not count most cache reads toward its input limit, so a tenant whose prompts cache well uses less of the shared limit; counting raw input overstates its load.</li>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: sales says any hard cap on paying tenants will lose a renewal. Which half of the protection do you give up, and what do you accept in writing?* Drop the hard cap for the five largest tenants, keep the soft throttle and an on-call alert, and write down the maximum daily exposure per tenant that finance accepts.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in a quarter of production data to remove per-tenant buckets and rely on spend caps alone?* No legitimate tenant ever reaching a bucket ceiling, and no incident where a burst from one tenant raised latency for the others.
- *Free-trial signups jump to 400 a day, all with similar prompts, each well under its limits. Two senior engineers disagree: one wants card verification on trials, the other says it kills conversion. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Measure the share of trial traffic that matches the coordinated pattern and its cost per day, and set that next to the conversion loss from verification at the same volume.

## Common mistakes

- Limiting requests per minute and leaving the size of each request to the caller.
- Pricing a stolen key at office hours and average request sizes.
- Treating a per-minute bucket as a spend control; it bounds rate, and the bill is rate times hours.
- Ignoring the provider's organisation-wide spend cap, which turns one tenant's abuse into an outage for all.
- Assuming one attacker equals one account.
