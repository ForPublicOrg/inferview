## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Our model weights represent years of research. Design the access control system that protects them. Tell me who can touch the bytes, how, and what you would notice if someone tried to take them."
</Callout>

The operating constraints are a scenario given by the interviewer:

- About 400 people; 120 researchers today copy checkpoints between a shared filesystem, their own GPU nodes and, sometimes, laptops.
- The current frontier model has 400 billion parameters. Weights are trained in a cluster the company runs and served from a fleet in a public cloud.
- A security team of six and two quarters. The head of research will oppose anything that slows experiment turnaround, and has the CEO's ear.
- The board wants a written threat model: which attackers the design is meant to stop, and which it is not.

Write the design, choose the numbers it needs, and end with a decision memo that has a rollout plan and rollback triggers.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask for an inventory of every place a full checkpoint exists today, including caches, scratch directories and backups, and who can read each.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask the head of research which tasks genuinely need the raw bytes, as opposed to running code against them.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Request the outbound traffic per host from the training cluster and the serving fleet over a normal week, before setting any limit.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>No person holds standing access to weight bytes. Weights are encrypted at rest with per-model keys, and the key service releases a key only to an attested workload (a training job, a serving host, an evaluation job) in an environment with metered egress. Researchers bring code to the weights instead of weights to their code, through job submission and notebooks inside that environment, with an export gate for results. Human access to raw bytes is just-in-time, approved by two people, time-boxed and recorded. Egress from every environment that holds plaintext weights is capped and budgeted per identity, so copying a model takes longer than our detection window. I give up local copies and some research speed, and I say in writing that a determined insider with enclave access, or a state-level attacker, is outside what this stops.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Threat model first.</strong> In scope: an external attacker with a foothold on a researcher's laptop or a CI system, a careless or departing employee, and a single malicious insider without enclave privileges. Out of scope, stated plainly: a state-level attacker and colluding privileged insiders. Anthropic's own ASL-3 security standard draws a similar line, excluding sophisticated and state-compromised insiders from its scope in version 2.2.</p>
<p><strong>Where plaintext exists.</strong> Three places only: the training enclave (cluster nodes and checkpoint storage), the serving enclave (GPU host memory), and the evaluation enclave. Everywhere else, including backups and the transfer from training to serving, holds ciphertext. Each checkpoint has its own data key, wrapped by a key-management key whose release policy names the workload identity, the enclave and an attestation of the host image.</p>
<p><strong>People.</strong> Tier 1 researchers submit jobs and use notebooks that run inside the training or evaluation enclave; the notebook's kernel can load weights, the researcher's browser cannot download them. Tier 2 engineers who debug numerics or serving get just-in-time access to a host in the enclave: a request names the checkpoint and the reason, a second person approves (the two-party authorization Anthropic describes for its own weight access), the session is recorded and expires. Nobody has Tier 2 by default, including the security team.</p>
<p><strong>Export gate.</strong> Anything leaving an enclave goes through one service: metrics and logs pass; files above a size limit wait for review; weight-shaped tensors (by size and shape) are refused unless a Tier 2 approval names them. This is where most of the research friction lives, so it gets the most engineering.</p>
<p><strong>Hosts.</strong> Serving hosts run an allowlisted image with no interactive shell; training nodes allow shells only through the just-in-time path. Change management on both, so a new binary on a host holding plaintext is a reviewed change.</p>
<p><strong>Detection.</strong> Egress caps per enclave, byte budgets per identity per day, alerts on key releases outside a job, and an insider program owned with HR and legal, because the controls above mostly stop people from taking weights by accident or in a hurry.</p>

### The numbers you choose

**Checkpoint size.** At 2 bytes per parameter in bf16, 400 × 10<sup>9</sup> parameters is 800 GB of serving weights. A training checkpoint with Adam state is larger: a 4-byte master copy and two 4-byte moments add 12 bytes per parameter, so 14 bytes per parameter is 5.6 TB. The serving copy is the one worth stealing, and the smallest one to steal.

**Egress cap.** The cap is set from the detection window, not from bandwidth you can spare. If the team reviews alerts within 24 hours, a cap below size ÷ window means a full copy cannot leave before someone looks: 800 × 10<sup>9</sup> B ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 9.3 MB/s for the bf16 weights. An attacker who quantizes to 4 bits first moves 200 GB, which brings the cap down to about 2.3 MB/s. The week of traffic from Hint 3 tells you whether serving and training can live under that; if serving responses alone need more, the cap goes on the paths that are not the API front end.

**Per-identity budget.** A rate cap alone loses to patience: at 1 MB/s, 800 GB leaves in 800,000 s, about 9.3 days, under any reasonable rate alarm. A daily byte budget per identity, well below one quantized copy and summed across days, catches the slow version. The number comes from the same week of traffic: the largest legitimate daily export per person, with headroom you can defend.

**Approvers.** Two for Tier 2, from different teams, so one team's deadline cannot approve itself. More than two adds delay without stopping collusion, which is out of scope anyway.

Unreasonable choices: a cap sized to the network's capacity; an approval rule where the requester's manager alone approves; a budget per host instead of per identity, which a researcher with three nodes triples.

### Two defensible designs

| | Code to data: no human touches raw bytes | Just-in-time human access as the main path |
|---|---|---|
| Research speed | Slower for debugging and ad hoc analysis; fast once tooling exists | Close to today's workflow, with an approval step |
| What stops a copy | Key release only to attested workloads, the export gate | Approvals, session recording, egress caps |
| Engineering cost | High: notebooks, job tooling and the export gate inside enclaves | Lower: an approval service and recorded sessions |
| Main failure | Researchers route around it with bulk "result" exports | Approvals become routine; a second approver clicks without reading |

I would make code-to-data the default and just-in-time access the exception, because the exception's failure (approval fatigue) is bounded by how rarely it is used. The constraint conflict is security against experiment turnaround, with a CEO who will hear about every slow week.

### What you give up

Local and laptop copies end. Some debugging that took an afternoon now needs an approval and a recorded session. External fine-tuning partners cannot receive weights; they get an API or a job slot in the enclave. Two quarters buys the encryption, key release, egress caps and the first version of the export gate, not a polished research environment, so turnaround gets worse before it gets better.

### Decision memo

**Decision.** No standing human access to plaintext weights. Plaintext exists only in three enclaves, behind workload-bound key release and metered egress. Tier 2 access is just-in-time with two approvers.

**Rollout.**

- Weeks 1 to 4: inventory every copy (Hint 1), delete or encrypt the strays, and turn on egress measurement without blocking.
- Weeks 5 to 12: per-checkpoint keys and workload-bound key release in training and serving; Tier 2 approvals live; laptop copies blocked.
- Weeks 13 to 20: egress caps and identity budgets enforced; notebooks and the export gate inside the training enclave; standing access removed.
- Weeks 21 to 26: the same for evaluation; first red-team exercise against the export gate.

**Rollback triggers,** agreed with the head of research before week 1:

- Median experiment turnaround worse than the baseline by more than the margin the head of research signs, for two weeks running: pause the next phase, not the controls already shipped.
- Tier 2 requests per week above an agreed number, which means the enclave tooling is missing something people need.
- A legitimate job blocked by the egress cap in production serving: raise the cap on that path with a written exception, and review it monthly.

### Where this design fails

It moves risk from people to hosts. A serving host holds plaintext weights in GPU memory by necessity, so an attacker with root on one serving host has the model, and nothing in key release helps after that. The allowlisted image, the missing shell and change management are what protect that host, and an attacker who compromises the image build pipeline gets past all three. Egress caps assume the attacker uses the network you meter; a person with physical access to a training node, or a backup system that copies ciphertext together with a key it should not hold, does not. And the design is only as good as the inventory in week 1: one forgotten scratch directory with plaintext checkpoints undoes the rest.

## Follow-ups

- *Your plan spends both quarters on enclaves and the export gate. Security leadership wants insider-threat analytics instead. Which do you cut, and what do you accept in writing?*
- *What evidence would make you reverse the "no laptop copies" rule for small models, and where is the size line?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree on whether evaluation jobs belong inside the enclave. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *A researcher's account exports 40 GB in a day through the export gate, all approved by one reviewer. What do you look at first, and what changes in the gate?*

## Common mistakes

- Starting with roles and permissions without stating which attackers are in and out of scope.
- Treating encryption at rest as the answer, while every serving host decrypts the weights into memory.
- Setting an egress limit from network capacity instead of from the detection window.
- Rate limits without cumulative budgets, which a patient insider waits out.
- Designing only for training and forgetting the serving fleet, which holds the most copies.
