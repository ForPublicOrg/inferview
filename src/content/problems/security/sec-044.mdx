## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design, 35 minutes">
"A researcher accidentally pushes a live API key to a public repository. Walk us through your incident response."
</Callout>

The situation (scenario):

- Friday, 18:40. A researcher pushed a notebook to a public repository in the company's GitHub organisation at 17:55 and noticed at 18:35. Secret scanning is not enabled on that repository.
- The notebook holds two credentials. One is a Claude API key from the company's organisation; it sits in a workspace shared by research batch jobs and a production summarisation feature. The other is a token for the company's internal evaluation-data API, issued by the company's own identity provider and valid for 30 days; it can read an evaluation set built from customer support transcripts.
- At 18:12, the production summarisation feature started returning errors. On-call is investigating it as an outage.
- A model launch evaluation runs over the weekend and reads the evaluation-data API with the same token. The research lead asks you to leave that token alone until Monday.

Take it from the first message to the review a week later.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask on-call what error the summarisation feature returns, and at what minute it began.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>For each credential, ask who issued it and whether anyone outside the company scans public repositories for its format.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Request the evaluation-data API's access log for the token since 17:55, with source addresses, and the list of jobs that use the token.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Treat the push as publication and handle each credential by its issuer. The Claude key is in GitHub's partner programme, so it was most likely deactivated automatically, and the 18:12 outage is that revocation: confirm it, issue new keys per workload, and pull the key's usage from 17:55. The internal token has no outside scanner, so it is live: revoke it now, accept that the weekend evaluation stops, and reissue a scoped token to that job. Then read the access logs for both, decide whether customer data left, start the 72-hour clock if it did, make the repository clean without relying on a history rewrite, and fix the conditions that put a long-lived key in a notebook.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>18:40 to 19:00: stop the exposure, credential by credential.</strong> The Claude key: GitHub forwards Anthropic keys found in public repositories to Anthropic, which deactivates them and emails the account holder. An outage beginning 17 minutes after the push, on the feature that shares the key, is most likely that deactivation; check the account email and the error before assuming anything else. Then delete the key in the Console if it is not already gone, and issue two new keys in two workspaces, one for production and one for research, so the next revocation takes down one of them. The outage ends when production gets its own key. The internal token: no partner programme covers your own issuer, so it is live. Revoke it at the identity provider now.</p>
<p><strong>The decision with two defensible answers: the weekend evaluation.</strong> Option A, revoke now: the exposure ends at 18:45, and the evaluation stops until someone reissues a token to the job, which costs the research lead a weekend and maybe the launch date. Option B, rotate first: issue a new token, redeploy the evaluation job with it, then revoke the old one; the evaluation keeps running and the token stays live and public for the redeploy, perhaps an hour, perhaps the weekend if the job cannot be restarted cleanly. The token reads customer transcripts and is valid for 30 days, and anyone who cloned the repository since 17:55 has it, so I take A and offer the research lead a scoped token for the evaluation job within the hour. If the token could reach only synthetic data, B would be the reasonable call.</p>
<p><strong>19:00 to 20:00: what happened while it was public.</strong> For the Claude key, the Console's Usage and Logs pages, filtered to 17:55 onward: requests outside the batch jobs' and the feature's normal pattern are the attacker's, and the cost is the bill. For the internal token, your own API's access log from 17:55 to the revocation: every request whose source is not the evaluation job's runner is an outsider, and the records it returned are the scope. The window is your choice and it has a floor: from the push, not from the moment someone noticed. If any transcript was read from an unknown source, it is a personal data breach; the data protection officer hears tonight, and Article 33 gives 72 hours from awareness, with details allowed in phases.</p>
<p><strong>20:00 onward: the repository.</strong> GitHub's guidance is to revoke first, after which a history rewrite may not be worth it: forks and clones keep the data, and cached views and pull-request refs keep the commit reachable by SHA until GitHub Support removes them. Make the repository private or remove the notebook in a new commit, ask Support to purge cached views if the notebook also held anything that cannot be rotated (customer data in cell outputs, for example), and do not let a rewrite stand in for revocation.</p>
<p><strong>The week.</strong> Root cause is the conditions, not the researcher: a long-lived token with customer-data scope, usable from a laptop; a key shared by research and production; secret scanning off on a public repository. Fixes: turn on secret scanning and push protection for every public repository in the organisation, which since 13 October 2026 blocks both Anthropic key types by default; register your own token format with a pre-receive or pre-commit scanner, since no outside programme will catch it; issue evaluation tokens per job, short-lived, through workload identity; separate workspaces with their own spend limits per workload. The review measures: time from push to revocation for each credential, minutes of production outage, records read from unknown sources, and how many other notebooks in the organisation's repositories hold secrets when scanned.</p>
<p><strong>The constraint conflict.</strong> A launch deadline against exposure of customer data: the researcher's evaluation and the transcripts' owners want opposite things on a Friday night.</p>
<p><strong>Failure modes of this response.</strong> Revoking the internal token without a list of its consumers can break more than the evaluation; if another production job used it, revocation becomes a second outage, which is why the consumer list is requested before the revocation, not after. The Claude key's deactivation is a provider's action whose timing nobody has published, so "it was probably revoked" is checked in the Console, not assumed. And push protection blocks only the formats it knows; your own token format passes until you register it.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why did production go down when a research notebook leaked?</em> The production feature shared the research key's workspace, so the provider's automatic revocation of one key took out both uses.</li>
<li><em>The access log shows three requests from an unknown address at 18:20, each returning one page of transcripts. Is it a breach?</em> Yes; customer personal data reached an unknown party, so the 72-hour clock runs from when you learned it, and the scope is the pages returned.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the research lead escalates and says the launch slips a week if the evaluation stops tonight. What do you accept, and in writing?* A scoped replacement token for the evaluation job tonight, and a written note that the launch risk is the cost of a 30-day customer-data token living in a notebook.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see to rotate first and revoke later next time?* A token whose scope holds no personal or confidential data, and a consumer that can be redeployed in minutes; then the exposure window costs less than the outage.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants every notebook repository forced private, the other says public notebooks are how research collaborates. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Count secrets found in the organisation's public notebooks in one scan, and the collaboration that depends on public notebooks; push protection and scoped tokens may make the private rule unnecessary.

## Common mistakes

- Debugging the 18:12 outage as unrelated, when it is the provider's revocation of the leaked key.
- Rewriting git history first and treating it as the fix, while the key stays valid and clones keep it.
- Leaving a live credential public over a weekend to protect a schedule without writing down who accepted that.
- Scoping the investigation from when the leak was noticed instead of from the push.
- Blaming the researcher and leaving the long-lived, broadly scoped token in place.
