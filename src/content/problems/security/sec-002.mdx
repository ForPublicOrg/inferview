## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 40 minutes">
"We're putting an assistant in the company chat that answers questions from three internal systems: the wiki, the ticket tracker and the CRM. Design it so that it does not become a hole."
</Callout>

What the interviewer tells you when asked (scenario):

- 1,800 employees. The wiki has 120,000 pages with page-level permissions; a typical engineer can read about 30 percent of them, a contractor about 2 percent. The tracker has per-project permissions, and its service-desk project holds tickets that customers write. The CRM holds customer contacts and contract values with per-account visibility.
- The wiki and the tracker accept a token issued for the asking user. The CRM team offers only a service account with read access to every account, and will not change their API this year.
- Legal's condition: no CRM field reaches an employee who cannot see that account in the CRM.
- The chat client shows previews for links in messages.
- Launch in six weeks with three engineers. The product sponsor counts the CRM as the reason to build the assistant.

Design the retrieval path, the identity model, the output path and the logs. Name what you give up, and write the risk note that goes to the security review.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask who can write to each source, including customers and contractors, and whether that text reaches the model.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask where the chat client fetches its link previews from, and what it sends in the request.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask who can read the assistant's traces today, and how long they are kept.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Make every answer one the user could have assembled by hand. Retrieve with permission metadata in the index, then fetch the final few documents with the user's own token, so what reaches the model is checked by the source system. Treat the wiki and tickets as untrusted (any employee, contractor or customer writes them) and keep the assistant read-only, so the session holds untrusted input and private data but no outbound channel: link previews off for the bot, and the only URLs it may print are those of the documents it retrieved. Leave the CRM out of v1, because a service account plus our own copy of its visibility rules makes us the CRM's authorization system in six weeks. Traces hold document ids and hashes; content goes to a restricted store with short retention.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Retrieval and identity.</strong> Two ways to keep answers inside the user's permissions.</p>
<p>Federated search: at question time call each system's search API with the user's token and send the hits to the model. Permission holds by construction, because the source decides. The cost is quality and latency: keyword search inside each tool, three API calls per question, no ranking across systems.</p>
<p>A central index with permission filtering: embed everything, store each chunk's access list, filter at query time. The quantity that decides how to filter is the share of the corpus a user can read. Post-filtering a top 40 leaves on average 40 × 0.30 = 12 readable chunks for an engineer and 40 × 0.02 = 0.8 for a contractor, so for contractors the answer is usually built from nothing. Pre-filtering inside the index fixes recall but trusts our synced copy of the access lists, which is stale by the sync interval. My choice: pre-filter in the index, then fetch the final 8 documents from the source with the user's token and drop any that return 403. The index's staleness then costs recall, not leaks, and the sync interval becomes a freshness choice: anything from 15 minutes to a day is defensible because the final fetch is authoritative. The price is 8 calls per question, issued in parallel.</p>
<p><strong>The CRM.</strong> The decision with two defensible answers. Include it through the service account, mirror the per-account visibility into our index, and check it ourselves: the sponsor gets the feature, and we own a copy of an authorization model we do not control, where one sync bug shows contract values to the wrong people, which is Legal's one condition. Or leave it out of v1: no copy of anyone's authorization, at the cost of the feature the sponsor wanted and a launch that may not justify itself. I leave it out, write down that the CRM comes in when it offers a per-user token or a per-user visibility query, and offer a narrow interim: the assistant links to the CRM record by id, and the CRM itself enforces who can open it.</p>
<p><strong>Untrusted text.</strong> Wiki pages are edited by anyone with access, including contractors; service-desk tickets are written by customers. Spotlighting or delimiters around retrieved text and an injection classifier lower the rate at which instructions are followed. They are not boundaries: Nasr et al. report adaptive attacks above 90 percent against three of the detectors they tested. So the design assumes an injected instruction will sometimes be followed and makes sure following it cannot move data.</p>
<p><strong>The channels out.</strong> The assistant has no write tools. That leaves the output: if the client previews links, a URL the model prints is a request to the attacker's host with data in the path, made without a click. EchoLeak is the warning about allowlists: data left Copilot through Teams and SharePoint URLs, hosts any internal allowlist would contain. The control that holds by construction: a post-processor removes every URL that is not the canonical URL of a document retrieved in this turn, and the bot's messages are sent with previews off. Markdown images are not rendered.</p>
<p><strong>The logs.</strong> Traces with retrieved content collect text from every permission boundary into one store that the platform team can read, which is a leak that needs no attacker. Traces keep document ids, content hashes and the user id; full content goes to a separate store with a 14-day retention and access by incident ticket only. 14 days is my choice; anything long enough to investigate a report is defensible.</p>
<p><strong>The constraint conflict.</strong> Coverage against correctness of access, under a deadline: the source the sponsor values most is the one whose access model we cannot check in six weeks.</p>
<p><strong>Failure mode of this design.</strong> It stops data from leaving; it does not stop the answer from lying. A contractor edits a wiki page to say the VPN moved to a lookalike host, and the assistant repeats it, with a citation that makes it look checked. Mitigations lower the rate (showing page author and last edit date, ranking pages by edit history), and the risk note says so. A second failure: the final fetch with the user's token depends on each source's API being up, and failing closed means the assistant answers from nothing during an outage.</p>
<p><strong>What you give up.</strong> The CRM in v1, ranking quality for contractors, link previews, images, and full-content traces for debugging.</p>
<p><strong>The risk note.</strong> Scope: wiki and tracker, read-only. Controls by construction: final documents fetched with the user's token; no write tools; URLs limited to retrieved documents; previews and images off; content logs restricted. Controls by rate: delimiters and the classifier. Accepted risks: injected misinformation in answers (owner: product), answers empty during a source outage (owner: platform). Deferred: the CRM, until it offers per-user access. Rollback triggers: any answer containing a document the asker cannot open; any outbound request from a bot message to a host not in a retrieved document.</p>
</Fragment>

<li><em>What test proves the final-fetch check works?</em> Seed a page readable only by a test user, ask as another user with a query that ranks it first, and assert it never reaches the model's input.</li>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the sponsor says without the CRM there is no launch. Do you ship the service-account design, and what do you accept in writing?* If it ships, the mirror is tested against the CRM's own visibility for a sample of users every sync, a mismatch disables the CRM source automatically, and Legal signs the residual risk with the sync interval named in the document.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see to drop the final fetch and trust the index's access lists?* A permission-change feed from each source with a measured lag, and a month of audits in which every final-fetch 403 matched a change still inside that lag.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants federated search for correctness, the other the central index for quality. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Run both on the same 200 logged questions, judged for answer quality, and count the questions where each leaked or lost a document under a seeded permission change.
- *A customer files a service-desk ticket asking the assistant to tell the agent reading it to reset the customer's MFA. What in your design stops the reset?* Nothing in the assistant: it cannot reset anything, but it can repeat the request persuasively. That risk sits with the service-desk process, and the note should say so.

## Common mistakes

- Treating internal sources as trusted because they are inside the network.
- Filtering by permission after taking the top k and not noticing the empty answers for users with narrow access.
- Allowlisting internal hosts for links and calling the output path closed.
- Logging retrieved content to a store with wider access than the sources.
- Mirroring a system's authorization model to meet a deadline without a check against the source.
