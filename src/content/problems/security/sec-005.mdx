## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Behavioural">
"Tell me about a time you had to get senior leadership to make a decision about a security risk in an AI feature. What did you put in front of them, what did they decide, and what was written down?"
</Callout>

If you have not had one, answer for this case (scenario). Sales leadership has promised the board an assistant in the CRM that reads inbound customer email, updates account records and sends follow-ups on the rep's behalf. Launch is in five weeks, at the start of the quarter. Your red team ran a fixed suite of 200 injected emails against the pilot; in 14 of them the assistant sent account data to an address that is not a contact on the account. There are 400 reps, each sending about 30 follow-ups a day through the pilot. The VP of Sales owns the launch; the CISO owns breach notification.

Say what you presented, the options and their costs, the decision, who signed it, and what would reopen it.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask the VP which decision she believes is on the table: ship, delay, or ship something narrower.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the red-team result with its conditions: which attack set, which attacker knowledge, and what data left.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask who would have to notify customers if account data left, and on what timeline.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>I put a one-page decision in front of the VP of Sales and the CISO, not a vulnerability report. It opened with the business case: any customer, or anyone who can email a rep, can write instructions the assistant may follow, and the assistant can both read account data and send email. Then the measured result with its limits (14 of 200 on a fixed suite, a floor rather than a ceiling), three options with their cost in rep hours, launch date and residual risk, my recommendation, and the decision required of them. They chose to launch with sends restricted to contacts already on the account and a confirmation for anything else; the VP signed the residual risk, the CISO signed the notification plan, and three triggers reopen it.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The framing.</strong> Leadership was not deciding whether prompt injection is real. They were deciding what the assistant may do on its own in week one. So the page started from what a customer or an outsider can cause: an email to a rep can make the assistant send account data, contract values included, to an address the attacker controls. The mechanism took one sentence.</p>
<p><strong>The number, with its limits.</strong> 14 of 200 is 7 percent on our own fixed suite. I said plainly that this is a lower bound: published defences that measured near zero on fixed suites fell to above 90 percent attack success for most of them when an attacker adapted. A security result reported without that sentence gets read as "93 percent safe".</p>
<p><strong>The options, each with its cost.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><em>Confirm every send.</em> 400 reps × 30 sends = 12,000 confirmations a day; at 5 seconds each (scenario: the pilot's median time to approve a draft) that is 60,000 seconds, about 16.7 rep-hours a day. Residual risk: confirmations clicked without reading, which Meta's own Rule of Two caveat and the CaMeL paper both name.</li>
<li><em>Restrict recipients in code.</em> The send tool accepts only contacts already on the account the email came from; anything else needs a confirmation. Exfiltration to an outside address becomes impossible by construction. Residual risk: an attacker who is, or has compromised, a listed contact still receives what the assistant puts in the email, and the body can carry data from other accounts unless the session reads only this account.</li>
<li><em>Delay eight weeks</em> to split reading and sending into separate sessions, so the session that reads inbound mail cannot send. Residual risk lowest; cost is the quarter's commitment to the board.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>The recommendation and the decision.</strong> I recommended the second option, with the session limited to the one account, and said what it does not cover. The two defensible positions in the room: the CISO preferred the delay, because the residual risk falls on customers; the VP preferred confirmation on every send, because it ships the full feature. Both were reasonable. The recommendation won on a number they could both check: none of the 14 successful sends in the red-team suite would have gone out without a rep's confirmation, and reps confirm only sends to non-contacts instead of all 12,000 a day.</p>
<p><strong>What was written down.</strong> A risk acceptance with the scenario, the controls and their labels (recipient restriction by construction, the classifier and prompt hardening by rate), the residual risk in one sentence, the VP's signature as risk owner, the CISO's notification plan, and an expiry: review at 90 days or on any trigger. Triggers: a confirmed send of account data to a non-contact; a red-team rerun with a higher success rate against listed contacts; a new tool added to the session.</p>
<p><strong>Where this answer fails.</strong> A signed acceptance can become permanent: nobody reopens it when it expires unless someone owns the date. And it can become cover, where the signature is cited instead of the control being improved. The 90-day review had my name on it for that reason.</p>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the VP says the confirmation path for non-contacts costs her reps more time than you estimated and wants it removed. What do you accept in writing, and what do you refuse?* Measure the confirmation rate for two weeks first; if it is high, add the missing contacts to the accounts rather than remove the check, because removing it restores the path the red team used.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see to recommend letting the assistant email non-contacts without a confirmation?* A session design where the part that reads inbound mail cannot choose recipients, verified by a test, and a red-team rerun with an adaptive attacker that fails to move data to any address outside the account.
- *Two directors disagree on whether this is a sales decision or a security decision. How do you decide who signs without deciding by rank?* The owner of the revenue signs the residual risk; the owner of the consequence, breach notification, signs whether the residual risk is acceptable to notify. Both signatures go on one page.
- *Sixty days in, the model vendor retires the model and the replacement fails 31 of 200 on the suite. Who decides whether to ship it?* The same two signers, on a one-page update that states the new number against the old one and whether the recipient restriction still holds, which it does, since it does not depend on the model.

## Common mistakes

- Opening with how prompt injection works instead of what the business stands to lose.
- Presenting the red-team rate as the risk instead of a floor under it.
- Offering one option, so leadership is asked to approve rather than decide.
- Getting a verbal yes with no named owner, no expiry and no trigger to reopen it.
- Rating everything critical, so the one decision that matters gets the same weight as the rest.
