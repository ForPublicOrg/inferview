## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 45 minutes">
Your claims team wants to use a vendor's fine-tuned model to triage insurance claims. The vendor publishes the weights, signed, with a model card naming the open base model and the fine-tuning method, but will not share the training data. Design the trust process that admits this model to production, and state the gaps that remain after it.
</Callout>

The scenario's facts, invented for this problem:

- **The model's job.** It reads each claim's documents and writes a summary and a routing label: fast-track payout, standard, or investigation. Claims labelled fast-track are paid without an adjuster when other checks pass.
- **Volume.** About 3,000 claims a day; in the pilot the model fast-tracked about 40% of them.
- **Compliance.** Your compliance team has classified the system as high-risk under the EU AI Act, so Article 15 applies, including measures against data and model poisoning where appropriate.
- **Team and deadline.** One ML engineer and one security engineer for the six weeks before launch.
- **Updates.** The vendor releases a new fine-tune every month, and the claims director wants each one live within a week.

Choose how many fast-tracked claims a person reviews after launch, and defend the number.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask the claims team what an attacker gains from one claim routed to fast-track, and who could put text into a claim document.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask the vendor what they will put in writing about their training data, their poisoning controls and how they would tell you about a problem found later.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Request the adjusters' review capacity per day, and the share of claims that adjusters overturned in the pilot.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Four layers, and a written statement that the fourth exists because the first three cannot prove the absence of a backdoor. Integrity: pin the release by digest and verify the signature against the vendor's pinned identity, covering every file the loader reads. Format: safetensors only, no remote code, the chat template reviewed and rendered in a sandbox. Behaviour: differential evals against the base model and the previous release on our own claims, plus a trigger sweep we can afford. Containment: the model alone cannot pay a claim; fast-track needs independent rule checks, payouts above a threshold need a person, and a person reviews a sample of fast-tracked claims every day. The gap that remains is a trained-in trigger we did not find, and the contract and the review sample are how we would learn of it.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Threat model first.</strong> The weights cannot open a socket; the harm a backdoored model can do is in its outputs and in what the system does with them. Here the valuable output is the fast-track label, and anyone who files a claim writes text the model reads. A trigger phrase in a claim document that flips routing to fast-track is a fraud tool that needs no access to your systems. Anthropic's study with the UK AI Security Institute and the Alan Turing Institute found 250 poisoned documents enough to plant a narrow trigger in pretrained models from 600M to 13B parameters, regardless of size, while cautioning that the behaviour was low-stakes and that larger models and harmful behaviours were not tested. Sleeper Agents found trained-in behaviour persisting through supervised fine-tuning, RL and adversarial training. The process is built on the assumption that a trigger could exist.</p>
<p><strong>Layer 1, integrity.</strong> Pin each release by manifest digest, verify the vendor's signature against the identity and issuer you pinned when the contract was signed, and refuse a release whose signed manifest does not cover every file the loader reads. The signature tells you the files are the vendor's; the OpenSSF specification is explicit that it says nothing about quality or behaviour.</p>
<p><strong>Layer 2, format and loader.</strong> Accept safetensors only and load without <code>trust_remote_code</code>, so no file in the release runs code. Review the chat template on every release and render it in a sandboxed template engine: CVE-2024-34359 was code execution through a model's chat template in llama-cpp-python. If the model card says the fine-tune is a low-rank adapter on the open base, check it: the difference between each fine-tuned matrix and the base should have the stated rank, which takes one singular value decomposition per matrix and needs no training data. A release that does not match its own card is rejected.</p>
<p><strong>Layer 3, behaviour, and its limit.</strong> Run the claims eval set on the base model, the previous release and the new one, and diff routing per segment. Then sweep for triggers the budget allows. With a vocabulary of <em>V</em> tokens, inserting each single token into a fixed set of <em>k</em> claim templates costs <em>V</em> × <em>k</em> generations: for an illustrative <em>V</em> = 128,000 and <em>k</em> = 20, about 2.6 million short generations, a batch job whose cost follows from your own measured throughput. Two-token triggers cost <em>V</em><sup>2</sup> × <em>k</em> ≈ 3.3 × 10<sup>11</sup>. The sweep finds clumsy triggers and nothing else; a trigger keyed to a phrase, a date or a document layout is outside any affordable search. This is the gap the question asks about, and it is why layer 4 exists.</p>
<p><strong>Layer 4, containment.</strong> Fast-track requires rule checks that do not read the model's output: policy active, claim amount under a limit, no prior claims on the item in a stated window. Above a payout threshold a person approves. Every routing decision is logged with the model release, so a rollback can find the claims a release touched.</p>
<p><strong>The review sample, computed.</strong> At 3,000 claims and 40% fast-track, about 1,200 claims a day are fast-tracked. Suppose a trigger is used on a fraction <em>f</em> of them and a reviewer recognises a triggered claim as wrongly routed. Reviewing <em>s</em> random fast-tracked claims a day finds at least one with probability 1 − (1 − <em>f</em>)<sup><em>s</em></sup>. With <em>f</em> = 0.5% and a 10% sample, <em>s</em> = 120: 1 − 0.995<sup>120</sup> ≈ 1 − e<sup>−0.60</sup> ≈ 45% in a day, and over a week of 840 reviews, 1 − e<sup>−4.2</sup> ≈ 98.5%. A 5% sample halves the exponent: about 26% a day and about 88% a week. I would choose 10% for the first quarter and revisit it against the adjusters' capacity; anything from 5% to 20% is defensible if the reviewers see the claim documents, not only the summary. A sample that a trigger can avoid, such as one drawn only from large claims, is not.</p>
<p><strong>The decision with two defensible answers.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Admit the vendor's model under the four layers</strong>, with a contract that requires a written statement of data sources and poisoning controls, notice within a fixed number of days of any finding, and an audit right. Launch fits the six weeks. The cost is a residual risk you accept in writing and a review team that is now part of the security design.</li>
<li><strong>Fine-tune the open base yourself</strong> on your own labelled claims. You know the data's provenance, and the vendor gap disappears. The cost is the deadline (labelling, training and evaluation do not fit six weeks with two engineers) and a different poisoning surface: your training data comes from claim documents that customers wrote.</li>
</ul>
<p>The conflict is the launch date against assurance about how the weights were made. I choose the vendor model under containment for launch, and start the in-house fine-tune as the reversal path, ready if the vendor will not sign the notification clause or review finds a trigger.</p>
<p><strong>The monthly update.</strong> Layers 1 and 2 are automated and take hours. Layer 3 takes days of compute. A one-week target is achievable only if the differential eval and the sweep run as soon as the release lands; a release that has not passed them stays off, and the claims director is told which check is late.</p>
<p><strong>Failure mode of this design.</strong> The containment covers routing, and the model also writes the summary an adjuster reads. A trigger that drops fraud indicators from the summary, rather than flipping the label, is invisible to the fast-track rules and to a reviewer who reads only the summary. Reviews have to sample claims against their source documents, and the eval set needs cases where the summary must mention a specific indicator.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>The vendor offers to let an independent auditor inspect the training data under NDA. Does that close the gap?</em> It moves it: the auditor sees the data the vendor shows, and a check of a sample against a stated corpus finds sloppy contamination, not a targeted one. It is worth having for Article 15's documentation.</li>
<li><em>Why not run the model in a network-isolated cluster and call it contained?</em> The weights do not make network calls; isolation protects against the serving code, not against outputs the system acts on.</li>
<li><em>What would make you skip the trigger sweep?</em> If containment made a flipped label worthless, for example if every fast-track claim were paid only after a rule check that the model's output cannot influence.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

### The written artefact

The risk acceptance note for the vendor model, signed by the claims director and the head of security.

- **Accepted risk:** a trained-in trigger that the intake checks did not find. Bounded by: fast-track requires rule checks independent of the model; payouts above the threshold need a person; 10% of fast-tracked claims are reviewed against their documents daily.
- **Intake per release:** digest pin and signature check against the pinned vendor identity; safetensors only, no remote code, chat template diffed and sandboxed; adapter rank checked against the model card; differential eval against base and previous release; single-token trigger sweep.
- **Vendor obligations:** written data-source and poisoning-control statement per release; notice of any finding within the contracted number of days; audit right.
- **Rollback triggers:** a reviewed claim whose routing cannot be explained by its documents; a routing-rate change in any segment beyond the pilot's weekly variation; any vendor notice; a release whose files do not match its card. Rollback is to the previous release, and fast-track pauses if no release has passed intake.
- **Reversal path:** in-house fine-tune of the open base, staffed from week 7.

## Follow-ups

- *The trigger sweep and the 10% review cost more than the fast-track saves in adjuster time in the first quarter. Which do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *What would you need to see in a year of reviews before you reduced the sample to 2%?*
- *The claims director says the vendor is a reputable company and the controls treat them as an attacker. Two senior engineers side with each of you. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *The vendor's next release changes the base model. Which parts of the intake start again from nothing?*

## Common mistakes

- Treating a valid signature as evidence about the training data or about backdoors.
- Claiming behavioural testing shows the absence of a trigger, when the search space is far beyond any budget.
- Isolating the model's network and calling it contained, when the harm travels through its outputs.
- Sampling only the routing label and never the summary a person relies on.
- Letting monthly updates skip intake because the vendor and the base model are unchanged.
