checklist:
  - Builds the inventory from what the model gateway sees, so an unregistered system or an undeclared model or tool is refused or flagged rather than missed
  - Computes the risk tier from declared capabilities (data classes, tools that change state, users, Annex III classification), not from a team's own rating
  - Defines change classes (prompt edit, model version, new tool or data source, new system) and a gate per class and tier, with most changes passing on automated evidence
  - Works out reviewer hours from change volume and review time, and shows the design fits the governance team's capacity
  - Has the pipeline write the evidence (commit, eval run, model version, approver, deploy time) into an append-only store auditors can sample
  - Gives the workflow states, idempotent eval runs keyed on the change, a bounded retry for flaky evals, and timeouts that escalate rather than auto-approve high-tier changes
  - Includes waivers with an owner, an expiry and a count reported upward
  - Samples auto-approved changes after the fact and checks that the eval suites still predict production problems
  - Records per-inference metadata, a kill switch per system and AI incident types in the incident runbook
  - Treats model vendors as subprocessors with recorded retention settings and finds unsanctioned AI tool use
  - Chooses between central pre-approval and owner approval with sampling, with the cost of each, and states the failure modes of the choice
  - Writes the one-page policy, the rollout plan with rollback triggers and what the company gives up

## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Workflow, 50 minutes">
"The board wants an AI governance process that satisfies our auditors without stopping teams from shipping. The VP of Engineering will fight any new approval gate; the CISO wants every model change reviewed. Design the process: what gets registered, what gets reviewed by whom, what evidence exists, and what happens when something goes wrong."
</Callout>

The company (scenario):

- 14 product teams ship AI features. Across them, about 40 AI changes a week: roughly 30 prompt edits, 4 model version changes, 4 new tools or data sources, 2 new AI systems.
- Most production traffic goes through an internal model gateway that holds the vendor keys. Two teams call vendors directly with their own keys.
- SOC 2 Type II next year will, for the first time, include AI features. Legal has classified one product as high-risk under Annex III, with obligations from 2 December 2027.
- The governance team is one GRC analyst and one security engineer, plus you, for ten weeks.
- A code change policy already exists: pull request, one approval, CI green.

Design the workflow: steps, state, retries, approvals and budgets. Choose the review time per human review and the share of changes that skip governance review you sample, and show that the design fits the team.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which of the 40 weekly changes have ever caused a customer-visible incident, and which kind they were.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask the auditor which AI evidence it intends to sample, and in what form it accepts an approval.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask the gateway team what it can enforce per application key today: models, tools, logging.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Make the gateway the control point and the pipeline the evidence writer. Every AI system has an inventory entry declared in code, and the gateway only serves the models and tools that entry declares, so the inventory cannot drift from production. The tier comes from the declaration: personal data, tools that change state, customer-facing, Annex III. Each change class gets a gate per tier, and most changes pass on automated evidence: a prompt edit in a low tier needs its eval run, not a person. Human review is kept for model version changes, new tools or data sources and new systems above the lowest tier, and every change to the Annex III product. That is at most 13 reviews a week instead of 40, roughly 11 hours with post-hoc sampling against 30 for reviewing everything. The trade: owners approve within policy and the governance team samples afterwards, which is faster and will occasionally let a bad prompt reach production.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>1. Register.</strong> An inventory entry per AI system, in a repository, with owner, models and pinned versions, access mode, data classes, tools and the actions they take, users, and legal's classification: the fields GV-1.6-003 of the Generative AI Profile suggests. The gateway reads the entries and refuses a model or tool a system has not declared. The two teams with direct keys move onto the gateway, and vendor keys are issued only to it; otherwise the inventory describes the systems that chose to be described.</p>
<p><strong>2. Tier from the declaration.</strong> Tier 1: internal users, no personal data, no state-changing tools. Tier 2: customer-facing or personal data. Tier 3: Annex III, or tools that move money, send external messages or change records outside the company. A rule computes it; a team cannot argue itself down a tier without changing what it declares, and changing the declaration is itself a reviewed change.</p>
<p><strong>3. Change classes and gates.</strong></p>
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Change</th><th>Tier 1</th><th>Tier 2</th><th>Tier 3</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Prompt edit</td><td>Eval run passes</td><td>Eval run and owner approval on the pull request</td><td>Eval run and governance review</td></tr>
<tr><td>Model version</td><td>Eval run passes</td><td>Eval and security suites, governance review</td><td>Same, plus owner approval and a canary</td></tr>
<tr><td>New tool or data source</td><td>Re-tier, no review</td><td>Re-tier, design review</td><td>Re-tier, design review</td></tr>
<tr><td>New system</td><td>Inventory entry, kill switch tested before launch</td><td>Same, plus design review with the context and risk list</td><td>Same as tier 2</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p><strong>4. The capacity arithmetic.</strong> I choose 45 minutes per human review and a 10 percent post-hoc sample at 30 minutes each. Reviewing all 40 changes: 40 × 0.75 = 30 hours a week, most of one person on a two-person team. With the gates above, assuming 3 of the 30 prompt edits are on the tier-3 product and all 4 model changes are tier 2 or 3, and counting every capability change and new system as reviewed (an upper bound, since tier-1 ones are not): 4 model changes + 4 capability changes + 2 new systems + 3 tier-3 prompts = 13 reviews × 0.75 = 9.75 hours, plus 10 percent of the 27 prompt edits that pass without a governance review (auto-approved in what follows, even when an owner approved the pull request), about 3 × 0.5 = 1.5 hours. About 11 hours a week. A 20-minute review time would be defensible for model version changes with a good eval report. At 90 minutes the load is 13 × 1.5 + 1.5 = 21 hours, half of one of the two people, which leaves too little of the ten weeks for the gateway enforcement this design depends on.</p>
<p><strong>5. Workflow state.</strong> A change record moves through proposed, gated, approved or rejected or waived, deployed, verified. The eval run is keyed on the system id and the change's content hash, so a retried pipeline reuses a result instead of rerunning or double-recording. A flaky eval gets two retries, then escalates; it never passes by timeout. Approvals have a two-business-day target; past it, an owner approval escalates to the team lead and a governance review to the CISO, never to automatic approval. Waivers carry an owner and an expiry of at most 30 days, and the count of open waivers goes to the risk committee monthly.</p>
<p><strong>6. Evidence the pipeline writes.</strong> Every deploy appends commit, change class, tier, eval run id and result, model version, approver and time to an append-only store. The gateway writes a per-inference record: system, model version, prompt version, tools called, outcome, latency. These are the categories the CSA note says auditors ask for, and none of them is produced by a meeting.</p>
<p><strong>7. Run and respond.</strong> Each system has a kill switch at the gateway with a named owner (MANAGE 2.4) and a written trigger. The incident runbook, built on SP 800-61 Rev. 3, gains AI incident types: cross-tenant data in an answer, an unauthorised tool action, a vendor model change with a regression. Vendors are recorded as subprocessors with their retention settings. Unsanctioned AI tool use is found from egress logs and expense data, and each finding either gets an inventory entry or a block.</p>
<p><strong>The decision with two defensible answers.</strong> Central pre-approval by the governance team for everything above tier 1 is what the CISO wants: one standard, and reviewers who are not judging their own team's work. Its cost is a queue: with two reviewers it caps throughput, and teams that wait will route around it, as the two direct-key teams already route around the gateway. Owner approval within policy plus post-hoc sampling keeps the queue short; its cost is that a bad change ships before the sample finds it, and an auditor may question approvals by the team that made the change. I take a split: owner approval and sampling for prompts and tier 2, central review for model changes, capability changes and new systems above tier 1, and for every change to the tier-3 product. The Annex III product also needs the testing frequency and accountability Article 17 asks of a quality management system, and central review is where that is written down.</p>
<p><strong>The constraint conflict.</strong> Speed against assurance, under a deadline and between two executives: every gate the CISO gets costs the VP lead time, and ten weeks is enough to build the gateway enforcement and the evidence store, not a review board's backlog.</p>
<p><strong>What I write down.</strong> A one-page policy (tiers, change classes, gates, waivers), signed once by both executives. A rollout plan: weeks 1 to 4 inventory and gateway enforcement, weeks 5 to 8 evidence store and gates in warn-only mode, weeks 9 and 10 gates enforced. Rollback triggers: if median lead time for tier-2 changes doubles against the warn-only baseline for two weeks, the gate that caused it returns to warn-only while it is fixed; if a sampled auto-approved change caused an incident, its class moves up a tier.</p>
<p><strong>What I give up.</strong> Teams lose direct vendor keys. Tier-3 changes wait for people. Low-tier prompt changes ship without a human looking at them.</p>
<p><strong>Failure modes of this design.</strong> The eval gate is only as good as the suite: a team whose suite has not changed in six months passes every prompt edit, so the sample has to check whether the suites still catch the incidents that happen (MEASURE 4). Declarations can be wrong at the start: a tool that "only reads" may have a write endpoint, and nothing in the gateway knows the difference unless tool scopes are checked against the declaration. And waivers become the real process if their count is not reported.</p>
</Fragment>

<li><em>The auditor asks who approved a tier-2 prompt edit. What do you show?</em> The change record: the eval run that gated it, the deploy, and the policy page that says the owner approves in that class.</li>
<li><em>Why compute the tier rather than ask teams?</em> Because the people asked have a reason to answer low, and a computed tier changes only when what the system can do changes.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the VP says gateway enforcement costs her teams a quarter of migration work, more than the audit is worth. Which part do you drop, and what risk do you write down?*
- *Reversal: after two quarters, sampling has found no bad auto-approved change. Would you remove the sample, or lower it, and what would you need to see before doing either?*
- *The CISO and the VP disagree on whether model version changes in tier 2 need central review. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *A vendor retires the model behind the Annex III product in sixty days; the replacement regresses on 12 of 40 eval cases. Which ships degraded, and who signs?*

## Common mistakes

- Building a review board and calling it a process, then finding that every review is a meeting and none leaves a record a sample can test.
- Letting teams rate their own risk.
- Keeping the inventory in a spreadsheet the gateway never reads.
- Approving on timeout to protect lead time.
- Treating governance as a launch gate, when most of the risk arrives in changes after launch.
