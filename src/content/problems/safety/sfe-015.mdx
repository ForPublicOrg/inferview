## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As a public question list phrases it">
"Your LLM does not admit when it does not know the answer. How do you make it say 'I don't know'?"
</Callout>

The product (scenario): a help assistant inside a payroll application answers about 3,000 questions a day from payroll administrators. Each answer carries a verifier score between 0 and 1. A question the assistant declines goes to a payroll specialist. The specialist team can take about 1,200 handed-off questions a day without a backlog.

Two specialists labelled a random sample of 2,000 recent questions as answered correctly or not, and grouped them by verifier score (scenario data):

| Verifier score | Questions | Correct |
|---|---|---|
| 0.95 to 1.00 | 700 | 97% |
| 0.90 to 0.95 | 300 | 91% |
| 0.80 to 0.90 | 400 | 78% |
| 0.70 to 0.80 | 300 | 70% |
| 0.50 to 0.70 | 200 | 55% |
| below 0.50 | 100 | 30% |

Score a correct answer at 1 (a ticket a specialist did not handle) and a handed-off question at 0. Finance puts the cost of a wrong answer, which a specialist later unwinds with a correction run, somewhere between 3 and 9 tickets' worth. Choose that cost, decide which score buckets the assistant answers, and give the share of all questions it answers. Then say what you do about the specialist team's limit.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask finance which wrong answers sit at the 9 end of their range and which at the 3 end, and whether those differ by question topic.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the correct rate per score bucket, with the bucket sizes, rather than one accuracy figure for the whole sample.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask the specialist team lead what a day over capacity does to their response time.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Answering a bucket pays when its measured correct rate p beats the wrong-answer cost: p − (1 − p) × c &gt; 0, which is p &gt; c / (1 + c), the confidence target t in Kalai and colleagues' scoring rule. At a cost of 3 to 3.5 the assistant answers the top three buckets, 70% of questions; from about 3.6 up to 9 it answers the top two, 50%. The thresholds go on the measured correct rate per bucket, not on the score itself. At 50% coverage the specialists get 1,500 questions a day against a limit of 1,200, so the cost of errors and the queue pull in opposite directions, and that conflict is the actual decision.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The break-even per bucket.</strong> Answering a bucket is worth p − (1 − p) × c per question; abstaining is worth 0. Solving for the cost at which a bucket stops paying gives c = p / (1 − p):</p>
<ul>
<li>0.97 / 0.03 = 32.3, so the top bucket is answered at any cost in finance's range.</li>
<li>0.91 / 0.09 = 10.1, answered up to a cost of 10.</li>
<li>0.78 / 0.22 = 3.55, answered only if a wrong answer costs less than about 3.5 tickets.</li>
<li>0.70 / 0.30 = 2.33 and below: never answered at any cost finance considers.</li>
</ul>
<p>At c = 3 the answered buckets hold 700 + 300 + 400 = 1,400 of 2,000 questions, 70%. At any c from 3.6 to 9 they hold 1,000, 50%. In the paper's terms, c = 3 is a target of t = 3 / 4 = 0.75 and c = 9 is t = 9 / 10 = 0.9: the confidence target and the penalty are the same choice written two ways.</p>
<p><strong>Value, computed for c = 3 on the sample of 2,000.</strong> Top bucket 679 correct − 21 wrong × 3 = 616. Second 273 − 27 × 3 = 192. Third 312 − 88 × 3 = 48. Answering those three gives 856. Answering everything gives 1,614 correct − 386 wrong × 3 = 456, a little over half.</p>
<p><strong>The dominant term</strong> is the third bucket. It is 20% of traffic and its value per question, 0.78 − 0.22c, sits close to zero across finance's range: +0.12 at c = 3, −0.10 at c = 4, −0.32 at c = 5. Every other bucket is answered or refused at any c in the range. The assumption that flips the conclusion is whether c is below about 3.5.</p>
<p><strong>The sample cannot settle that bucket.</strong> 400 questions at 78% have a standard error of √(0.78 × 0.22 / 400) = 0.021, so the 95% interval runs from about 74% to 82%. The break-even cost at those ends is 0.74 / 0.26 = 2.8 and 0.82 / 0.18 = 4.6. If finance's honest answer is "about 4", the sample is too small to say whether to answer this bucket. Labelling 400 more questions from it narrows the interval by a factor of √2; splitting it at 0.85 may show a half that clears the bar and a half that does not.</p>
<p><strong>The queue.</strong> At 50% coverage, 1,500 questions a day go to specialists, 300 over their limit. Two defensible answers:</p>
<ul>
<li>Answer the third bucket anyway to protect the queue (70% coverage, 900 handoffs). The cost at c = 5 is 3,000 × 0.2 × 0.32 = 192 tickets' worth of correction work a day, which lands on the same specialists a few days later, so it does not relieve them for long.</li>
<li>Keep 50% coverage and make the extra 300 a day cheaper rather than answering them: route them to a next-business-day queue with the draft answer attached for a specialist to confirm, which costs response time on those questions and needs the payroll team to accept a slower path.</li>
</ul>
<p>I would take the second for the high-cost topics (tax filings, pay runs already submitted) and the first for topics where finance puts c near 3, which requires finance's cost to be split by topic, not one range.</p>
<p><strong>Unreasonable choices.</strong> Comparing the overall accuracy, 1,614 / 2,000 = 80.7%, with the target and answering everything: the average hides buckets at 55% and 30%. Thresholding on the score itself: at c = 4 the target is 0.8, the third bucket's scores are all at or above 0.8, and its measured rate is 78%, so a score threshold answers a bucket that loses 400 × (0.78 − 0.22 × 4) = 40 tickets' worth per 2,000 questions. Anything below 50% coverage refuses the second bucket, which pays at every cost finance named.</p>
<p><strong>Where this answer fails.</strong> The table is a snapshot. A model update, a new payroll feature or a change in who asks shifts the correct rate per bucket without moving the scores, and the thresholds then sit in the wrong place with nothing alerting. The labelled sample has to be refreshed on every model or prompt change and on a schedule, and the per-bucket rates compared before the thresholds are reused.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Can the model do this itself if the prompt states the target, as the paper proposes, instead of an external verifier?</em> The paper calls that behavioural calibration and proposes auditing it by comparing error rates across targets; you would still need the labelled sample to check it, so it moves where the threshold lives, not whether you measure.</li>
<li><em>The verifier is a second model call on every question. When is it not worth it?</em> When nearly all traffic lands in buckets that are answered or refused at every cost in the range; here 20% of traffic sits near break-even, so the score is doing work.</li>
<li><em>What do you show the payroll administrator when the assistant declines?</em> The handoff status and an expected response time, and the documents retrieval found, so a decline is still useful.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *The product manager wants 85% coverage for the launch announcement. What do you put in writing?* The 15 points above 70% come from the 0.70 to 0.80 bucket, whose value per question, 0.70 − 0.30 × c, is negative at every cost in finance's range, from −0.2 tickets' worth at c = 3 to −2 at c = 9; the memo states that per day and names who owns the correction work.
- *Finance revises the cost of a wrong answer on submitted pay runs to 30 tickets. What changes?* The second bucket's break-even is 10, so it stops being answered for that topic; if that topic's scores spread like the sample, its coverage falls to 35% and its handoffs need their own capacity line.
- *What would make you lower the thresholds after launch?* A month of production labels showing the third bucket above its break-even rate with an interval that clears it, not one good week.

## Common mistakes

- Setting the threshold on the verifier score instead of the measured correct rate per bucket.
- Comparing the overall accuracy with the target, which approves every bucket.
- Treating the sample's 78% as exact when its interval straddles the break-even.
- Picking the coverage without checking the specialist queue, then watching the queue force the threshold down later.
- Relying on "say I don't know if unsure" in the prompt, with no cost and no measurement behind it.
