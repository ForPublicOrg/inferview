## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate">
"Trust and safety wants Prompt Guard 2 on every user message and Llama Guard 4 on every reply, blocking the turn if either fires. Before we agree: how many honest users hit a block, what does it add to time to first token, and when is your number wrong?"
</Callout>

The product, as a scenario: a consumer chat assistant with 400,000 conversations a day and a mean of 5 user turns per conversation. Nine in ten user turns fit in one 512-token segment; the other tenth paste text that splits into four segments. Replies stream today, with a p95 time to first token of 900 ms against a 1,200 ms budget.

Published figures, checked 2026-10-28:

| Guard | Placement | Meta's reported operating point | Latency on the card |
|---|---|---|---|
| Llama Prompt Guard 2 86M | each 512-token segment of the user turn | recall 97.5% at 1% FPR, Meta's private direct-jailbreak set | 92.4 ms per 512-token classification, A100 |
| Llama Guard 4 12B | each reply | recall 69% at 11% FPR, Meta's in-house English output set | none published |

Your own measurements, as a scenario: on 5,000 benign production segments, Prompt Guard 2 at Meta's 1% operating point flagged about 1%. On 2,000 benign production replies, Llama Guard 4 at its default decision flagged 30. On your GPUs, Llama Guard 4 takes 210 ms at p50 to classify one complete reply.

1. Choose the false-positive rate you will use for each guard, and say why.
2. Each segment and each complete reply is checked once, and a flag from either guard blocks that turn. Estimate the share of benign conversations that see at least one false block. **Enter this figure, in percent.**
3. Estimate what the guards add to time to first token with the input check in series, and with it in parallel with the model call. Say what the output check does to streaming.
4. Say when the independence assumption behind your figure breaks, and which way it moves the number.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Use your own rates, not the card's: about 1% per segment for Prompt Guard 2 and 30 / 2,000 = 1.5% per reply for Llama Guard 4. A user turn averages 1.3 segments, so its input check falsely fires 1.29% of the time; with the reply check, a turn is falsely blocked 2.78% of the time, and a 5-turn conversation 1 − 0.9723<sup>5</sup> = 13.1% of the time, about 52,500 conversations a day. The input check costs 92.4 ms in series and close to nothing in parallel; the output check either waits for the complete reply, which ends streaming, or runs per chunk, which multiplies the checks. The figure assumes independent errors. They cluster by topic and user, which lowers the share of conversations hit and concentrates the blocks on a few users who are blocked nearly every time.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Which rates.</b> Meta's 11% is measured on its own output-filtering set, which is built to contain hard cases; it says nothing about the benign share of your traffic. Your 30 of 2,000 is 1.5%, with a 95% Wilson interval of about 1.05% to 2.13%, so anything in that range is a defensible choice. Prompt Guard 2's 1% is Meta's chosen operating point, and your 5,000-segment sample agrees with it.</p>
<p><b>Input check per turn.</b> A one-segment turn is falsely flagged with probability 1%. A four-segment turn passes only if all four segments pass: 1 − 0.99<sup>4</sup> = 3.94%. Mixed 90/10: 0.9 × 1% + 0.1 × 3.94% = 1.29%.</p>
<p><b>Per turn, both guards.</b> A benign turn survives with probability (1 − 0.0129) × (1 − 0.015) = 0.9723, so 2.78% of turns are falsely blocked.</p>
<p><b>Per conversation.</b> Five turns: 1 − 0.9723<sup>5</sup> = 13.1%, which is 52,500 of 400,000 conversations a day. At the ends of the Wilson interval for the reply guard the figure runs from 11.1% to 15.9%. The dominant term is not either guard's rate but the number of checks a conversation passes through: 5 × 1.3 = 6.5 segment checks plus 5 reply checks, 11.5 chances to be wrong, each around 1%.</p>
<p><b>Latency.</b> In series, a one-segment turn waits 92.4 ms (Meta's A100 figure at 512 tokens) before the model starts. A pasted turn waits the same if its four segments are classified in parallel, and 4 × 92.4 = 369.6 ms if one device runs them one after another, which takes p95 from 900 ms to about 1,270 ms and breaks the budget. In parallel with the model call, the verdict arrives long before the first token is shown, so time to first token barely moves; the price is model tokens spent on turns that end up blocked. The reply check cannot overlap the generation it judges. Run once on the complete reply, it adds the whole generation time plus 210 ms before the user sees anything, which removes streaming. Run on four chunks per reply at the same 1.5% each, a turn is falsely blocked 1 − (1 − 0.0129)(0.985)<sup>4</sup> = 7.08% of the time and a conversation 30.7% of the time.</p>
<p><b>Correlation.</b> The calculation treats every check as an independent coin. Real false flags cluster: a security engineer asking about exploit code, a nurse asking about overdose thresholds. Take a scenario where 3% of conversations are on such topics and each of their turns is falsely blocked 40% of the time, with the other conversations at 1.62% per turn so the average per-turn rate stays 2.78%. Then 10.4% of conversations see a false block instead of 13.1%, and the hard 3% are blocked 1 − 0.6<sup>5</sup> = 92.2% of the time. The average improved and the product got worse for a group that will leave. Correlation hurts recall too: the two recalls come from different benchmarks measuring different events (a jailbreak attempt, a harmful reply), so multiplying their miss rates (2.5% × 31% = 0.78%) is not a combined recall, and an adaptive attacker who has beaten one guard is the one most likely to beat the second.</p>

### Which answers are unreasonable

- **47.7%**: Meta's 11% used as the reply guard's rate on your traffic. That number describes Meta's hard test set, and you have a measurement of your own.
- **2.8%**: the per-turn rate reported as the per-conversation rate. Nobody meets a turn in isolation.
- **0.015%**: the two rates multiplied, which is the false block rate of a stack that blocks only when both guards agree, not when either does.
- **11.8%** ignores the pasted segments and still sits inside the band, because one turn in ten adds little here. On a product where most turns carry documents, the same omission would land far outside it.

### The decision: tighten the thresholds, or change what a flag does

The conflict is safety against usability: the guards as proposed block 13% of honest conversations, and a budget of 2% (scenario) is more than six times smaller.

**Tighten both thresholds to the budget.** Use each model's score (Prompt Guard 2's malicious probability, Llama Guard 4's first-token probability) to pick a threshold near 0.18% per check. Cost: recall at that point is lower than at the default and unknown until you measure it on your own red-team set, and you may find a category where it is unacceptable.

**Make the input flag escalate instead of block.** A Prompt Guard 2 flag routes the turn to a stricter path (a refusal-tuned system prompt, no tools, the reply guard at a stricter threshold) and only the reply guard blocks. False blocks then come from replies alone, 1 − 0.985<sup>5</sup> = 7.3% at today's rate, so the reply guard still needs a threshold near 1 − 0.98<sup>1/5</sup> = 0.40% per reply. Cost: a jailbreak that Prompt Guard 2 catches is no longer stopped outright, so you rely on the stricter path holding. This is the cascade logic Anthropic describes, where the first stage escalates rather than refuses and can therefore afford more false positives. One trap: Prompt Guard 2 detects jailbreak technique, not harmful intent, so it cannot be the gate that decides whether Llama Guard 4 runs at all. A plainly worded harmful request contains no jailbreak.

I would escalate on input, tighten the reply threshold, and run the input check in parallel with the model call. Written down: the per-category threshold, the recall measured at it, the share of conversations with a false block per user segment, and a rollback trigger (false blocks above 2% of conversations for a day, or any user segment above 20%). The failure mode of this answer is the one the correlation section predicts: the average meets the budget while one topic, often a professional one, is blocked most of the time, and a dashboard that shows one rate never shows it.

## Follow-ups

- *Your plan needs labelled production traffic and a red-team set per category, and you have two weeks. Which categories do you measure, and which do you ship on Meta's numbers with the risk written down?*
- *The reply guard's false blocks concentrate on one language. Do you raise its threshold for that language, route that language to a different guard, or accept the rate, and who signs off?*
- *A senior engineer proposes Prompt Guard 2 22M (Meta reports 88.7% recall at 1% FPR and 19.3 ms) to save latency. What does it change in your estimate, and what does it cost?*

## Common mistakes

- Pricing one check and reporting it as the user's experience, when a conversation passes through about a dozen checks.
- Taking a vendor's false-positive rate on its own hard test set as the rate on your traffic.
- Multiplying two recalls measured on different benchmarks into one combined recall.
- Forgetting that a 512-token window turns one long message into several checks, each with its own chance of a false flag.
- Moving the reply check to streamed chunks to save latency without recounting the false blocks it adds.
