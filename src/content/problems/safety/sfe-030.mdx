## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As a public interview guide phrases it">
"Your model started refusing benign requests after a safety-training round. What do you investigate?"
</Callout>

Your team post-trains an open-weights model for a consumer assistant (scenario). Release R7 added a safety round on top of R6. Everything below is from the team's tracker.

```text
R7 change log
  preference data   +6,000 refusal pairs (chosen: refusal, rejected: compliance)
                    helpfulness mix unchanged: 40,000 pairs
  DPO beta          0.10 -> 0.05
  eval harness      refusal detector: regex on refusal phrases -> LLM judge (same week)

refusal data card
  source            red-team prompt generator seeded with a keyword list
  seed keywords     kill, shoot, bomb, crack, exploit, overdose, poison, hack, steal, weapon, ...
  prompts           6,000, from the generator
  chosen responses  refusals with a short safety note

evals, R6 -> R7 (no system prompt)
  unsafe prompts (600, in-house)                     refused  91% -> 97%
  borderline benign prompts (250, in-house)          refused   8% -> 31%
  win rate vs R6, 1,000 prompts without sensitive
    words (LLM judge, ties split)                    50% -> 49%

production canary, R7 on 5% of traffic, one week
  "should not have been refused" reports per 10,000 conversations
                                                     R6 0.9   R7 6.1

20 refusals sampled from the canary (prompts R6 answered); six of them
  "How do I kill a Python process that ignores SIGTERM?"
  "Best way to shoot portraits in harsh midday sun?"
  "How do I crack an egg with one hand?"
  "Is chocolate poison for dogs, and how much is too much?"
  "Write a villain's monologue for my tabletop campaign."
  "How do I test my own login form for SQL injection?"
```

The safety team wants R7 shipped to everyone on Monday for the 91 to 97% gain. Which defect explains the refusals users are reporting? Then rank the others, say what you would collect before Monday, and decide what ships.

## Answer

The correct choice is the refusal data. Every one of the 6,000 refusal prompts contains a seed keyword, every chosen response is a refusal, and nothing in the round pairs those words with a helpful answer. The cheapest feature that separates "refuse" from "answer" in that data is the presence of the word, and the canary sample is what that looks like: Python processes, portraits and eggs. Count the seed words in the 20 sampled refusals and 17 of the 20 contain one. Prompts without sensitive words held their win rate, 49% against R6, so the change is concentrated, not general. XSTest describes the same failure from the outside: refusals of safe prompts that share language with unsafe ones.

**Why the other options are tempting, and where they rank.**

1. The data: explains the symptom.
2. The DPO beta: a real change in the same run. A lower beta lets the policy move further from the reference, which makes whatever the data teaches stronger. It is an amplifier, not the cause; reverting it alone would leave a smaller keyword effect. Re-run with 0.10 to size its share.
3. The judge: a real measurement change in the same week, which is why the borderline 8 to 31% cannot be read at face value until R6's outputs are rescored with the new judge. The production reports, 0.9 to 6.1 per 10,000 conversations, are a separate signal the judge does not touch, so the regression is real even if its size on the eval is not yet known.
4. The fixed-trade belief: Llama 2's appendix reports false refusals rising from 15% to 27% on borderline prompts and from 0.006% to 0.05% on ordinary ones as the safety data share grew. The trade lands on prompts that look unsafe, and what the data contains decides how much.

**What to collect before Monday, and what to do without it.**

- R6's outputs rescored by the new judge, to know the true borderline regression.
- The canary refusal rate split by seed-keyword presence: if the rate on prompts without the words is flat, the diagnosis is confirmed without an attribution method.
- A borderline set not built from the seed list. The in-house 250 may share its vocabulary with the training data; XSTest's 250 safe prompts or OR-Bench's hard subset of about 1,000 are independent of it.

Without those, the canary reports and the 17-of-20 sample are enough not to ship R7 to everyone on Monday.

**The decision.** Two defensible answers:

- **Hold R6, rebuild the round.** Add contrast pairs (benign prompts with the same seed words, chosen response helpful) and re-run, with beta back at 0.10 unless the re-run shows the lower value is needed. Costs the 91 to 97% unsafe-prompt gain for the length of the rebuild, and the safety team's schedule.
- **Ship R7 behind a router.** A classifier sends prompts with seed words that it scores as benign to R6 and everything else to R7. Costs a second checkpoint in serving, a classifier call on every request, and a router to maintain. Its worst case is bounded: a prompt routed wrongly gets R6, which refused 91% of the unsafe set.

I would hold R6 and rebuild, because the router fixes the symptom for one keyword list and leaves a model that refuses by vocabulary for every word the router does not cover. The router is the answer if the safety gain is needed for a deadline the rebuild cannot meet.

**The gate, a quantity to choose.** The release gate needs two numbers, a floor on unsafe refusals and a ceiling on borderline refusals, and the ceiling is limited by the set size. On 250 prompts at about 10% refused, the standard error is √(0.1 × 0.9 / 250) = 1.9 points, so a difference smaller than about 4 points from R6 is not distinguishable. A ceiling of 12% against R6's 8% is defensible; 9% is not measurable on this set; 20% concedes most of the regression. A gate of unsafe at least 95% and borderline at most 12%, measured on an independent set, is one reasonable pair.

**Where the rebuild fails.** Contrast pairs written from the same seed list teach the model those words are sometimes benign, and they can lower the unsafe rate along with the borderline rate: the 97% may come back as 93%. And if the contrast pairs, the borderline eval and the unsafe eval all come from one generator and one list, the gate passes on vocabulary the model has memorised in both directions and fails on the next word. The independent borderline set is part of the fix, not a nice-to-have.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the safety team says a rebuild costs two weeks of exposure at 91% instead of 97% and they will not sign it. What do you ship Monday, and what risk do you accept in writing?* R7 behind the router for the seed-word prompts only, with the written risk that unlisted words still over-refuse and the router is retired when the rebuilt round passes the gate.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see to ship R7 unrouted after all?* The rescored R6 borderline rate within 4 points of R7's, and canary reports per 10,000 conversations back near R6's on prompts with seed words.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants contrast data, the other wants the refusal pairs halved. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Train both on the same budget and compare on the gate's two numbers, measured on the independent borderline set.
- *Every model release now has to clear both numbers. Who owns the borderline set, and how often is it refreshed so it does not become the training target?*

## Common mistakes

- Reading the borderline jump without asking whether the judge changed.
- Reverting the beta and calling it fixed.
- Treating over-refusal as the fixed price of the safety gain.
- Building the contrast data and the eval from one keyword list.
- Fixing it with a system-prompt line telling the model not to over-refuse, which leaves the trained behaviour in place.
