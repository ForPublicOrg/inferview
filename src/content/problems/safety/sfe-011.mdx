## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 40 minutes">
A product team wants the support agent to get write access to the payments database so refunds go faster. "Talk them out of it, or tell me the guardrails that would make it acceptable." Then: "Product shows you a slide. Manual refunds cost far more than the fraud you are worried about. Respond."
</Callout>

The situation, as a scenario:

- The support agent answers customers in chat, reads their order history, and today hands refunds to a human queue.
- 8,000 refund requests a week. A human takes a median of 9 minutes per refund at a loaded $0.80 a minute, so $7.20 each and $57,600 a week. Product's slide puts refund abuse at under $3,000 a week.
- By amount: 70% under $50 (average $21), 22% from $50 to $200, 8% above $200.
- The payments database belongs to the payments team. Money moves only through their refund service, which calls the payment processor and accepts an idempotency key. They will not rewrite it this quarter.
- You have two engineers for six weeks.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what the 9 minutes are spent on: finding the order, deciding, or entering the refund.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask the payments team what their refund service already checks, and what a direct database update would skip.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask fraud for last year's refund abuse by claim type, not as one total.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>No database writes: the agent reads text anyone can send, holds customer data and would change state, all three of Meta's Rule of Two properties, and a database row is the wrong layer anyway, because money moves through the refund service and the processor. Give the agent one tool, <code>request_refund(order_id, amount, reason)</code>, whose code takes the customer from the session, checks ownership, the refundable balance and the eligibility rules, refunds only to the original payment method, and passes an idempotency key to the refund service. Under $50 it executes; from $50 to $200 a person approves the exact call with the order record beside it; above $200 stays manual. To product: you are right about expected loss, and this design takes about 90% of the cost out. What I refused is a capability whose worst case the refund policy does not bound.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Why not the database.</b> Customer messages are untrusted input, order history is private data, and a write changes state: Meta's rule says such an agent needs supervision, by a person or by another reliable validation method. A raw write has neither. It also bypasses the system that actually refunds: an <code>UPDATE</code> that marks an order refunded without the processor call is a reconciliation break, and one that does call it is a second refund path the payments team never reviewed. OWASP's LLM06 names the three causes (excessive functionality, permissions, autonomy) and the fix, complete mediation in the downstream system.</p>
<p><b>The tool firewall.</b> The model chooses an order and an amount and states a reason. The code decides everything else:</p>
<p>1. Identity from the session token, never from the message. The order must belong to that customer.</p>
<p>2. Amount at most the refundable balance (order total minus prior refunds), currency from the order.</p>
<p>3. Payee is the original payment method, read from the record. There is no destination argument.</p>
<p>4. Eligibility rules in code: return window, delivery status from the carrier, item category. A reason that the record contradicts ("not received" on an order the carrier shows delivered) goes to a person.</p>
<p>5. An idempotency key per logical refund, derived from order and reason and reused across retries. The Agents SDK's documentation is explicit that approval does not make a call exactly-once, and the refund service rejects a reused key with different parameters, which catches a retry that changed the amount.</p>
<p>6. Caps the code enforces: per customer per day, and a global daily ceiling on automatic refunds that stops the tool and pages someone when crossed.</p>
<p><b>Choose the numbers.</b> The automatic threshold and the caps are yours to defend. With these inputs, $50 covers 5,600 refunds and $117,600 of refunded value a week, about $16,800 a day; a global automatic ceiling of 1.5 times that, about $25,000 a day, absorbs a normal peak and bounds a campaign to one day's ceiling. A threshold of $200 covers 92% of requests but lets a single bad call cost four times as much; $20 would cover too little to matter.</p>
<p><b>The answer to the slide.</b> Under $50: automatic, saving 5,600 × $7.20 = $40,320 a week. Abuse there would have to exceed 40,320 / 117,600 = 34% of the refunded value before automation lost money, so on expected value product is right. $50 to $200: the agent prepares the call and a person approves it in about a minute, 1,760 × $0.80 = $1,408 a week. Above $200: manual, 640 × $7.20 = $4,608. Total $6,016 a week against $57,600. The argument against the database write was never expected fraud; it was that its worst case (any row, any amount, no processor call) is unbounded, while the tool's worst case is one day's ceiling.</p>
</Fragment>

### Two defensible designs

**Automatic below a threshold, approval above it (recommended).** As above. Cost: the eligibility rules have to be built and owned, most of the two engineers' six weeks; refunds are instant for customers only below $50; and every rule is a place where policy and code can drift.

**The agent prepares every refund and a person approves every one.** No eligibility engine is needed, because a person judges eligibility. At a minute per approval, refunds up to $200 cost 7,360 × $0.80 = $5,888 and the manual band $4,608, $10,496 a week, still 82% below today. Cost: about 123 reviewer hours a week, customers wait for a queue, and a minute per decision is exactly the condition under which approvers stop reading; OWASP lists approval fatigue for that reason. This wins if the eligibility rules cannot be written down in six weeks, or if legal will not accept automatic refunds at all.

The assumption that decides between them is the approval time. Most of the $57,600 is spent gathering, not deciding; if a prepared approval really takes a minute, the human design is cheap and the engine buys only speed. If approvals settle at ten seconds, they are a rubber stamp and the human design is the automatic one with extra latency.

### The decision memo, written down

1. Weeks 1 to 2: the tool and its checks, in shadow mode. The agent proposes, humans decide as today; measure agreement between the proposal and the human decision by claim type.
2. Weeks 3 to 4: approval for $50 to $200 with the exact arguments, the order record and the refundable balance on the reviewer's screen. Automatic below $50 for 10% of customers.
3. Weeks 5 to 6: automatic below $50 for everyone if the triggers stay quiet.
4. Rollback triggers: automatic refunds per 1,000 conversations above 1.5 times the shadow-mode rate for a day; "not received" claims above their pre-launch share; any duplicate refund for one idempotency key; any refund above a cap (that one means a bug, not abuse).

**What you give up:** instant refunds above $50, refunds that need judgement the rules cannot express, and six weeks of two engineers.

### Where the recommended design fails

The tool bounds who is paid and how much, but the reason is a claim the model relays. A customer who learns that "never arrived" refunds instantly under $50 will say it on every order, and at chat speed the abuse that was $3,000 a week across human agents can grow before a weekly report shows it. The carrier check catches the claims the record contradicts; the per-customer cap and the claim-type trigger catch the rest late. Expect the first abuse wave to come from customers, not from injected documents.

## Follow-ups

- *The eligibility rules take the whole six weeks and product wants a launch in three. Which half do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants approval on every refund, the other wants automatic refunds up to $200. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *Six months in, the payments team offers a scoped database role that can only call a stored procedure with your checks inside it. Do you move the checks, and what changes in the failure modes?*
- *A reviewer approves 99.5% of prepared refunds in a median of eight seconds. Is the approval step working?*

## Common mistakes

- Granting the write and constraining it with the system prompt.
- Taking the customer or the order owner from the message rather than the session.
- Letting the model choose the destination of the money.
- Arguing with product's expected-loss numbers instead of agreeing with them and moving the argument to the worst case.
- Putting every refund behind approval and calling it safe, when approvers who see a hundred refunds an hour stop reading.
- Forgetting the idempotency key, so a timeout and a retry refund twice.
