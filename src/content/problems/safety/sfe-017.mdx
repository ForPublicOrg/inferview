## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported by a public interview guide as an Anthropic question">
"A deployed conversational model gives confident but factually wrong answers in high-risk contexts. How would you investigate and mitigate it?"
</Callout>

Treat "high-risk" as a domain where a wrong answer causes real harm, such as a benefits, legal, or clinical help assistant. The company attribution comes from a secondary interview guide; weigh it as a reported account.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Before fixing anything, where would you look? An average hides the slice that hurts.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>"Confident" is a calibration problem as much as an accuracy one.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>What is the order: measure, constrain, escalate?</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Investigate first: slice errors by domain and by topic to find where the confident-wrong answers cluster, and check calibration on those slices, not just accuracy, because the problem is high confidence on wrong answers. Then constrain: answer only from approved sources with a citation per claim, abstain when retrieval is empty or sources conflict, and raise the abstention threshold where a wrong answer is costly. Then escalate: route low-confidence and high-stakes questions to a person, and make the interface show a hedge as a hedge. Keep humans in the loop on the high-stakes path rather than trusting the model because it is usually right.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Investigate. Pull production traces and label a sample for correctness and confidence. Slice by domain, topic, user segment and whether retrieval found a source. The failure almost always concentrates: a particular topic, a region the documents do not cover, questions phrased a certain way. Measure calibration on the bad slice (does the 0.9-confidence bucket get 90% right there), because a model that is well calibrated overall can be badly calibrated on the slice that matters.</p>
<p>Constrain. Ground answers in approved sources and require a citation per claim; refuse to answer from the model's own knowledge in this domain. Abstain when retrieval is empty or the sources disagree. Set the abstention threshold from the cost of a wrong answer: with the penalty rule, a wrong answer that costs k times a right one means answer only above confidence k/(k+1). Add verification where it pays: a chain-of-verification pass or a tool (a dosage range check, a date calculation) that can catch a specific class of error. Remember a citation guarantees a valid pointer, not that the span supports the claim, so check support separately.</p>
<p>Escalate. Route the high-stakes, low-confidence slice to a human queue, and design the interface so a hedge reads as a hedge instead of being buried under a confident summary. Treat the model's output as something to be checked, not trusted, which is the habit that keeps oversight in place.</p>
<p>Report. Show error rate, coverage and human-queue load per slice, before and after, and let product set the operating point for low-stakes routes while policy fixes the high-stakes ones. Do not offer a single accuracy number as the safety story.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>The model is confidently wrong because a source document is wrong. Does abstention help?</em> No; that needs source ownership, versioning and freshness checks. Grounding faithfully repeats a wrong source.</li>
<li><em>How do you keep the human queue from being ignored under load?</em> Fix the high-stakes thresholds in policy, size the queue, and monitor its latency; a threshold that floods people gets quietly lowered.</li>
<li><em>What do you tell leadership about residual risk?</em> The error rate on the high-stakes slice at the chosen coverage, and that it is not zero, with the escalation path as the control for the rest.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *How is this different from general hallucination work?* The order is the same; the thresholds are stricter and the escalation path is mandatory because the cost of a wrong answer is high.
- *Would fine-tuning fix it?* It can raise accuracy on the slice, at the cost of a project and its own evals; measurement, grounding and abstention come first and are cheaper.

## Common mistakes

- Reporting one overall accuracy number and missing the slice where harm happens.
- Treating this as accuracy only and ignoring calibration.
- Grounding without abstention, so empty retrieval still produces a confident answer.
- Designing no human path for the high-stakes, low-confidence cases.
