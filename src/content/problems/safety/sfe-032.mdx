## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Behavioural, as a public interview guide phrases the core of it">
"Explain to a manager why we can't just 'delete' sensitive data from a trained AI model."
</Callout>

Answer as a story: a time you had to explain this upward, what you proposed instead, and what it cost. If you need a case, use this one (scenario). Your team fine-tuned an open-weights model on 18 months of support tickets to draft replies for agents; the fine-tune takes about 14 hours on 8 GPUs, then two days of evals and review before release. Two things arrive in one week: a customer's data protection officer sends an erasure request for their employees' personal data, which appears in that customer's tickets and is quoted in other customers' tickets; and security finds that some tickets contain API keys pasted by customers. Your manager says: "Delete those tickets from the model. Legal says we have a month." The product team relies on the drafting model every day.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask legal which date the month runs from, and whether they intend to use the extension.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request a count of tickets that mention the customer's employees by name, including tickets filed by other customers.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask security whether the leaked keys have been revoked, independently of anything done to the model.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>I told my manager there is no row to delete: training changed the weights, and the tickets now exist as a diffuse influence plus, for rare strings like names and keys, possibly verbatim text that a prompt can pull back out, which Carlini and colleagues showed on GPT-2 for sequences that appeared in a single document. Unlearning methods suppress outputs more than they remove influence, so the only removal we could stand behind was re-running our fine-tune from the base model on cleaned data, which is days, not the impossible "retrain from scratch" people picture. Within the week: keys revoked, an output filter on the named identifiers, an extraction test to measure what the model actually reproduces, and a retrain scheduled inside the legal deadline. Then the change that mattered: personal data out of training and into retrieval, where deletion is a delete.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The explanation, in the manager's terms.</strong> A fine-tuned model is not a database with the tickets in it; it is a set of weights that training adjusted until the model behaves like the tickets. The cake comparison in the source question captures that much and misses the part that matters here: rare strings can come back verbatim. A name, an email address or an API key that appeared once can be extracted with the right prompt, so "it is diffused" is not a safe claim about the data we care about. The EDPB's opinion makes the same test the legal one: whether personal data can be extracted through queries is part of deciding whether a model is anonymous, case by case.</p>
<p><strong>Why not unlearning.</strong> I looked, because "an algorithm deletes it" is what the manager wanted. TOFU's authors report that none of the baseline methods they tested unlearned effectively; Hu and colleagues brought "unlearned" knowledge back by fine-tuning on loosely related public text; Cooper, Lee and 36 co-authors argue that removing influence from parameters and suppressing outputs are different goals that current methods do not deliver together. An approximate unlearning run is an output filter with extra steps, and I was not willing to tell the data protection officer it was erasure.</p>
<p><strong>What I proposed instead.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>The keys: revoked and rotated by security the same day. That removes their harm whatever the model remembers, and it is the one step that does not depend on the model at all.</li>
<li>The model, this week: an output filter on the customer's employee names and email addresses and on key formats, in front of the drafting model; an extraction test that prompts with the opening lines of the affected tickets and counts verbatim continuations, so we know what the model reproduces rather than guessing.</li>
<li>The model, this month: re-run the fine-tune on the cleaned set. For a fine-tune, "retrain" means our 14 hours on 8 GPUs from the same base model, plus two days of evals, not pretraining.</li>
<li>The hard part was lineage, not compute: the employees' names appear in other customers' tickets, so removing the requesting customer's tickets is not enough. We searched the whole training set for the names and addresses before cleaning.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>The quantity I had to choose: the retrain cadence.</strong> Requests will keep coming, and Article 12(3) gives one month from receipt, extendable by two months only where complexity justifies it. If retrains run every N days and a release takes about 3 days (14 hours of training, two days of review), the worst case for a request that arrives just after a cut-off is N + 3 days. Monthly retrains give about 33 days, over the limit. Every two weeks gives 17 days, with margin for a failed eval. I chose two weeks: 26 runs a year at 112 GPU-hours each is 2,912 GPU-hours, against 1,344 for monthly. Weekly is defensible if requests are frequent; monthly is not, unless legal decides to rely on the extension, which is their call and not a plan.</p>
<p><strong>The two defensible positions, and the disagreement.</strong> Keep the model live with the filter until the retrain ships: the product keeps its drafts, and we accept that the filter misses paraphrase and partial strings for up to two weeks. Or take the drafting model offline until the retrain: no exposure, and the support team loses its drafts for the same period. The product lead wanted the first, security the second. I put the extraction test result in front of both: if the model reproduced none of the identifiers verbatim in the test, the filter covers the known form of the risk and the first is defensible; if it reproduced any, offline. In the scenario it reproduced two email addresses, so we took it offline for nine days, and the decision was written down with both names on it.</p>
<p><strong>The structural fix.</strong> Training on raw tickets made every future request a retrain. We moved to training on tickets scrubbed of names, addresses and secret formats, with customer-specific facts supplied at request time from a retrieval index, where an erasure is a delete and a re-index. SISA's idea of splitting training so a deletion retrains only one part applies here as one adapter per period; the paper reports 1.36x to 4.63x faster unlearning depending on the dataset, with a small accuracy cost on its ImageNet setup.</p>
<p><strong>Where this answer fails.</strong> Scrubbing before training misses identifiers in free text: a name in a signature block, a key inside a pasted log, a customer referred to by job title. The extraction test only tests the prompts we thought of, so a pass is evidence, not proof. And the base model's own pretraining data is outside all of this; if it memorised the same person from the public web, our retrain does not touch it.</p>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: retraining every two weeks costs more than the feature earns. Which half do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?* Keep the scrubbing and the retrieval move, and drop to retraining only when a request names data the lineage search finds in training; the written risk is the requests whose data the search misses.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see to rely on an unlearning method instead of a retrain?* The method passing a relearning attack of the kind Hu and colleagues ran, on our model and our data, and the data protection officer accepting that test as the standard; neither exists yet.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one says the scrubbed model is anonymous and erasure requests no longer apply to it, the other says it is not. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Run the extraction test against the scrubbed model with the identifiers from the last quarter's tickets and give the result to legal, whose call it is under the EDPB's case-by-case test.
- *The base model's vendor retires the version you fine-tune in sixty days. What happens to your deletion record?* The new fine-tune is built from the cleaned set, so the deletions carry over; the extraction test is re-run on the new model before it serves.

## Common mistakes

- Saying deletion is impossible and stopping there, with nothing proposed.
- Calling an unlearning run or an output filter erasure.
- Treating "retrain from scratch" as pretraining when the team owns only a fine-tune.
- Removing the requesting customer's tickets and missing the same people in other customers' tickets.
- Leaving leaked keys valid while debating the model.
- Deciding the legal question in engineering instead of handing legal a measurement.
