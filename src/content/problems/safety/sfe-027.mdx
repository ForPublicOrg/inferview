## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 20 minutes">
"We added spotlighting to the mail assistant: inbound mail is datamarked before the model sees it. The paper says it takes attack success from above 50 percent to below 2 percent. How many successful injections should we expect per day, and the team wants to close the injection ticket on this. Should it?"
</Callout>

Inputs:

- Published (Hines et al., arXiv 2403.14720, 2024-03-20): attack success fell from greater than 50 percent to below 2 percent in the authors' experiments with GPT-family models.
- Scenario, measured by the team: the assistant processes inbound mail for all tenants, and a hand-audited sample of last month's mail puts injection attempts at about 5,000 emails a day.
- Scenario: the assistant can summarise, label and draft; drafts render as Markdown, and the client loads remote images.

Choose the per-attempt success rate you plan for on this system and state why. Give successful injections per day, then answer the ticket question.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which model, which spotlighting mode and which attacks the authors measured, and how they compare with this assistant's.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask whether the 5,000 attempts come from many unrelated senders or a few senders retrying variants.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Request the list of things a successful injection can make the assistant do, and which of them leave the tenant.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Successful injections per day = attempts × per-attempt success. The abstract gives an upper bound, "below 2 percent", from the authors' own tests, so I plan on the bound: 5,000 × 0.02 = 100 a day. Planning on 0.5 percent gives 25. Either way it is tens a day, every day. The dominant term is the per-attempt rate, and the assumption that flips the conclusion is whether attackers adapt: against adaptive attacks, Nasr et al. took most of 12 defences from near zero to above 90 percent. So the ticket stays open. Spotlighting is a layer that lowers a rate; the control is deterministic and on the impact, like Microsoft's block of Markdown image exfiltration, which makes the number of leaks zero whatever the rate is.</p>
</Fragment>

<p>My choice is 2 percent, 100 a day: the abstract bounds the rate from above and does not say how far below 2 percent it fell for which model, so anything lower is precision the source does not give. Reasonable answers sit between about 20 and 110 a day.</p>
<p><strong>The dominant term and what flips it.</strong> The attempt count is measured; the per-attempt rate is borrowed from a different model, different attacks and a 2024 paper. If attackers adapt to the defence, the borrowed rate stops applying: Nasr et al. report adaptive attacks above 90 percent against most of 12 defences that had reported near-zero rates. The abstract does not say whether spotlighting was one of them, so I do not claim it; I plan as if it could be. At the "greater than 50 percent" undefended rate the same volume gives 5,000 × 0.5 = 2,500 a day. For comparison, a vendor-measured figure for a different system and attacker, Anthropic's browser agent with safeguards, is about 1 percent against an adaptive attacker, and Anthropic calls that meaningful risk.</p>
<p><strong>Why it is not the control.</strong> At any of these rates, injections succeed every day. What matters is what a success can do. Here a success can put a remote image in a draft, and the client loads it: a zero-click request carrying whatever the model put in the URL. A rule that renders no remote images in drafts, and no links outside an allowlist, makes the leak count zero at 100 successes a day or at 2,500. That is the shape of Microsoft's response to Markdown image exfiltration: block the technique deterministically, and do not rely on blocking every injection. Spotlighting stays, because 100 a day is better than 2,500, and because fewer successes means fewer bad summaries and drafts, which no render rule touches.</p>
<p><strong>Where this estimate fails.</strong> It assumes attempts are independent. A campaign that retries variants of one injection against the same tenant is not 5,000 coin flips; it is a search, and its success over a day is closer to "at least one" than to a rate. The 5,000 is from a hand-audited sample, so attempts that the auditors did not recognise are missing from it, and the count is a floor.</p>
</Fragment>

### Choices that are not reasonable

- Treating "below 2 percent" as roughly zero and closing the ticket: at 5,000 attempts even 0.5 percent is 25 a day.
- Multiplying by all inbound mail instead of by injection attempts, which counts benign mail as attacks.
- Reading the authors' bound as a measurement of this model with this prompt and these tools.
- Planning on a rate far below the bound, such as 0.01 percent, with no measurement on this system.

## Follow-ups

- *What changes in the number if the client stops loading remote images but the assistant can still send mail on its own?*
- *Product asks for the residual rate in the launch notes. What number do you publish, and with which conditions attached?*

## Common mistakes

- Quoting the paper's rate without its conditions: its models, its attacks, its date.
- Stopping at the count of successful injections without asking what one success can do.
- Treating a rate-lowering layer and a deterministic control as alternatives rather than layers.
