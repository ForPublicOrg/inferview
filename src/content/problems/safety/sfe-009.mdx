## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 40 minutes">
You have two weeks and one engineer, yourself, to red-team an LLM feature before a launch date that will not move. Scope the exercise, choose the attack families, define what counts as a finding, and say what blocks launch. Then write what you send the launch review.
</Callout>

The feature and its constraints (constructed for this problem):

- A customer-facing assistant answers product questions from the help centre, looks up the signed-in customer's orders, and opens support tickets on their behalf.
- The order tool calls the order service with an order id the model supplies. The order service belongs to another team and will not change before launch; the tool wrapper is yours.
- Tickets land in the support console, which renders Markdown, and an internal summariser reads every new ticket for the support team.
- The launch date is tied to a marketing event ten working days away.
- The product manager wants a pass or fail. The security lead wants no launch with any high-severity finding open.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask who wrote down what the assistant must never do, and whether security and product agree on that list.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask which systems read what the assistant writes, and who owns each of them.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask how many runs of the same attack the release review will accept as evidence that it no longer works.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Write the policy first, then spend the two weeks where the damage is: one day turning the cross-customer risk into a deterministic check in the tool wrapper, five days of manual adaptive attacks on ticket creation and everything downstream of a ticket, and two days of automated breadth with garak and promptfoo as a smoke run. A finding is a policy violation with the exact input, versions, tool calls, outcome and a success rate over repeated samples. Launch is blocked by any reproduced cross-customer exposure or unrequested ticket, and by lower-severity findings only above a stated rate. What the launch review gets is a memo listing the findings, the risks accepted with owners, and the triggers that turn the feature off.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Policy before attacks.</strong> The assistant must never return another customer's order data, never open a ticket the customer did not ask for or under another customer's name, never act on instructions found in help-centre articles or order fields, and never put content in a ticket that the console or the summariser will execute or obey. Each line gets an oracle that decides pass or fail from the transcript and the tool log, never from reading prose: a customer id that differs from the session's, a ticket with no matching request turn, a rendered link or image in a ticket body.</p>
<p><strong>The decision: depth or breadth.</strong> Both are defensible.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Breadth first.</strong> Most of the ten days on automated suites across every family in the OWASP list, with promptfoo generating attacks from the application's purpose and garak running its probe families at 10 generations per prompt. It produces a coverage table the product manager can read. Cost: static and generated attacks overstate a defence; The Attacker Moves Second took most of 12 defences that had scored near zero to above 90 percent with adaptive attacks. Triage of hundreds of generated hits also eats days.</li>
<li><strong>Depth first.</strong> Most of the days on manual, adaptive attacks against the two capabilities with real damage: the order lookup and ticket creation, including what happens after a ticket exists. Cost: role-play, many-shot and multi-turn attacks on the content policy get only a smoke run, and there is no coverage number to show.</li>
</ul>
<p>I would go depth first, because one of the two high-impact risks can be removed rather than tested. The wrapper is mine: it fetches the order, compares its customer id with the session's, and returns only the fields the policy allows. That turns "can the model be talked into another customer's order" into a unit test that passes or fails every time. The conflict is the deadline against what the remaining days can show; spending a day on engineering buys certainty on the highest-impact risk and costs a day of attacks.</p>
<p><strong>The plan, by day.</strong> Day 1: policy, oracles, a harness against staging. Day 2: the wrapper check and its tests. Days 3 to 7: adaptive attacks on ticket creation, then on the ticket's afterlife, because the summariser reading tickets is an indirect-injection path into an internal tool the attacker never touches directly. Days 8 and 9: garak (promptinject, encoding, leakreplay, xss) and a promptfoo run as the smoke layer; every hit gets triaged into the same finding format. Day 10: regression suite in CI, the memo.</p>
<p><strong>What counts as a finding.</strong> An input that makes the system violate a policy line, recorded with the input verbatim, the model, prompt and tool versions, the tool calls, the oracle's verdict, and the rate over repeated samples. A single success is not a rate: Best-of-N reports that a successful text jailbreak reproduced only about 30 percent of the time on resampling. I would run each case 30 times. That sees an attack with a 10 percent per-sample rate at least once with probability 0.96, and a 5 percent one with probability 0.79; to see a 5 percent attack with 95 percent probability takes 59 runs. Twenty to sixty runs per case is defensible; one run is not, and neither is a fixed number with no rate behind it.</p>
<p><strong>What blocks launch.</strong> Severity times rate, written down before the testing starts so nobody argues the threshold after seeing a result.</p>
<ul>
<li>Any reproduction of cross-customer data, or of a ticket opened without a request or under another customer: block, at any rate.</li>
<li>Content in a ticket that the console renders as a link or image, or that changes what the summariser reports: block, at any rate, because it reaches staff.</li>
<li>Wrong statements about refunds or policy above 3 of 30 runs: block; below that, an accepted risk with an owner and a date.</li>
<li>System prompt disclosure with no secrets in the prompt, off-brand or rude text: logged, not blocking.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>What a clean result means.</strong> Little. Zero hits in 30 runs puts the per-sample rate below about 10 percent with 95 percent confidence, for that case only. Anthropic's bug bounty on its classifier prototype ran more than 3,000 hours without a universal jailbreak, and its public demo then took about 3,700 hours from 339 people to find one; ten days is 80 hours. The memo says that the exercise found what it found, and that the launch relies on the wrapper, the policy oracles in production and the kill switch, not on the absence of findings.</p>
<p><strong>Where this fails.</strong> Depth first leaves the content-policy families shallow, so the likeliest post-launch incident is a screenshot of the assistant saying something it should not, found by multi-turn or many-shot attacks that only got the smoke run. The regression suite built in ten days is also small; it will stay green while attackers move to families it does not hold, so its pass rate is not evidence of safety after the first month.</p>
<p><strong>What you give up.</strong> A coverage number for the product manager. Depth on the content policy. A day of attack time for the wrapper. And, if the ticket path produces a high finding that the console team cannot fix in time, ticket creation itself at launch.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>The product manager says the wrapper change is engineering, not red-teaming, and asks you to spend the day attacking instead. What do you say, and what do you put in writing if you are overruled?</em></li>
<li><em>What would you need to see in the first thirty days to drop the rule that any reproduction of an unrequested ticket blocks?</em> A production oracle on ticket creation showing zero unrequested tickets over a volume that bounds the rate below the support team's tolerance, and a confirmation step whose bypass you have tested.</li>
<li><em>Two senior engineers disagree on whether a system-prompt leak blocks launch. How do you decide without deciding by rank?</em> By what the prompt contains: list it, and if nothing in it grants access or reveals another customer, the leak is a content issue with a logged rate.</li>
</ul>

### The written artefact

A launch memo to the launch review, one page.

- **Scope tested:** order lookup (wrapper check plus adaptive attacks), ticket creation and the console and summariser path, smoke run of garak probe families and a promptfoo run. Not tested in depth: multi-turn and many-shot attacks on the content policy.
- **Findings:** each with severity, rate over 30 runs, status (fixed with a regression case, or accepted).
- **Accepted risks:** each with an owner, a review date and the production signal that would reopen it.
- **Kill switches and rollback triggers:** a flag that disables ticket creation, and one that disables the order tool. Any confirmed cross-customer exposure in production disables the order tool within the hour. More than five wrapper denials for one session in a day pages security. Any ticket rendered with an external link or image disables ticket creation until the console escapes tool content.
- **What the result does not claim:** that the feature resists an attacker with more than 80 hours.

## Follow-ups

- *On day 8 you find that a crafted ticket makes the summariser tell the support team a customer is owed a refund. The console team cannot ship a fix for three weeks. Launch, slip, or launch without ticket creation, and who signs it?*
- *After launch, who owns the regression suite, and what makes it go stale first?*
- *The security lead asks why the 30-run threshold is 3 hits and not 1. Defend the number or change it.*

## Common mistakes

- Attacking the chat box and not the systems that read the assistant's output.
- Recording a single success as a finding with no rate, or a single failure as proof that an attack is fixed.
- Deciding launch thresholds after the results are in.
- Reporting a clean probe-suite run as evidence of safety rather than as evidence that those probes failed.
- Fixing a cross-customer finding in the prompt when the wrapper can check it in code.
