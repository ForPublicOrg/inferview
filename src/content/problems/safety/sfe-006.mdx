## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As a public interview guide phrases it">
"A prompt-injection classifier catches 95% of attempts in eval. Do you ship it?"
</Callout>

Before answering yes or no, estimate the number first: if each attempt independently gets past the classifier with probability 0.05, how many attempts does an attacker need for a better than even chance (over 0.5) that at least one gets through? Enter that count, then read the worked answer.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>The chance that all n attempts are caught is 0.95 to the power n.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Solve 1 minus 0.95 to the n is greater than 0.5, or just multiply 0.95 by itself until the product drops below 0.5.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>P(at least one gets through in n tries) = 1 - 0.95<sup>n</sup>. That passes 0.5 between 13 and 14 tries: at n = 13 it is 0.487, at n = 14 it is 0.512. So an attacker who can retry fourteen times is more likely than not to succeed once. A 95% classifier is a layer, not a decision to ship on its own. Ship it only with the architecture that makes one success survivable: the lethal trifecta broken, least-privilege tools, and output handling that cannot leak.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Worked calculation. Each attempt is caught with probability 0.95, so n independent attempts are all caught with probability 0.95<sup>n</sup>, and at least one succeeds with probability 1 - 0.95<sup>n</sup>. Compute: 0.95<sup>13</sup> = 0.513, so the success chance is 0.487; 0.95<sup>14</sup> = 0.488, so it is 0.512. The crossover is just under 14 attempts. For context, it passes 0.90 at 45 attempts and 0.95 at 59.</p>
<p>Independence is generous to the defender. A real attacker learns from each failure, and Best-of-N simply resamples input variations until one lands, so the per-attempt rate rises as they work. The Attacker Moves Second found that adaptive attacks bypassed most published defences more than 90% of the time, even though those defences scored near zero on static sets.</p>
<p>So the answer is: ship it as one layer, never as the control. Willison's line is that 95% is a failing grade in application security, where an escaping function is expected to be exact. The classifier lowers volume and gives you a signal to log and alert on. The thing that makes a single bypass non-catastrophic is structure: the session that reads untrusted text has no private data or no way out, tools are allowlisted and argument-checked, and secrets are not in the prompt.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>What if attempts are rate-limited to three per user per hour?</em> That raises the cost per attempt and is worth doing, but a zero-click indirect injection does not need a user account, and attackers rotate identities.</li>
<li><em>Where would you set the threshold?</em> From the false-positive cost on real traffic; a higher catch rate that blocks more real users may be worse overall.</li>
<li><em>Does a second, different classifier help?</em> Somewhat, if its errors are uncorrelated, which they usually are not for similar models on similar data.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

## Follow-ups

- *Your PM reads "95%" as nearly solved. How do you reframe it?* As a 5% per-attempt bypass that compounds with retries, and show the fourteen-attempt number.
- *What catch rate would be enough to ship alone?* None, for a security control. You design so that a bypass does limited damage, rather than chasing a rate.

## Common mistakes

- Reading 95% as a 5% total risk rather than a per-attempt rate.
- Assuming attempts stay independent; adaptive attackers improve.
- Shipping the classifier as the boundary and skipping the architecture.
