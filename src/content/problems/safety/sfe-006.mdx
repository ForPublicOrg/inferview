## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As a public interview guide phrases it">
"A prompt-injection classifier catches 95% of attempts in eval. Do you ship it?"
</Callout>

Answer it for this product (constructed for this problem). An email assistant reads every inbound email for business customers, summarises it, and drafts a reply using the customer's CRM notes. A person reviews and sends the draft. Summaries render as Markdown in the web client, images included.

- Inbound volume: 2,000,000 emails a day.
- The vendor reports 95 percent recall on its own benchmark.
- You ran the classifier in shadow mode for a week. It flagged 0.4 percent of emails. Your team labelled 500 flagged emails at random and found 12 real injection attempts.

Estimate how many injection emails get past the classifier each day, and decide whether to ship it and what a flag should do. The recall on your own mail has not been measured: choose it and defend the choice.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask how the 500 labelled emails were chosen, and what the labellers counted as an attempt.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask whether the vendor's benchmark contains attacks written against this classifier, and how old its newest attack is.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what an email that fools the assistant can make it do: which data it can read and which outputs leave the building.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The shadow week gives the caught attempts: 8,000 flagged a day, 2.4 percent of them real, so about 192 caught. With recall r on your mail, the misses are 192 × (1 − r) / r: 10 a day at the vendor's 95 percent, 48 at 80 percent. Ship it, as a routing signal rather than a gate: a flagged email gets a plain-text summary with no CRM access. What makes the 10 to 48 daily misses survivable is removing the way out: no remote images in rendered summaries, and no CRM text in drafts for senders the customer has never written to.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Caught per day.</strong> 2,000,000 × 0.004 = 8,000 flagged emails. Of the labelled sample, 12 of 500 were real, 2.4 percent, so about 192 attempts a day are caught. That is the measured part.</p>
<p><strong>Missed per day.</strong> If 192 is a fraction r of all attempts, the misses are 192 × (1 − r) / r.</p>
<ul>
<li>r = 0.95: 10 a day.</li>
<li>r = 0.90: 21 a day.</li>
<li>r = 0.80: 48 a day.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Choosing r.</strong> The vendor's 95 percent is a ceiling for your mail: it was measured on attacks the vendor had, and nothing in it was written against this classifier. The shadow week ran before launch, so most attempts were untargeted spray, the case closest to the benchmark. For that traffic, 0.8 to 0.95 is defensible, and I take 0.9, 21 a day. It is a launch-week number. Once attackers know the product, targeted attempts grow, and against adaptive attacks published defences have dropped from near zero to above 90 percent attack success, so recall on that slice can be close to zero.</p>
<p><strong>The other uncertainty is as large.</strong> Twelve of 500 has a 95 percent interval of about 1.4 to 4.1 percent, so the caught count is somewhere between 110 and 332 a day, and the misses at r = 0.9 between 12 and 37. The dominant term is still (1 − r) / r: moving r from 0.95 to 0.8 multiplies the misses by 4.75, and nothing has measured it.</p>
<p><strong>Measuring r.</strong> Random labelling will not find the misses. At 21 a day out of 2,000,000, about one email in 94,000 is a miss, so labelling 10,000 random emails finds about one tenth of one. Seed instead: plant known attack emails from each family into shadow traffic and count the catches. Catching 190 of 200 seeded attacks puts r at 0.95 with an interval of about 0.91 to 0.97, for the families you seeded and nothing else.</p>
<p><strong>The false positives decide what a flag does.</strong> 8,000 − 192 = 7,808 legitimate emails a day are flagged. Withholding them from the assistant breaks the product for that many messages a day, so a flag downgrades instead: plain-text summary, no CRM lookup, no draft. That is the conflict here, recall against usefulness, and a stricter threshold makes both numbers larger.</p>
<p><strong>Ship, as a layer.</strong> Willison's line applies: "in web application security 95% is very much a failing grade." The assistant has all three legs of his trifecta: private data (CRM notes), untrusted content (every inbound email) and a way out (rendered Markdown images fetch any URL, and drafts go back to the sender). The classifier cuts volume and gives an alert signal. The controls that bound the 10 to 48 daily misses are structural: summaries render no remote images or links, and drafts to a sender the customer has never written to carry no CRM text.</p>
<p><strong>Where this fails.</strong> The draft is still a way out. An email that gets past the classifier, from a sender the customer has written to before, can ask for a reply that quotes the CRM notes, and the only check is a busy person clicking send. The person reviewing is the last control, and the residual count is the number of times a day you are relying on them.</p>
<p><strong>The assumption that flips the conclusion</strong> is the way out. With no external channel, 48 misses a day produce 48 misleading summaries, an annoyance. With a channel that sends without a person, even one a day is a data-breach rate, and the classifier's recall is not the number to argue about.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Your PM reads 95 percent as nearly solved. What do you show them?</em> The 10 to 48 misses a day at your volume, and the list of what each miss can reach.</li>
<li><em>Security wants the flagged emails blocked outright. What does that cost, and in which number?</em> 7,808 legitimate emails a day without the assistant, and support tickets about missing summaries.</li>
<li><em>A second vendor's classifier also claims 95 percent. Does running both halve the misses?</em> Only if their misses are uncorrelated; compare them on the same seeded attacks with paired outcomes before counting on it.</li>
</ul>

### Choices that are not reasonable

- An r above 0.95. The vendor's benchmark is the best case for this classifier, and your mail is not easier than it.
- Applying 5 percent to all traffic: 100,000 a day treats every email as an attack.
- Treating the 0.4 percent flag rate as the attack rate: 8,000 attempts a day with 400 missed ignores that 97.6 percent of the flags are legitimate mail.

## Follow-ups

- *Three months after launch the flag rate doubles and the labelled precision rises to 8 percent. What changed, and what does it do to your estimate of the misses?*
- *Legal asks for a single number for the risk register. Which one do you give, with which interval, and what do you write next to it?*

## Common mistakes

- Reading 95 percent as a 5 percent total risk instead of a per-email miss rate on a population you have to estimate.
- Labelling random traffic to find misses, which needs hundreds of thousands of labels per miss.
- Gating on the classifier and keeping the Markdown image channel, so each miss can still send data out.
