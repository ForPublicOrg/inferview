## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 30 minutes">
Your support assistant can issue a refund of up to $200 per conversation when it judges the customer eligible. A jailbreak classifier screens every customer message before the model sees it. Estimate how many attempts an attacker needs for a 90 percent chance that one jailbreak gets past the classifier, say what that count means for the rate limit, and decide where the fix belongs: the classifier, the rate limit or the refund tool.
</Callout>

Scenario inputs (constructed for this problem):

- The classifier catches 1,980 of the 2,000 prompts in your red-team set, which was built from public jailbreak families and then frozen.
- It blocks 0.5 percent of legitimate customer messages.
- A blocked message gets the same fixed reply every time, and the customer can send another.
- The rate limit is 50 messages per hour per account. An account needs one completed order; the cheapest item costs $5.
- About 40,000 accounts open a support conversation on a busy day; count a long legitimate conversation as 20 messages.
- For this estimate, a jailbreak that passes the classifier gets the refund.

Published inputs, each with its conditions. Best-of-N (December 2024) reports GPT-4o's own refusals giving way on 50 percent of 159 harmful requests at 100 augmented samples, 56 percent at 500 and 89 percent at 10,000. The Attacker Moves Second (October 2025) reports adaptive attacks above 90 percent success against most of 12 published defences that had reported near zero. Anthropic (November 2025, its own measurement on browser-use injections) reports 1.4 percent attack success for Claude Opus 4.5 with its improved safeguards against an internal attacker given 100 attempts per environment.

What you do not have is the bypass rate per attempt that a motivated attacker gets against this classifier. Choose it and defend it, then give the attempts for a 90 percent chance of one bypass.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>With a per-attempt bypass rate p, the attempts for a 90 percent chance of one bypass are ln 0.1 / ln(1 − p), about 2.3 / p. I take p = 2 percent, which gives 114 attempts: under three hours on one account at 50 messages an hour, for a $200 payout on a $5 account. No rate limit a real customer tolerates changes that, and a lockout after three blocks still leaves the attack profitable above p = 0.84 percent. So the fix belongs in the refund tool: code decides eligibility from order state, the model proposes. The classifier and a lockout stay, as a signal and as a cost to the attacker.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The count.</strong> Treating attempts as independent, at least one of n attempts gets through with probability 1 − (1 − p)<sup>n</sup>. Setting that to 0.9 gives n = ln 0.1 / ln(1 − p), which is close to 2.3 / p for small p. The dominant term is p: the count scales with its inverse, and nothing else in the estimate moves it as much.</p>
<ul>
<li>p = 1 percent: 230 attempts (69 for an even chance).</li>
<li>p = 2 percent: 114 attempts (35 for an even chance).</li>
<li>p = 5 percent: 45 attempts (14 for an even chance).</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Choosing p.</strong> One percent is the floor, not the estimate. The 20 prompts the classifier misses come from public families the attacker can read too, and the fixed block reply tells them after every message whether it was blocked, which is the feedback an adaptive search needs. The ceiling is set by what adaptive attacks have done to defences measured the way this one was: The Attacker Moves Second took most of 12 defences from near zero to above 90 percent. The best vendor number on the page, Anthropic's 1.4 percent with an adaptive attacker, is a vendor's own measurement on a different task, with safeguards built for that task, and the vendor still calls 1 percent meaningful risk. I take 2 percent; anything from 1 to 5 percent is defensible for an attacker who iterates on a fixed reply.</p>
<p><strong>Independence is wrong in both directions.</strong> Best-of-N gives a check. If GPT-4o's 50 percent at 100 samples came from the same p on every request, p = 1 − 0.5<sup>1/100</sup> = 0.69 percent, which predicts 97 percent at 500 samples and effectively 100 percent at 10,000. The paper reports 56 and 89 percent. Requests differ: some fall within a few dozen samples, and some survive 10,000. This attacker has one goal, this refund, so their p is either well above the average or close to zero, and nobody knows which until someone tries. The estimate cannot settle that, which is why it ends in the architecture and not in a better number.</p>
<p><strong>The rate limit.</strong> At 50 messages an hour, 114 attempts take 2.3 hours on one account; 230 attempts take 4.6 hours. Cutting the limit to 10 an hour stretches 114 attempts to 11.4 hours, one evening, for $200. Add a lockout after three blocked messages in a day. An account then gets about three attempts, so it yields a bypass with probability 1 − (1 − p)<sup>3</sup>: 5.9 percent at p = 2 percent, or one success per 17 accounts, $85 of $5 orders for a $200 refund. The attacker profits whenever 200 × (1 − (1 − p)<sup>3</sup>) &gt; 5, which holds for any p above 0.84 percent. The lockout is cheap for real customers: at 0.5 percent false blocks, a 20-message conversation hits three blocks with probability 1.3 × 10<sup>−4</sup>, about 5 locked accounts on a 40,000-account day. Keep it; it does not close the hole.</p>
<p><strong>The decision has two defensible answers.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Keep the model deciding, and change the payout.</strong> Lockout, then cap automatic refunds at $50 per account per month and send anything above to a person. With the $50 cap the attacker needs 50 × (1 − (1 − p)<sup>3</sup>) &gt; 5, so p above 3.45 percent, inside the band I called defensible. Cost: refund abuse becomes a fraud-operations line measured in dollars, and the human queue grows by every refund above the cap.</li>
<li><strong>Move eligibility into code.</strong> The refund tool checks delivery state, the return window and earlier refunds on that order, and the model only supplies the reason; anything outside the rules goes to a person. A jailbreak now buys text, not money. Cost: the model loses the discretion the feature was built for (the goodwill refund on a late order), and the engineering time to write the policy down.</li>
</ul>
<p>I would move eligibility into code for the money path and keep the classifier and the lockout in front of it. The conflict is autonomy against safety: every refund the model may grant on judgement is a refund a jailbreak can grant. Tightening the classifier instead trades cost against recall, since a stricter threshold blocks more real customers; ask for its curve before promising that.</p>
<p><strong>Where the recommendation fails.</strong> Rules in code move the attack to whatever the rules trust. "Item not received" is self-reported, so a rule that approves non-delivery claims inside the window is abused without any jailbreak, and the model's judgement of whether a claim was plausible was doing work that the rule does not. Expect refund abuse to shift from prompts to claims, and watch the rate of non-delivery claims per account after the change.</p>
<p><strong>The assumption that flips the conclusion</strong> is that a bypass pays. If the refund tool can only grant what code already allows, the classifier's catch rate stops mattering for this risk, and the 99 percent becomes a question about other harms. If accounts cost more than one success is worth, the rate limit and lockout start to matter again.</p>
</Fragment>

<ul>
<li><em>Finance says writing the refund policy as code takes the quarter and launch is in three weeks. Which refunds does the model keep deciding at launch, and what loss do you accept in writing?</em></li>
<li><em>What would you need to see in production to let the model grant refunds above $50 on its own judgement again?</em> A bypass rate measured with adaptive attacks against the live classifier whose upper bound keeps an account's expected payout below its cost, and refund losses inside the fraud budget for a quarter.</li>
<li><em>Two senior engineers disagree: one wants a second classifier, the other wants the lockout at one block. How do you decide without deciding by rank?</em> Price both: the second classifier lowers p only as far as its misses are uncorrelated with the first one's, which is unlikely for similar models trained on similar data; the one-block lockout cuts attempts per account to one and raises false lockouts, which the 0.5 percent block rate lets you compute.</li>
</ul>

### Choices that are not reasonable

- A p below 1 percent. The frozen red-team set is the floor: its misses are public prompts, and an attacker who tries those first does at least as well.
- The Attacker Moves Second's 90 percent as a per-attempt rate. It is the success of an optimisation campaign that already spent its attempts; plugging it in gives one attempt and hides the search the attacker paid for.
- Best-of-N's 0.69 percent per sample as this classifier's p. It measures a model's own refusals on harmful requests, not your classifier on your refund attack.
- Reading 99 percent as one attacker in a hundred succeeding. It is a per-message rate, and the attacker sends many messages.

## Follow-ups

- *The refund conversation also reads the delivery notes on the order, which the customer writes at checkout. How does an injection there change the attempt count, and who pays for each attempt?*
- *The lockout message tells the attacker when they are locked. Does hiding it change p, the attempts per account, or neither?*
- *Your classifier vendor ships a new version that scores 99.6 percent on your frozen set. What do you measure before you change the lockout or the refund cap?*

## Common mistakes

- Quoting the red-team set's catch rate as the attacker's experience, when the set was frozen and the attacker is not.
- Using the rate limit as the control. It turns attempts into hours, and hours are cheap against a $200 payout.
- Treating attempts as independent without saying so; the Best-of-N numbers show some goals fall quickly and others hardly at all.
- Fixing the classifier while the tool still pays out on the model's judgement.
