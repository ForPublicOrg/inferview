## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported question">
A candidate report aggregated by landedjobs lists "What would you build at OpenAI in the first 90 days?" in the hiring-manager round for an AI Engineer role. The report gives no follow-ups. The version below is general: it applies to any staff role on an AI team, and nothing in the answer depends on knowing one company's internals.
</Callout>

The hiring manager asks what you would build in your first 90 days on their team. You know the team's public products and what came up in earlier rounds; you do not know its internal backlog. Answer in five minutes, then take pushback on the plan.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask the hiring manager what the team shipped in the last quarter and what it missed.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask who on the team is waiting on something they cannot build themselves.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask how the team decides whether a model or prompt change is ready to ship today.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A plan in three parts, each with something shipped. Days 1 to 30: learn by shipping a small fix on the team's hardest path and by reading a month of incidents and evals, ending with a written list of the three gaps that cost the team most. Days 31 to 60: build the smallest piece of the largest gap, chosen with the manager, usually the measurement the team lacks for a decision it makes weekly. Days 61 to 90: put it in the release path for one real change and show the decision it changed. What I would not do: a rewrite, a new framework, or a platform nobody has asked for. The risk in the plan is that my gap list is wrong, which is why the bet is named at day 30, not today.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Two defensible shapes.</b> <em>Arrive with a bet:</em> name a specific thing to build from what you know of the product and the earlier rounds, and say what would change your mind. It shows judgement and gives the manager something to push against; it costs credibility if the bet rests on a wrong guess about internals. <em>Arrive with a method:</em> describe how you would find the right thing, with dated outputs. It is safe and honest; it costs specificity, and a plan with no bet can sound like a process with no opinion. The strong version combines them: a method with dated deliverables and one named hypothesis for what it will find, labelled as a hypothesis.</p>
<p><b>The quantity to choose.</b> How much of the 90 days goes to listening before building. Under a third (under 30 days) risks building the wrong thing; over half leaves too little time to ship anything with a result by day 90. Somewhere between three and six weeks is defensible, and the answer states which and why.</p>
<p><b>The constraint conflict.</b> Showing impact quickly against building the right thing. A day-90 deliverable that changed one real decision is worth more than three that changed none.</p>
</Fragment>

<li><em>I think your hypothesis is wrong; our gap is serving cost, not evals. What would you need to see in the first month to agree with me?</em></li>

### A worked plan, labelled as a hypothesis

The following assumes a team that ships model-backed features to customers and decides on prompt and model changes most weeks. It is an example of the shape, not a claim about any company.

**Days 1 to 30.** Ship one small fix through the team's normal release path, end to end, to learn the path by using it. Read the last quarter's incidents and rollbacks and, for each, what evidence existed before the release. Inventory every model call the team makes: model and version, prompt version, who owns it, whether it is in change management. (The CSA note's warning about AI tools outside change management falling out of audit scope is the kind of gap this finds.) Check the vendor deprecation calendar against the inventory; a dependency such as fine-tuning on a vendor whose new jobs end on a set date (OpenAI's is 6 January 2027) is a gap with a deadline. Output: a one-page gap list ranked by cost, reviewed with the manager.

**Days 31 to 60.** Hypothesis: the largest gap is that release decisions rest on evals that are not paired against production or on judges never compared with human labels. Build the smallest fix: a paired run for the team's most-changed feature, with a small human-labelled set to check the judge. Output: the runner, the labelled set, and a report on the last three changes run retrospectively.

**Days 61 to 90.** Put the paired run in the release path for that feature, run it on a real change, and record the decision it informed. Output: one decision changed or confirmed with evidence, and a proposal for the next quarter.

**Not in 90 days.** A platform for other teams, a new orchestration framework, a migration of the team's stack. Each might be right later; none is justified by what can be known in a month.

### The failure mode

The plan assumes the manager will give the first month for learning. A team in the middle of a launch will not, and the plan then starts at day 31 with a hypothesis that was never checked. Say that in the answer and offer the alternative: join the launch, and run the inventory alongside it.

## Follow-ups

- *Your day-30 inventory finds a model call in production with no owner. What do you do in week five, and whose permission do you need?*
- *The manager wants a demo at day 45 for leadership. How does that change the plan, and what do you refuse to show?*
- *At day 90 your runner exists and nobody else on the team uses it. What do you do in the next 30 days?*

## Common mistakes

- Naming a specific product to build for the company with confidence about internals the candidate cannot know.
- A plan made only of listening, with no deliverable before day 90.
- A plan made only of building, starting on day 1 with no way to find out it is the wrong thing.
- No statement of what the plan will not do.
- Deliverables measured by what was built rather than by a decision it changed.
