## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design, 45 minutes">
"Thirty product teams keep their prompts in one Langfuse organisation. Today anyone with the member role can move a production label, and last quarter that caused two customer-facing incidents. Design who may move production, for which prompts, and how. I want a one-page policy I can send to the team leads on Monday."
</Callout>

The organisation is a scenario. What you have:

- About 400 prompts across 30 projects. Some are internal (ticket summaries for staff), most are customer-facing (support replies, onboarding emails), and twelve generate collection messages to customers in arrears, which compliance must approve, change by change, with a record of who approved what.
- Langfuse Cloud on the Core plan, where protected labels are not available. Moving to Pro with the Teams add-on needs a budget request that takes a quarter.
- A platform team of two engineers.
- Product managers edit prompts in the UI and ship the same day, and their director has said in writing that a two-day process for a wording change is not acceptable.
- Generations are not linked to prompt versions in most projects.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask the compliance team what record they accept for an approval, and whether a rollback to an approved version counts as a change.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how many of last quarter's incidents came from internal, customer-facing and regulated prompts.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask which projects' SDK clients override the cache TTL.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Tier the prompts by blast radius and make the rule for moving production depend on the tier, not the role: internal prompts stay self-serve; customer-facing prompts reach production only through a release job that runs a paired evaluation and records the reviewer; the twelve regulated prompts add a named compliance approval to that record. Rollback to the version that was production before is always allowed, for anyone on call, with no approval. Until protected labels are bought, the app reads a label only the release job writes, so the member role cannot ship by accident. What we give up: same-day shipping for customer-facing wording, unless the change passes the fast path.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Tiers.</b> Tier 0, internal: anyone in the owning team moves production; the only requirement is that generations are linked to the prompt version. Tier 1, customer-facing: production moves only through a release job after a paired evaluation against the current version, with one reviewer from the owning team. Tier 2, the twelve collection prompts: tier 1 plus a compliance approver whose name and the approved version number go into the change record. The tier is a tag on the prompt and the release job refuses a prompt without one.</p>
<p><b>Enforcement without protected labels.</b> On Core, members can move any label, so the policy cannot rest on the production label alone. Two defensible answers.</p>
<ul>
<li><i>Indirection now.</i> Tier 1 and 2 apps fetch a label named <code>released</code> instead of <code>production</code>, and only the release job writes it. PMs keep moving <code>production</code> in the UI for previews and evaluations, and nothing customer-facing reads it. Cost: a naming convention that a new service can ignore by fetching the default label, so the release job also has to scan service configs for tier 1 and 2 prompts fetched without an explicit label. It ships in two weeks with two engineers.</li>
<li><i>Buy protected labels.</i> Protect <code>production</code>, keep PMs on the member role, and limit the admin role to the platform team that runs the release job. Cost: a quarter of waiting, a plan upgrade, and a coarse control: every admin can still move the label, so the approval record still lives in the release job.</li>
</ul>
<p>I would do the first now and file the budget request for the second, because the second hardens the same design rather than replacing it.</p>
<p><b>The fast path.</b> The director's constraint is real: a two-day process will be bypassed by copying prompts into code. A tier 1 change ships the same day when the paired evaluation shows no loss on any tagged slice beyond its threshold and the reviewer approves in the release tool. Measure the path: if its median time from edit to production goes above a day, the policy is failing, whatever the incident count says.</p>
<p><b>Rollback.</b> Moving <code>released</code> back to the previous released version needs no approval, in any tier, and the release job records it after the fact. For tier 2 this needs compliance's agreement in advance that the previous approved version is pre-approved as a rollback target; get that in writing before the policy goes out. Rollback takes effect at each process's next cache refresh, so the policy fixes the TTL for tier 1 and 2 clients at the 60-second default and the release job rejects services that override it.</p>
<p><b>Audit.</b> Every generation in tier 1 and 2 links to its prompt version, which also gives per-version cost, latency and scores for the paired evaluation's follow-up. The change record holds the version, the evaluation result, the reviewer, and for tier 2 the approver. Prompt changes and model changes go through the release job separately, so each record has one cause.</p>
<p><b>The written page.</b> A table of tiers with who may move <code>released</code>, the evaluation required, the approver and the rollback rule; the date protected labels are requested; and the two numbers that will be reviewed in a quarter: incidents by tier and median edit-to-production time on the fast path.</p>
<p><b>What you give up.</b> PM autonomy for customer-facing prompts; a release job that becomes a dependency of thirty teams and has two maintainers; and the illusion that the registry's role model is the control. The control is the release job.</p>
<p><b>How the design fails.</b> The release job's evaluation needs a labelled set per tier 1 prompt, and most of the 400 prompts have none. Teams will tag prompts as tier 0 to skip the job. Make the platform team, not the owning team, assign tier 1 and 2 by what the prompt's output reaches, and audit tier 0 tags against the traces once a month: a tier 0 prompt whose generations end in customer-facing traces is mis-tiered.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li>Pushback: the release job is down during an incident and on-call needs to roll back a tier 2 prompt. What do they do, and what does compliance see afterwards?</li>
<li>Reversal: what would the quarterly numbers have to show for you to make tier 1 self-serve again?</li>
<li>Operating: a model is retired in sixty days and 40 of the 400 prompts regress on its replacement, three of them tier 2. Which ship degraded, and who signs off?</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the PM director says the evaluation gate blocked a typo fix for four hours last week. Which changes skip the evaluation entirely, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *Reversal: the budget request is approved. What changes in the design, and what stays exactly as it is?*
- *Two staff engineers disagree: one wants prompts in git with code review as the only path, the other wants the registry UI as the source of truth. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Write down which incidents each would have stopped last quarter, and what each costs the teams that edit prompts every day.

## Common mistakes

- Designing approvals for the forward path and forgetting that rollback must be faster than any approval.
- Treating the registry's role model as the control, when on Core every member can move every label.
- One policy for all 400 prompts, which makes internal summaries as slow to change as collection letters.
- Ignoring the client-side cache, so a governed rollback still takes as long as the longest TTL in the fleet.
- Leaving tier assignment to the teams that benefit from a lower tier.
