## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 30 minutes">
"We want to know whether the new model lifts task resolution from 70% to 72% on the billing assistant. How many conversations do we need, what share of customers goes on the new model, and how many days does the test run?"
</Callout>

The setup, all of it scenario:

- A support assistant on the billing queue. A conversation counts as resolved when it closes without a handoff to a person and the customer does not come back about the same issue within 7 days. The rate over the last eight weeks is 70.0%.
- Product says a lift of 2 points pays for the candidate model's higher price, and nothing smaller would.
- Customers are assigned by a stable hash of the user id. The metric is computed per conversation.
- The billing queue gets 2,400 conversations a day averaged over a week; weekend days carry about half the volume of weekdays.
- From last month's logs: a customer active in any 14-day window had 2.0 billing conversations in it on average, and the intra-customer correlation of the resolved flag was 0.20.
- Two-sided alpha 0.05, power 0.8.
- The release freeze starts in 30 days, and the decision has to be made before it.
- The support lead wants as few customers as possible on an untested model.

Give the sample size per arm in conversations, the treatment share you choose, and the duration in days. Say which number moves the answer most.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask how many conversations one customer has during the window you plan to run, and how similar their outcomes are to each other.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask whether volume and resolution differ by day of week before you settle on a number of days.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask product whether they would act on a 2-point loss as readily as on a 2-point gain.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Treating conversations as independent, detecting 70% to 72% at two-sided alpha 0.05 and power 0.8 needs 8,077 conversations per arm. Customers are the unit of assignment and have 2.0 conversations each with correlation 0.20, so multiply by a design effect of 1 + (2.0 − 1) × 0.20 = 1.2: 9,693 per arm. At 50/50 that is 19,385 conversations, 8.1 days of traffic, which I round up to 14 days so both arms see every weekday twice. Once the test runs 14 days anyway, the treatment share can drop to 20% at no cost in calendar time: 29,916 conversations, 12.5 days. My answer: 20% treatment for 14 days. The dominant term is the minimum effect, which enters squared; product asking for 1 point instead of 2 needs four times the sample and misses the freeze.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Independent conversations first.</strong> For two proportions with unpooled variances, n per arm = (z<sub>α/2</sub> + z<sub>β</sub>)² × (p₀q₀ + p₁q₁) / δ². With z<sub>0.975</sub> = 1.960 and z<sub>0.80</sub> = 0.842 the multiplier is 2.802² = 7.849. The variances are 0.70 × 0.30 = 0.2100 and 0.72 × 0.28 = 0.2016. So n = 7.849 × 0.4116 / 0.02² = 8,076.5, rounded up to 8,077. A pooled-variance formula gives 8,080; either is fine.</p>
<p><strong>The design effect.</strong> Assignment is by customer, the metric is per conversation, and two conversations from one customer are not two independent draws. Miller's clustered standard error adds the within-cluster cross terms to the naive variance; with clusters of equal size m and intra-cluster correlation ρ the variance grows by 1 + (m − 1)ρ = 1 + 1.0 × 0.20 = 1.2. Per arm: 8,077 × 1.2 = 9,693 conversations. Uneven cluster sizes push the factor higher, so 1.2 is a floor.</p>
<p><strong>Allocation, the choice that is yours.</strong> With a treatment share f, the total is N = 7.849 × (0.2100 / (1 − f) + 0.2016 / f) / 0.0004 × 1.2.</p>

<p>Any share above about 18% finishes inside two weeks. Rounding to whole weeks is a choice too: an 8-day test counts one weekday twice and the rest once, and if weekend customers resolve differently the estimate carries that imbalance.</p>
<p><strong>Two defensible answers.</strong> 50/50 for 14 days buys more power than the brief asks for, which helps if the true effect is a little under 2 points, and costs half the customer base on an untested model for two weeks. 20% for 14 days meets the stated effect, matches the support lead's constraint, and gives up the margin: a true lift of 1.8 points will often come back inconclusive. 10% for 28 days protects customers further and leaves two days before the freeze for analysis, a schedule nobody should sign. I would run 20% for 14 days and write in the plan that the test is powered for 2 points and no smaller.</p>
<p><strong>Dominant term and the assumption that flips it.</strong> δ enters squared. At 1 point, n per arm is 32,643 before the design effect, and the 50/50 test needs 32.6 days, past the freeze at any share. At 3 points the 50/50 test needs 3.6 days. The design effect comes second: it was measured over 14 days, and a 28-day test gives each customer more conversations, so m and the factor grow with the window.</p>
<p><strong>Where the recommendation fails.</strong> The metric counts "closed without a handoff". A candidate that talks customers out of asking for a person scores higher and is worse, and the test as specified would call it a win. Add guardrails before launch: handoff requests that were not honoured, 7-day recontact on its own, and cost per conversation. The 14 days are also a commitment: the dashboard may cross significance on day 6, and stopping there inflates the false-positive rate the alpha was chosen to bound. Check the arm counts for sample ratio mismatch before reading any outcome.</p>

### Choices that are not reasonable

- Ignoring the design effect: 16,153 conversations at 50/50, 6.7 days, then rounding down to a week. The interval it reports is too narrow by the square root of the design effect.
- Switching to a one-sided test to save a fifth of the sample (6,362 per arm) while still planning to roll back on a loss. A one-sided test says nothing about the side you dropped.
- Running 10% treatment for exactly 22 days. It ends mid-week and leaves no slack before the freeze for a sample ratio check, guardrails or a rerun.
- Planning to check daily and stop at the first significant day.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the support lead says 20% is still too many customers on an untested model and asks for 5%. Compute what that costs in days, then say which constraint you give up in writing.*
- *Reversal: at the end of 14 days the lift is +1.4 points with an interval from −0.3 to +3.1. What would you need to see to extend the test rather than call it, and who agrees to the extension before anyone looks?*
- *Operating: the control model receives a retirement notice the week the test starts; Anthropic's floor is 60 days' notice. Does the test still answer the question you need answered, and what do you run instead?*

## Common mistakes

- Using customers as the unit of assignment and treating their conversations as independent samples in the arithmetic.
- Treating the result as fixed by the formula when the allocation, the week boundary and the minimum effect are all choices.
- Accepting "no handoff" as the primary metric without a guardrail on handoffs that customers asked for.
- Quoting a duration without a stopping rule, which lets the first significant day end the test.
