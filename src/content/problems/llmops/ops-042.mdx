## Question

At an invented company, a customer-facing assistant gave wrong fee information for two days, and the risk function drafted the policy below. It goes to the executive committee next week, and the head of engineering asks you to review it first. List every flaw you find, ranked by how much it matters, before opening the checklist.

```text
AI Change Governance Policy, v0.9 (draft)

1. Scope. This policy applies to all changes to production prompts
   managed in the prompt registry, for every AI feature.

2. AI Change Review Board. A board of five (risk, legal, security,
   product, engineering) meets every Tuesday. Each prompt change is
   presented by its author with the before-and-after prompt text.

3. Approval. A change is approved when a majority of the board
   votes in favour. Approvals are recorded in the meeting minutes
   with the change ticket number.

4. Deployment. After approval, the author moves the production label
   to the approved version. Changes not approved may be resubmitted
   the following week.

5. Emergencies. During an incident, a rollback to a prior version
   requires approval from at least three board members, who may be
   contacted by phone.

6. Exclusions. Prompts embedded in application code are covered by
   the existing code review process and are out of scope. Changes to
   model parameters (temperature, max tokens) are out of scope.

7. Model changes. Adoption of a new model version is approved by the
   engineering lead of the owning team.

8. Review. This policy will be reviewed annually.
```

Context you may ask about: the company has fourteen AI features, of which four reach customers and one drafts messages that go to customers after an agent's review. Teams changed production prompts about thirty times last month. The fee incident was caused by a retrieval index that kept a superseded fee table; the prompt had not changed.

## Answer

The policy would not have caught the incident that prompted it. The fee error came from a retrieval index, and section 1 scopes the policy to prompts in the registry; section 6 removes prompts in code and model parameters; section 7 hands model versions to one engineering lead. The changes most likely to alter behaviour are the ones the board never sees, while thirty prompt edits a month, most of them on internal features, go to a board of five.

### The flaws a first read catches

**One review for every change.** Fourteen features, four customer-facing, all treated alike. With about thirty changes a month, the board spends most of its time on internal tools and gives the customer-facing drafting feature the same few minutes.

**Rollback needs three board members.** Section 5 makes the fastest safe action in an incident (moving the label back to a version that was already approved) wait for phone approvals. A previously approved version has already passed its review; rolling back to it should be pre-approved, and rolling forward during an incident is the decision that needs a person.

**Prompts in code are exempt.** Section 6 gives teams a documented route around the board: move the prompt into code, where an engineer on the same team approves it. Expect that within a month, and expect the registry, which is where the versions and audit trail live, to empty out.

### The flaws a senior reviewer misses on first read

**Approvals are not tied to what runs.** Section 3 records approval in minutes keyed by a ticket number. Registry versions are immutable and a deploy is a label move, so the evidence an auditor reportedly wants (the approval that promoted this exact version, a change record showing it was tested and reversible) needs the approval attached to the version id. Minutes plus a ticket cannot prove that the version in production is the one the board saw, and section 4 lets the author move the label to any version.

**The board reviews text, not behaviour.** Section 2 presents a before-and-after prompt. No eval result, no slice comparison, no rollout plan or rollback trigger. Five people reading a prompt diff cannot see what it does to fee questions; a paired eval can.

**Model versions are the riskiest change and have the lightest gate.** Section 7 gives a new model version to one lead with no evidence requirement, and nothing covers a vendor updating a model behind an alias.

**No exception path.** No waiver, no owner, no expiry. A policy without one is followed until the first deadline and then bypassed without a record.

### What to propose instead

Tiers by consequence: internal and assistive; customer-facing text; actions or regulated decisions. Scope by what changes behaviour: prompts wherever they live, model and version, parameters, retrieval sources, and tool definitions. Rights per tier: within the team with an eval result attached for the lowest tier, a second approver for the middle, the named risk owner for the top, and the board only for new vendors, new data classes and waivers. Enforcement where the change executes: protected production labels or a deploy step that checks the approval on the version id. Rollback to the last approved version pre-approved for on-call. A waiver register with expiries, reviewed monthly.

The cost of the replacement: risk and legal see fewer changes, which they may read as less control, and building the deploy check takes engineering time the policy did not ask for. The failure mode: tier assignment becomes the new negotiation, with teams arguing their feature down a tier, so the tier record states its reasons and the risk owner signs it.

## Follow-ups

- *Risk says a tiered scheme lets engineering mark its own homework on tier 1. What evidence would satisfy them, and what risk do they accept in writing if they agree?*
- *The head of legal wants every customer-facing change reviewed by legal, whatever the tier. What does that cost per month at the current change rate, and what do you offer instead?*
- *What would you need to see in six months of the tiered process to move a change type from the board to the team?*

## Common mistakes

- Critiquing the board's cadence and missing that the incident's cause is out of scope.
- Proposing to add retrieval changes to the same board, which makes the queue longer without fixing the review method.
- Removing the board entirely, leaving risk and legal with no role and no reason to support the replacement.
- Leaving rollback behind an approval because it "is still a change".
- Accepting minutes as audit evidence without asking how an approval maps to a running version.
