## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As an interview-prep article quotes it">
"You own an internal AI platform that other teams build agents on. What's your strategy, and if you could own only one metric, what would it be?"
</Callout>

Answer from a platform you have owned or worked on, or, if you have not, from the closest platform you have run for other teams. Ten minutes: the strategy in a few decisions, the metric, and why the alternatives lose. Then the interviewer argues for a different metric and holds the position.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask how many teams build on the platform today, and what share of their agents take actions with external effects.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask who is accountable today when an agent built on the platform takes a wrong action: the platform or the team.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what leadership currently reads about the platform each month.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The strategy is three decisions: the platform owns the things every agent needs and no team should build (tool registration with permissions, model access, run traces, budgets per run, and an eval harness for multi-step runs); product teams own their agents' behaviour and outcomes; and actions with external effects go through a permission layer the platform enforces. The one metric: the share of agent runs that reach a successful end state without a human correcting them, measured per team on runs the teams themselves label. The cost of that choice is that it depends on teams labelling outcomes, and a platform metric that rests on other teams' labels can be gamed by them.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The account.</b> Name the platform, its customers and its size, then the two or three decisions you made about what it owned and what it refused to own, with what each refusal cost. Then the metric you used and one time it was wrong or misleading. If the metric you used was adoption, say what it hid.</p>
<p><b>The metric, with two defensible alternatives.</b> <em>Successful runs without correction:</em> measures whether agents built on the platform work; costs labelling and attribution, since a failed run can be the team's prompt or the platform's tool layer. <em>Lead time from a new agent idea to a production run with traces and permissions:</em> measures whether the platform makes teams faster; it is the platform's own lever, but a platform can be fast at helping teams ship agents that do not work. <em>Adoption</em> (teams or runs on the platform) is the weakest: it rises with a mandate whatever the platform's quality. I would pick successful runs without correction if the platform also owns the eval harness, because then the platform controls how runs are labelled; lead time if it does not.</p>
<p><b>The constraint conflict.</b> Autonomy against safety: the faster the platform lets teams give agents write tools, the more of those runs need a permission check or a human approval, which is latency and friction the metric will register.</p>
</Fragment>

<ul>
<li><em>Your metric needs teams to label outcomes, and labelling every team's runs costs more than your platform's headcount. Which teams' runs do you label, and what do you accept in writing about the rest?</em></li>
<li><em>I would own lead time, not success rate, because the platform cannot control a team's prompt. Convince me, or concede and say what you would watch alongside it.</em></li>
<li><em>What would you need to see in a quarter of data to switch the metric you chose?</em></li>
</ul>

### The strategy, in decisions

**What the platform owns.** Model access through the shared gateway; tool registration, where a tool declares whether it reads or writes and what it can affect; per-run budgets on steps, tokens and spend; traces of every step with the tool calls and their results; and a harness that replays recorded runs against a candidate model or prompt. Huyen's advice to start without an orchestrator applies: the platform should not own the agent loop itself until several teams have written the same one.

**What it refuses.** Agent behaviour, prompts, and the decision to ship. A platform that owns "agent quality" becomes the review queue for every team's prompt change.

**Actions with effects.** Write tools carry a permission class. The platform enforces it at call time: some calls run, some need a human approval, some are denied for a given team. The CSA note's point about agents is that autonomous actions lack a clear responsible party; the platform closes that by recording, for each action, the run, the team, the tool owner and any approver.

### Defending the metric

Expect the interviewer to argue the other side. Concede what is true: the platform cannot control a team's prompt, so a team's bad agent drags the platform's number down. Then show how the metric is read: per team and per tool, so a fall localises to one team or one tool, and the platform's own work is visible in tool-level failure rates. The failure mode of this metric is that teams under pressure label more generously; the platform samples runs for independent review each month and reports the agreement rate beside the number.

### What the account needs to contain

Ownership of a decision that turned out to be wrong, with the evidence that showed it. Alternatives you considered with their costs. A measured result with its conditions. A disagreement with another team or a leader, and how it was settled without rank.

## Follow-ups

- *A team builds an agent that sends emails to customers and wants it exempt from the approval step because approvals hurt its conversion metric. Who decides, and on what evidence?*
- *Leadership asks for the platform's cost per successful run. What does that number include, and which team does it make look worst?*
- *Your traces store every tool result. Legal asks how long you keep them and who can read them. What do you answer, and what does it break?*

## Common mistakes

- Choosing adoption as the one metric and calling it success.
- Owning the agent loop and agent quality, then becoming the reviewer of every team's prompt.
- A metric that cannot be broken down by team and tool, so a fall cannot be attributed.
- No position on actions with external effects, which is the risk leadership will ask about first.
- A strategy with no refusals: a list of everything the platform will do, with no statement of what it will not.
