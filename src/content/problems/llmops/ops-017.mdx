## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 30 minutes">
"We are at 80% after a month. Product wants 95% before launch. How long, and how much labelling do we need?"
</Callout>

The feature, as a scenario: an assistant that answers members' questions about job postings, built like the one LinkedIn described in April 2024 (route, retrieve, generate). LinkedIn reported 80% of the basic experience within the first month and four more months trying to pass 95% of the full experience. Those are the only published inputs here; everything below is scenario.

Quality is the pass rate on a rubric applied by human graders. Today it is 80%. Graders have reviewed 2,000 production conversations and classified the 400 failures:

| Band | Modes | Failures each in the 2,000 | Total failures |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frequent | 4 | 60 | 240 |
| Middle | 20 | 5 | 100 |
| Seen twice | 10 | 2 | 20 |
| Seen once | 40 | 1 | 40 |

In the first month a team of three engineers shipped 10 verified fixes. Measured on a regression set, each fix removed between 50% and 90% of its mode's failures, median 75%. The grading team can review 500 conversations a day, five days a week, the capacity LinkedIn reported for its linguists. The rubric is stable and the scope does not change.

1. Estimate the weeks from today until a human-graded measurement shows 95%. **Enter that number.**
2. Say how you would verify each weekly release and how many graded conversations the plan uses per week.
3. Name the assumption that changes your answer most, and the point at which you would stop fixing modes and propose a structural change.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether the fix efficiency you plan with is the first month's median or its best case, and why later fixes would differ.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask whether two versions can be graded on the same frozen inputs, and whether a judge validated against these graders exists.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what the modes seen once or twice in 2,000 conversations say about the modes not seen at all.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>About 20 weeks, inside a band of 13 to 31 that is almost all fix efficiency. At the measured median of 0.75 it takes 74 fixes, about 30 weeks at 2.5 a week; at 0.85 it takes 31 fixes, about 12 weeks; add about a week for the final human-graded measurement. The dominant term is the count of tail fixes, each worth less than a tenth of a point. Below an efficiency of about 0.75 the census cannot reach 95% at all, so the plan needs a structural option priced before the date is agreed. Labelling is not the constraint if releases are verified on a paired regression set under a validated judge; it becomes impossible if each release is verified by human grading on fresh traffic.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Turn the census into points.</strong> Each failure in 2,000 conversations is 0.05 points of pass rate. Frequent modes are 60 × 0.05 = 3.0 points each, middle 0.25, seen-twice 0.1, seen-once 0.05. Together they are 12 + 5 + 1 + 2 = 20 points, the gap from 80% to 100%.</p>
<p><strong>Choose an efficiency and fix in order of size.</strong> At 0.8, a reasonable choice if later fixes come with better targeted cases:</p>
<ul>
<li>Frequent: 4 × 3.0 × 0.8 = 9.6, reaching 89.6%.</li>
<li>Middle: 20 × 0.25 × 0.8 = 4.0, reaching 93.6%.</li>
<li>Seen twice: 10 × 0.1 × 0.8 = 0.8, reaching 94.4%.</li>
<li>Seen once: 0.6 / (0.05 × 0.8) = 15 fixes, reaching 95.0%.</li>
</ul>
<p>That is 49 fixes; at 2.5 a week, 19.6 weeks. At the median 0.75 the same arithmetic needs every mode in the census, 74 fixes and 29.6 weeks, with nothing left over. At 0.85 the middle band reaches 94.45% and 7 seen-twice fixes close the gap: 31 fixes, 12.4 weeks. At 0.7 the whole census recovers 8.4 + 3.5 + 0.7 + 1.4 = 14 points, a ceiling of 94%. Add the final measurement below and the band is about 13 to 31 weeks. LinkedIn's four months, about 17 weeks, sits inside it, which says the shape is plausible and nothing more.</p>
<p><strong>Verification and labelling.</strong> A 95% pass rate needs n = 1.96² × 0.95 × 0.05 / 0.01² ≈ 1,825 independently graded conversations for an interval of plus or minus 1 point, about four days of the team, so the final acceptance is a one-off cost of about a week. Weekly releases cannot be verified that way: detecting a 0.5-point regression between two versions on different traffic needs about (1.96 + 0.84)² × 2 × 0.95 × 0.05 / 0.005² ≈ 29,800 conversations per arm, 119 days of the team for one comparison. Grade both versions on the same frozen inputs instead; if a release flips 1% of items, the paired comparison needs about (1.96 + 0.84)² × 0.01 / 0.005² ≈ 3,140 items, which a judge can grade overnight. The human budget then goes to validating that judge (200 a week, half from judge passes and half from judge fails, is one reasonable split), to a monthly discovery sample of 1,000 fresh conversations for modes the census missed, and to the final acceptance. That is roughly 400 a week in steady state against 2,500 available, and the spare capacity is the argument for a larger discovery sample, not for human-grading every release.</p>
<p><strong>What flips it.</strong> Efficiency, first: 0.75 versus 0.85 is the difference between seven months and three. Second, the tail you cannot see: 40 modes seen once in 2,000 conversations means more modes exist that were seen zero times, and each one found during the work adds fixes. Third, interaction: a prompt fix that breaks another mode lowers net efficiency, and only the paired regression set reveals it.</p>
<p><strong>The decision.</strong> Two commitments are defensible. Commit to about 20 weeks with a checkpoint at week 8, where the measured efficiency of the fixes so far replaces the assumption; the cost is a date that may move by months. Or commit to a structural option now (a stronger generation model, a fine-tuned router, or launching on the intents that already pass) and to local fixes only for the frequent and middle bands, about 24 fixes and 10 weeks; the cost is the structural work's own risk and, for the scope cut, a smaller launch. The second is the better choice when the measured efficiency stays near the median, because the tail fixes buy under a tenth of a point each. Its failure mode is that the new model or router moves the census: frequent modes come back in different forms, and the first month's error analysis has to be redone on the new system before any date holds.</p>
<p><strong>Unreasonable choices.</strong> Extrapolating the first month's slope (20 points in four weeks, so 15 more in three) ignores that the frequent modes are spent. An efficiency of 1.0 or the best observed 0.9 for every fix contradicts the measured spread. Verifying each release by human grading of fresh traffic cannot resolve the effect sizes of tail fixes within any budget on the page.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the three engineers say they can ship five fixes a week if verification is lighter. What does lighter verification cost, measured in what you can no longer detect?*
- *Reversal: what would the week-8 checkpoint need to show for you to promise the 12-week end of the band?*
- *Operating: the grading team is reassigned to another product at half capacity in week 10. Which of the three uses of their time do you cut first?*

## Common mistakes

- Extrapolating the first month's rate of improvement.
- Planning with the best observed fix efficiency instead of the median.
- Treating the census as complete when 60 of the 400 failures belong to modes seen once or twice.
- Verifying weekly releases with human grading on fresh traffic, which cannot see the effect of a tail fix.
- Giving a single date with no checkpoint where measured efficiency replaces the assumption.
