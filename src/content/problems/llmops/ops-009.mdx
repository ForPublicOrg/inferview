## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Incident replay">
Replay the April 2025 GPT-4o update from OpenAI's two public posts. At each checkpoint, commit before the next event appears. Then the staff question: you own the release gate for an assistant with millions of weekly users, and the next candidate adds a reward from user ratings. Add the gate that would have stopped this one, give it an owner and a threshold, and say what it costs and what it misses.
</Callout>

The events are taken from OpenAI's posts of 29 April and 2 May 2025, in order. The checkpoints ask what you would have believed or done with only the events above them.

## Hints

## Answer

What the posts say. The April 25th candidate combined changes that each looked beneficial on their own, including an additional reward from thumbs-up and thumbs-down data. In aggregate they weakened the primary reward signal that had been holding sycophancy in check, and user feedback can favour agreeable answers. Offline evals, including behaviour evals, looked good; the small A/B test showed users liked the model; some expert testers said it felt slightly off; no deployment eval tracked sycophancy. OpenAI launched on the positive signals and says that was the wrong call. A system prompt change late on Sunday cut much of the harm, and a full rollback began on Monday and took about 24 hours. The commitments: behaviour issues formally block launches, on proxy measurements or qualitative signals even when A/B metrics look good; an opt-in alpha phase; more weight on spot checks; better offline evals and A/B tests; sycophancy evals in the deployment process; and announcing updates, subtle or not.

The mechanism a release owner should take from it: the A/B metric and the new reward were the same signal. A model trained harder on thumbs will win an A/B test read on thumbs, so the test could not have caught the failure. Any gate for this candidate has to measure something the reward did not optimise.

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Two gates, one quantitative and one procedural. A paired sycophancy eval in the deployment gate: about 900 prompts where the user states a wrong belief or a bad plan, scored as agreement or not, run on the current and candidate models, blocking when the paired increase in agreement has a 95% interval above zero and a point estimate over 3 points. And a written qualitative veto: any expert tester's flag blocks the launch until it is turned into an eval the candidate passes, or a named owner overrides it in writing. The eval catches this failure class; the veto is what catches the next one, and it costs launch time.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The eval, with its size worked out.</strong> Suppose the current model agrees with a false or harmful premise on 10% of prompts and you want to catch a rise to 13%. Paired on the same prompts, the variance of the per-prompt difference is p<sub>A</sub>q<sub>A</sub> + p<sub>B</sub>q<sub>B</sub> − 2ρ√(p<sub>A</sub>q<sub>A</sub>p<sub>B</sub>q<sub>B</sub>) = 0.0900 + 0.1131 − 2 × 0.5 × 0.1009 = 0.1022, taking ρ = 0.5 from the middle of the 0.3 to 0.7 range Anthropic reports for question-score correlations between models. Then n = 7.849 × 0.1022 / 0.03² ≈ 891 prompts for power 0.8 at two-sided alpha 0.05. Unpaired it would be 1,771 per model. The 3-point threshold is the quantity you choose: smaller needs more prompts (1 point needs nine times as many), larger lets a visible regression through.</p>
<p><strong>The veto, with its owner.</strong> OpenAI had the signal and overruled it. A gate that only adds evals leaves that decision where it was. Write the rule down: a behaviour flag from a designated tester blocks; the block lifts when the flag becomes an eval the candidate passes, or when the model behaviour owner signs an override that names the flag, the evidence against it and the rollback trigger. Track the override rate. If every flag is overridden, the veto is ceremony.</p>
<p><strong>The decision has two defensible answers.</strong> The eval alone is cheap to run on every candidate, repeatable and arguable in numbers, and it misses any failure class nobody has written prompts for, which is what this one was in April 2025. The veto alone covers unknown classes and costs days on every flagged launch, depends on who the testers are, and invites arguments about taste with no number to settle them. Both together cost the most launch velocity. The conflict is weekly release cadence against coverage of behaviour no metric tracks, with an A/B test that says ship.</p>
<p><strong>Where it fails.</strong> The eval measures the class you wrote down; the next reward change will move a behaviour you did not. Graders for agreement are themselves models, and a candidate tuned on ratings can learn phrasing that reads as disagreement to a grader while conceding the point. And the veto depends on testers spending real time with the model: if the launch calendar squeezes the spot checks, the flag never gets raised.</p>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: your 900-prompt eval and the tester veto add four days to a weekly release train. Product asks you to cut one. Which goes, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see over a year of launches to remove the qualitative veto and rely on evals alone?*
- *Operating: the rollback target is a model your provider retires in sixty days. Your rollback plan for the next candidate assumes it exists. What changes?*

## Common mistakes

- Treating a positive A/B test as independent evidence when the metric is the signal the candidate was trained on.
- Answering "add a sycophancy eval" and stopping, without giving the qualitative flags a path to block a launch.
- Calling the rollback slow. It took about a day because a rollback is a deployment, and the prompt mitigation covered the gap.
- Blaming memory, which made things worse in some cases and is not what the postmortem names as the cause.
