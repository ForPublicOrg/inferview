## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design, 45 minutes">
"We are a regulated financial company with five LLM features planned: customer-facing chat, document extraction, code assistance for our engineers, summarisation, and compliance review. Design the model selection, adaptation, evaluation and lifecycle approach."
</Callout>

What you learn by asking (all scenario):

- Customers are in the EU and the UK. Customer data must be processed in the EU.
- The model risk team must validate any model, or any change of model, used in a customer-facing or compliance decision before it goes live. A validation currently takes 90 days.
- Contracts exist with two hosted model providers. No fine-tuning has been run with either.
- Six engineers are available for the platform and the five features, this year.
- The head of compliance does not want a generative model making compliance decisions and has said so to the board.

Produce the strategy: which model class serves each feature and why, how each is adapted and evaluated, how model changes are governed, what you give up, and the written artefact you would take to the model risk committee.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask the model risk team which parts of a validation must be redone for a model change, and which carry over from the last one.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask each provider for its retirement notice period in your contract, and on which platforms the model is offered.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask the head of compliance what the model may do in review if it may not decide: rank, retrieve, draft, or nothing.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Tier the features by consequence, not by model. Code assistance is bought; summarisation and extraction run on hosted models behind a gateway, with batch where they are not interactive; customer chat runs on a hosted model with retrieval and a human handoff; compliance review uses a model to retrieve and rank evidence for a person who decides. The binding constraint is lifecycle: a 90-day validation is longer than the 60-day minimum retirement notice, so every validated feature needs a second model validated in advance, or a model whose retirement the company controls. That conflict, not model quality, is what the strategy is built around.</p>
</Fragment>

<p>Code assistance is bought for the reasons most enterprises bought in 2025: no differentiation, a mature market, and nothing in it touches customer data. Compliance review follows the head of compliance: the model surfaces evidence and a person decides, which keeps the board commitment and still uses the model where it is strongest. The eval for it measures recall of items a reviewer would flag, because a missed item is the costly error.</p>
<p><strong>Data handling.</strong> EU processing narrows the hosted options to endpoints with EU processing in the contracts. On one provider, European residency requires Zero Data Retention or Modified Abuse Monitoring, both approval-gated, and stateful endpoints (assistants, threads, vector stores, files, evals, batches) keep data until deleted and fall outside Zero Data Retention. So the endpoint choice is part of the design: stateless calls, retrieval in the company's own store, and batch only where its retention is acceptable.</p>
<p><strong>The lifecycle conflict.</strong> Anthropic gives at least 60 days' notice before retirement; validation takes 90. A retirement notice arriving on the day it is issued leaves a fully validated feature with nowhere to go. Three ways out, each with a cost:</p>
<ul>
<li><em>A validated standby.</em> For each fully validated feature, validate a second model (a different provider or the next version) while the first is live. Costs a second validation per feature per cycle, and the standby itself ages.</li>
<li><em>A platform with a longer schedule.</em> Partner platforms set their own retirement dates; Claude Opus 4.1 is listed as retired except on Bedrock and Google-managed Vertex. A contract term on notice period with the platform can buy time. Costs negotiation and a dependence on a schedule that is still someone else's.</li>
<li><em>Weights the company controls.</em> Self-host an open-weight model for the feature whose validation is most expensive. A permissive licence such as Apache 2.0 matters here: Gemma's terms reserve Google's right to restrict usage remotely or otherwise, which a model risk committee will read as a dependency. Costs a serving stack, probably lower quality, and the security patching of the stack.</li>
</ul>
<p>Separately, cut the 90 days for model changes by agreeing with model risk what carries over: the eval set, the test protocol and the monitoring are validated once; a model change reruns them and reviews the differences.</p>
<p><strong>Adaptation.</strong> Prompting and retrieval for all five. No fine-tuning in year one: neither provider has been used for fine-tuning, OpenAI's self-serve fine-tuning closed to new organisations on 7 May 2026, and a tuned model adds its own validation and its own retirement (inference on an OpenAI fine-tune ends with its base model).</p>
<p><strong>Two defensible designs.</strong> Hosted everywhere, with validated standbys from the second provider for chat and compliance review: best quality, six engineers can run it, and the cost is two validations per feature per model generation. Or hosted for chat, extraction and summarisation, and a self-hosted Apache 2.0 model for compliance review: the most expensive validation happens once per model the company chooses to change, at the cost of a serving stack and a ranking model that may score below the hosted one. With six engineers I would start with the first and keep the second as the fallback if a retirement notice arrives during a validation.</p>
<p><strong>What we give up.</strong> Speed of adopting new models on the validated features, which will lag hosted releases by a validation cycle; the option of fine-tuning this year; and the compliance use case the board might have expected, a model that decides.</p>
<p><strong>The artefact for the model risk committee.</strong> A one-page lifecycle policy: the validation tier per feature; the eval set, metric and threshold that define "no regression" for each; the standby model per fully validated feature and the date its validation completes; the trigger to start migration (a successor release, not a retirement notice); the rollback path (the standby); and an owner per feature. It also states the open risk: a retirement notice for both the primary and the standby inside one validation cycle.</p>
<p><strong>How this fails.</strong> Standbys age too. A standby validated in January can be deprecated by the time it is needed, so the policy needs a calendar of every model's earliest possible retirement date, and a rule that no feature has both its primary and its standby retiring inside the same 90 days. And a model that ranks for compliance review shapes decisions even if it does not make them: reviewers who read the top ten stop reading the rest, so the recall eval has to cover what reviewers actually look at, not the full ranked list.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- The CTO wants one provider for everything to simplify contracts. What do you lose, and is any of it recoverable through contract terms?
- Model risk agrees to a 30-day re-validation for model changes that pass the existing eval. Which of the three lifecycle options do you drop?
- The extraction feature starts feeding a credit decision. What changes in its tier, eval and lifecycle?

## Common mistakes

- Choosing models per feature on leaderboard quality and never reaching the lifecycle question.
- Planning provider fine-tuning without checking whether the organisation can still create jobs.
- Treating the head of compliance as an obstacle rather than defining what the model may do in review.
- One validation tier for all five features, which either over-validates the internal tools or under-validates customer chat.
- Calling an open-weight model "frozen" without reading the licence clauses that let its publisher restrict use.
