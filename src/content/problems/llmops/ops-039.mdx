## Question

Eight product teams at an invented company each evaluate their LLM features in their own notebooks. Some sample twenty examples by hand; some run a judge model nobody has compared with human labels; one reports a single average with no interval. Twice this year a change that "passed evals" was rolled back within a week. Leadership asks you to build a shared eval platform with one engineer and one quarter. Two of the eight teams say their notebooks work and they will not move.

Design the platform, decide what it standardises and what it leaves to teams, and say what you would measure to know, after two quarters, whether it works.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Standardise the runner and the report, not the content: a service that takes a team's cases, graders and slices, runs production and candidate paired on the same cases, reports per-slice differences with intervals, labels every judge with its measured agreement against human labels, and attaches the result to the candidate's registry version. Teams keep their cases and rubrics. It works if, after two quarters, changes that pass it are rolled back less often than changes that passed notebooks, and teams use it for decisions without being told to. What I give up: no company-wide quality score, and two teams outside it for now.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The decision with two answers.</b> Mandate the platform for any change to a customer-facing feature, or offer it and let adoption show its value. Mandate: every change gets comparable evidence, and the rollbacks have a common record to learn from; it costs the two refusing teams' goodwill and makes one engineer's service the gate on eight teams' releases. Offer: no gate risk, and teams arrive when it is faster than their notebook; it costs comparability, and the rollbacks keep happening in the teams that do not adopt. With one engineer, I would mandate only the output format (paired result, interval, judge agreement) for customer-facing changes, and offer the runner. A team that produces that format from its own notebook is compliant. That keeps the two refusing teams in the evidence without forcing them onto the runner.</p>
<p><b>The quantity to choose.</b> How many human labels a judge needs before its result counts. The answer below computes one choice.</p>
<p><b>The constraint conflict.</b> Labelling budget against coverage: every judge needs human labels to be trusted, and the experts who label are the teams' scarcest people.</p>
</Fragment>

### What the platform standardises

**Suites as registered objects.** A team registers cases (with their source: production sample, hand-written, regression from an incident), graders (code checks and judges), and the slices it reports. The platform versions them, so a result names the suite version it came from.

**Paired runs.** Production and candidate run on the same cases, and the report is the per-case difference aggregated with an interval, per slice. A notebook that runs the two configurations on different samples reports noise as change.

**Judges carry their measurement.** Each judge has a true positive and true negative rate against a held-out set of human labels, with the date measured. A judge with no measurement is reported as unvalidated, and its results are shown but cannot gate.

**Results attach to versions.** The run is linked to the candidate's prompt version in the registry. In Langfuse's model a release is moving the `production` label to an immutable version, so a deploy step can check that the version being labelled has a passing run.

**Production traces feed suites.** Cases sampled from production logs and from incidents, so the suite tracks traffic as it shifts. The trace store is the platform's, from the gateway.

### How many labels a judge needs

Choose how precisely the judge's true positive rate must be known, then compute. Suppose a team wants the judge's rate of catching real failures known within ±5 points at 95 percent confidence, and expects it near 0.9. With the normal approximation, n = 1.96² × 0.9 × 0.1 / 0.05² = 3.8416 × 0.09 / 0.0025 = 138.3, so 139 labelled failures. If failures are 10 percent of outputs, that is about 1,390 labelled outputs to find 139 failures, before counting the passes needed for the true negative rate. A ±10 point requirement needs a quarter of that: about 35 failures and 350 outputs.

Reasonable choices are ±5 to ±10 points for a judge that gates releases, and looser for a judge used only to rank cases for human review. Unreasonable: requiring ±2 points (about 865 failures at 0.9, and over 8,600 outputs at a 10 percent failure rate), which no team will label, or no measurement at all, which is how the two rollbacks passed.

### Knowing whether it works

Three measures, read after two quarters:

- **Escaped regressions:** rollbacks or incidents within two weeks of a change, per change shipped, for changes that went through the platform against changes that did not. This is the outcome the platform was built for. Its weakness is small numbers: a handful of rollbacks a year cannot show a difference quickly, so it is read alongside the next two.
- **Decisions changed:** the count of changes held, revised or rolled back before release because of a platform result. Zero after two quarters means the platform is producing reports nobody acts on.
- **Voluntary use:** runs started by teams on changes the mandate does not cover, such as internal tools. Use without a mandate is the evidence it is faster than a notebook.

Not a measure: number of suites registered or runs executed. Both rise with the mandate whether or not the platform helps.

### The failure mode of this design

The output-format mandate can be satisfied with bad content: a team registers ten cases and an unvalidated judge, and gets a paired interval that is wide but formally compliant. The report shows interval width and judge status beside the verdict, and the release rule for customer-facing changes requires a validated judge or a human-labelled check on the slices that changed. That raises the labelling cost, which is the constraint the design started with.

## Follow-ups

- *A team's suite was sampled from production a year ago and traffic has shifted toward a new market. How does the platform notice, and who decides to resample?*
- *Leadership asks for one quality number per feature on a dashboard. What do you show, and what do you refuse to show?*
- *A vendor updates a model behind a stable alias overnight. Which part of the platform detects it, and what runs automatically?*

## Common mistakes

- Shipping a default judge for every team, which produces a number teams quote and nobody has validated.
- Unpaired comparisons: candidate and production run on different samples or different days.
- Measuring the platform by runs executed or suites registered.
- Forcing the two refusing teams onto the runner instead of requiring the evidence format, and losing them entirely.
- Treating labelling as free: no budget, no owner, and judges that are never rechecked.
