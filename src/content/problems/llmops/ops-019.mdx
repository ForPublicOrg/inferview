checklist:
  - Audits a stratified sample before changing anything, with the sample size chosen against the support lead's hours and the error rate reported with an interval per stratum
  - Separates the examples written before the pricing change and treats their price and plan facts as a known defect with a measured rate, not as noise
  - Moves checks that a lookup can decide (prices, plan limits, refund amounts) into code against the current pricing table, and spends the lead's hours on judgement
  - Removes exact and near-duplicates, and caps copies per template rather than keeping one, with the threshold chosen on a sample
  - Builds the eval set before the training set, from the most recent months, split by customer, and labelled by the support lead
  - Runs a leakage check between the eval set and the training set as a gate
  - Chooses between filtering to a smaller set and repairing for volume, and states the cost of the one not chosen
  - Compares any quality filter against a random subset of the same size before trusting it
  - Checks that the examples may be used for training (consent, contract) and that personal data is scrubbed before anyone outside support reads them
  - Writes the dataset card or decision memo, including what the set does not cover
  - States a failure mode of the plan, such as a filter that removes the rare intents the model needed

## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Workflow design, 40 minutes">
We have 8,000 labelled support examples collected over two years, and we want to fine-tune on them. Before we train, how do you assess and improve them, and what do you hand the training job and the eval?
</Callout>

The situation, constructed for this problem:

```text
Examples      8,000 (customer message, agent reply) pairs, marked "good" by agents at the time
Authors       35 agents; 6 of them wrote half the examples
Product       pricing and plan limits changed 9 months ago; 3,100 examples predate the change
Intents       tagged by agents with a free-text field; no fixed taxonomy
Expert        the support lead, 30 hours over the next three weeks
Deadline      the fine-tuning run is booked for the end of week 3
Contracts     enterprise customers (about 20% of examples) have terms that legal has not reviewed for training use
```

Design the work: what you measure first, how the set is cleaned and split, how the support lead's hours are spent, what you decide about the old examples, and what you hand over at the end of week 3.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask the support lead how long it takes her to judge one example, and time her on twenty.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask which facts in an agent's reply can be checked against a table the company already owns.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask whether the six most prolific agents are the most accurate ones.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Measure before cleaning: audit a stratified sample with the support lead, split by era and by author, so the error rate of the pre-change examples is a number with an interval. Build the eval set first, from the last three months, split by customer, and labelled by her. Then clean the training side in order: scrub, exclude the unreviewed enterprise contracts, exact and near-dedup against itself and the eval set, check price facts in code against the current pricing table, and decide whether to drop or repair the old examples from the audit numbers. Hand over a training set, a frozen eval set, a leakage report and a dataset card that says what is not covered.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Budget.</b> Suppose the lead judges an example in 3 minutes, which the 20-example timing replaces. 30 hours is 600 judgements. I would spend 300 on the audit (150 before the pricing change, 150 after), 200 on labelling the eval set, and keep 100 for adjudicating the filter and the final spot check.</p>
<p><b>What the audit decides.</b> Suppose it finds 36 of 150 old examples wrong (24%, Wilson 95% interval 17.9% to 31.4%) and 9 of 150 recent ones (6%, 3.2% to 11.0%). The intervals do not overlap, so the old era is measurably worse, and at roughly 24% of 3,100 old examples about 740 wrong answers would be trained in. Breaking the same sample down by author shows whether the six prolific agents are the problem; with 300 examples across 35 authors only the largest authors get usable per-author rates, which is enough for the decision that matters.</p>
<p><b>Cleaning, in order.</b> Scrub personal data first, so templated replies collapse. Exclude the enterprise customers' examples until legal reviews their terms; that removes about 1,600 examples and is written down as a coverage gap. Exact-dedup, then near-dedup with a threshold chosen on a sample of pairs, and cap copies per template, because the macro an agent pasted 400 times would otherwise be 5% of the set. Check price, plan-limit and refund figures in code against the current tables; those failures do not need the lead.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why build the eval set first?</em> Because every later choice, including the filter threshold, is judged on it, and anything that touches it later contaminates it.</li>
<li><em>Why split by customer?</em> The same customer asks the same question in several tickets; a row split puts the copies on both sides.</li>
<li><em>Why not let a model score all 8,000 for quality?</em> It can, once its scores are checked against the lead's audit labels and compared with a random subset of the same size.</li>
</ul>

### The decision about the old examples

**Filter.** Drop the pre-change examples that state prices or limits, keep the rest if the audit says their non-price error rate matches the recent era, and train on what survives, probably 3,000 to 4,000 examples. LIMA and AlpaGasus are the evidence that a smaller, cleaner set can match or beat a larger noisy one, with the conditions that their results are for general instruction following and AlpaGasus's gain over a size-matched random subset was about two MMLU points. The cost: intents that were common two years ago and rare since lose most of their examples.

**Repair.** Keep the old examples and rewrite the price and limit facts in code from the pricing table, with the lead spot-checking 50 repaired replies. The cost: a programmatic rewrite produces replies no agent wrote, and policy changes that are not numbers (what a plan includes, what qualifies for a refund) survive the repair.

I would filter, because the deadline leaves no time to validate a repair and the eval will show whether the lost intents matter. The failure mode is the one the dataset card must name: the filter removes rare intents along with the stale ones, and the eval, built from the last three months, under-represents them too, so a regression on them shows up in production first. A small slice of eval examples sampled by intent rather than by recency is the guard.

### What goes in the handover

A training set with a manifest of every exclusion and its reason; a frozen eval set with the lead's labels; a leakage report showing no eval example has a near-duplicate in training; a dataset card listing the excluded enterprise examples, the dropped era, the audit numbers and the intents with fewer than a handful of examples. The rollback trigger for the fine-tuned model is written before training: if it scores below the current model on the frozen eval, or on the rare-intent slice, it does not ship.

## Follow-ups

- *The support lead can give 10 hours, not 30. Which part of the plan do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?* Keep the eval labels and a smaller audit of the old era; drop the per-author analysis and accept that one bad author's examples may survive.
- *Legal clears the enterprise terms in week 2. Do you add those 1,600 examples to training, to eval, or to both?*
- *What would you need to see in the first month of production to reverse the decision to drop the old era?* A rise in failures on intents that were common before the change and are now under-represented, measured on the rare-intent slice.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants all 8,000 because "more data is better", the other wants 1,000 hand-picked. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Train both at the same cost and compare on the frozen eval with an interval.

## Common mistakes

- Cleaning before measuring, so nobody can say what the cleaning fixed.
- Splitting by row, so the same customer's repeated question lands on both sides.
- Treating "marked good by the agent at the time" as a quality label for facts that have since changed.
- Spending the lead's hours on price errors a lookup can find.
- Keeping one copy of each macro, which removes the template the model should learn, instead of capping copies.
- Training on enterprise examples whose contract terms nobody has read.
