## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Concept, 25 minutes">
"What do you monitor before ground-truth labels arrive, and which signal fires first for each kind of drift?"
</Callout>

The system, as a scenario. An assistant drafts replies for a B2B support team, 300,000 drafts a day. Agents accept, edit or discard each draft. The real outcome, whether the ticket reopened within 14 days, arrives two to three weeks later. The model is a hosted one called through the provider's alias; retrieval runs over the help centre, which is re-indexed nightly; two tools look up accounts and orders. Traffic falls into eight product areas; the smallest, billing disputes, is 1% of drafts and the one legal cares about.

The budget: an LLM judge on 2% of drafts and 200 human-graded drafts a week.

Answer three things. Name the signals you would watch and, for each source of drift (the provider's model, your own changes, traffic mix, serving infrastructure), the one that fires first and how long after the change. Choose how the 2% judge budget is spread across the eight areas, with numbers. Say what a frozen replay set adds that the production signals cannot.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Version stamps and the response's model field fire first, at the first request, but only for changes something announces. For everything else the fastest signals are cheap measurements of the output's shape on every request: output length, finish reasons, schema and assertion failures, refusals, tool calls per task, and input-side distributions for traffic. The agents' accept, edit and discard rates follow within hours, the sampled judge within a day, and the outcome labels after weeks. Spread the judge budget with a floor per area, about 300 a day each, so the 1% area gets an estimate of plus or minus 3 to 4 points daily instead of 7 to 8. A frozen replay set holds the inputs still, so a change in its outputs is the model, the serving stack or your own config, never traffic.</p>

<p>Behind those, in order of lag: agents' accept, edit and discard rates and edit size (hours, every draft, but they also move with staffing and ticket mix); the sampled judge pass rate (a day, but it costs money and can drift itself); the 200 weekly human grades (a week); reopened tickets (two to three weeks).</p>
<p><strong>Why output shape is worth more than it looks.</strong> Chen, Zaharia and Zou's two GPT-4 snapshots differ on the same 1,000 questions: the paper reports accuracy from 84.0% to 51.1% and mean response length from 638.3 characters to 3.9. A length histogram would have fired on the first day with no labels at all. Anthropic's 2025 postmortem is the counter-case: a routing bug reached 0.8% of Sonnet 4 requests on 5 August and 16% in the worst hour on 31 August while benchmarks, spot checks and canaries missed it; Anthropic's fix includes detection tests for unexpected character output and quality evaluations on production systems. Cheap signals catch changes in shape; a small share of degraded requests needs a sampled quality signal sized to see it.</p>
<p><strong>The judge budget, as a decision.</strong> 2% of 300,000 is 6,000 judged drafts a day. Uniform sampling gives billing disputes 1% of that, 60 a day; at a pass rate near 90%, the 95% interval is 1.96 × √(0.9 × 0.1 / 60) ≈ ±7.6 points, so a 5-point drop is invisible for a week. A floor of 300 a day for each of the eight areas uses 2,400 and gives ±3.4 points per area per day; the other 3,600 go proportionally, so the large areas still get most of the budget. Floors anywhere from 200 to 500 are defensible; the conflict is judge cost against coverage of the small areas, and the floor is where you state which area you care about. Reweight by traffic share before reporting a fleet-wide rate. Spend the 200 human grades on checking the judge, half from judge passes and half from judge fails, not on a second pass-rate estimate.</p>
<p><strong>What the frozen replay set adds.</strong> A few hundred fixed inputs, with their retrieved context frozen too, sent daily through the production path with fixed parameters. Production signals mix four causes; the replay removes traffic as one of them. If replay outputs change (length, assertion failures, judge pass rate, or the share of inputs whose answer differs from yesterday's, which is the paper's mismatch measure) while the version stamp did not, the change is upstream of you. Run a second copy with live retrieval to separate the index from the model.</p>
<p><strong>Where this fails.</strong> Every fast signal is a proxy for shape, and a regression that keeps length, format and refusals the same passes all of them; that is the class only the judge and the human grades see, a day and a week late. Agents' edit rate looks like quality but moves with who is on shift. The replay set ages: it holds last quarter's inputs, so a new product area is absent from it until someone adds cases. And the response's model field only helps if the service logs it; many do not.</p>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: your monitoring adds a judge, a replay job and per-area floors, and the team is two engineers. Which one do you drop first, and which kind of drift then goes unseen until the outcome labels arrive?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see to drop the per-area floors and go back to uniform sampling?* Per-area pass rates that move together for a quarter, so a fleet-wide alarm never missed an area-specific drop.
- *The provider announces that the alias will move to a new snapshot in two weeks. What do you run before the move, and what threshold on the replay set makes you pin the old snapshot instead?*

## Common mistakes

- Watching only the judged pass rate, which arrives a day late and samples the small areas too thinly to see them.
- Logging the requested model and not the model that answered.
- Treating agents' edit or accept rates as quality without controlling for staffing and ticket mix.
- Reading an aggregate change as model drift when per-area rates held and the mix moved.
- Building a replay set once and never adding the areas that appeared after it was built.
