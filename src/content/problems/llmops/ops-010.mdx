## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 40 minutes">
"The search team has a new reranker. Offline NDCG@10 goes up a few points. Design the online experiment that decides whether it ships, and tell me what you give up."
</Callout>

The situation, all of it scenario:

- An internal assistant answers engineers' questions from the company's docs. Hybrid retrieval returns 50 passages, a cross-encoder reranks them, and the top 8 go into the prompt. The answer shows its citations; the ranked list itself is never shown.
- The candidate is a larger cross-encoder. On 380 queries labelled eight months ago, NDCG@10 moves from 0.612 to 0.641. In staging it raises p95 time to first token from 1.9 s to 2.1 s; the latency objective is 2.2 s.
- It needs one more GPU node. The platform team renews node reservations in 21 days and wants a yes or no by then.
- Traffic: about 2,500 queries per weekday and 300 per weekend day, from about 1,400 weekly users.
- Signals you have today: thumbs on about 2.5% of answers; a reformulated query within two minutes after 18.0% of answers; citation opens on 31%.
- From logs: over a 14-day window a user asks 12 questions on average, and the intra-user correlation of the reformulation flag is 0.05.
- Two engineers own retrieval, and neither can be pulled onto this for more than a week.

Write the experiment plan: the stages, the unit of assignment, the primary metric and the effect you test for, the guardrails, the duration, and the rollback triggers.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The user never sees the ranking, so click interleaving has nothing to measure; the model is the reader of the list, and the online test has to be on outcomes of the whole answer. A superiority test on reformulation powered for 1.5 points takes 21 days and leaves nothing for an offline stage before the reservation date, and a modest NDCG gain probably moves reformulation by less than that. I would run two stages: a paired offline comparison on about 2,000 recent production queries (four working days), then a 14-day user-randomised non-inferiority test with a 1.5-point margin on reformulation and a latency guardrail. It ships if the offline gain holds on production queries and the online test rules out harm. What I give up in writing: the plan never measures the user benefit causally, so the GPU node is bought on offline evidence plus proof of no harm.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Why not interleaving.</strong> Team-draft interleaving compares rankers through clicks on a merged list. Here the list goes into a prompt, the user sees an answer with citations, and a citation open is a click on whatever the model chose to cite. The comparison that is cheap and sensitive is paired, offline, on the same queries; the online test has to be an A/B on the answer's outcome.</p>
<p><strong>Stage 1, paired comparison on production queries (days 1 to 4).</strong> Sample 2,000 queries from the last two weeks, stratified by team, and run both rerankers on the same 50 candidates. First count how many queries have a different top 8: if it is 15%, the average online effect is 15% of the effect on those queries, since the other 85% get the same context either way, and product learns what to expect before anyone waits two weeks. On queries where the top 8 differs, have a judge grade each passage's relevance with both lists hidden behind random labels, and hand-label 150 of the disagreements to check the judge. Report the paired difference with its standard error (Miller Eq. 7), not two separate means. The 380 labelled queries are eight months old; this stage is also the check that the gain survives today's query mix.</p>
<p><strong>Stage 2, online test, assignment and metric.</strong> Assign by a stable hash of the user, measure per query, and inflate the variance by the design effect for a user's queries: 1 + (12 − 1) × 0.05 = 1.55. The primary metric is reformulation within two minutes, because it exists for every answer; thumbs cover 2.5% of answers, so the same number of rated answers would take 1 / 0.025 = 40 times the traffic. Citation opens go on the dashboard but not in the decision, since a better context can make a citation less necessary to open.</p>
<p><strong>The effect, which is your choice.</strong> With z<sub>0.975</sub> + z<sub>0.80</sub> = 2.802 and unpooled variances, n per arm = 7.849 × (p₀q₀ + p₁q₁) / δ² × 1.55. Weekly volume is 5 × 2,500 + 2 × 300 = 13,100 queries.</p>

<p>The non-inferiority row uses (z<sub>0.95</sub> + z<sub>0.80</sub>)² = 2.486² = 6.183 and 2 × 0.18 × 0.82 / 0.015² for a true difference of zero: 8,112 per arm before the design effect. The 1-point superiority test needs six weeks. The 1.5-point test fits in 21 days only with no offline stage, ends on the reservation date with no time to analyse, and runs longer than the 14-day window the design effect was measured over, so its 1.55 is low.</p>
<p><strong>Two defensible plans and their costs.</strong> A superiority test at 1.5 points measures the benefit and is the only plan that can say the reranker helps users; it spends the whole deadline and probably returns an inconclusive result if the true effect is under a point. The two-stage plan takes 18 days, four for stage 1 with the canary running alongside it and fourteen for the A/B, which leaves three days before the reservation date, and rests the benefit on a judge and on production queries rather than on user behaviour. Product has to sign the second plan knowing that "no worse than 1.5 points" is the online claim.</p>
<p><strong>Guardrails, fixed before launch.</strong> p95 time to first token under 2.2 s, measured per arm in production rather than staging; answer cost per query, since the prompt does not change but the GPU does; the "no answer found" rate; and the sample ratio of assigned users, checked before any outcome is read.</p>

<p><strong>Where this plan fails.</strong> The judge and the new cross-encoder can share a bias, for example toward long passages with high lexical overlap, and then stage 1 measures agreement between two models rather than relevance; the 150 hand labels are the only check on that, and they are small. The non-inferiority test at 80% power also passes a reranker that is truly 1 point worse a fair share of the time, because 1 point sits inside the 1.5-point margin. And holding k at 8 tests the reranker as a drop-in; a better ranker might allow k = 5, fewer prompt tokens and a lower TTFT, which this plan leaves for a second test.</p>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the GPU node costs as much as the retrieval team's offline labelling budget for a quarter. Product asks you to skip the online test and ship on stage 1. What risk do you accept in writing, and what canary signal would make you pull it?*
- *Reversal: three weeks after shipping, reformulation is up 0.8 points against the pre-launch month. What would you need to see to reverse the decision, given the A/B allowed up to 1.5 points?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants the 21-day superiority test, the other the two-stage plan. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *Operating: the judge model in stage 1 gets a retirement notice for next month. What do you rerun, and what do you keep?*

## Common mistakes

- Proposing click interleaving for a ranking that only the model reads.
- Treating an offline NDCG gain on eight-month-old labels as an estimate of the online effect.
- Powering the test for an effect the change cannot plausibly produce, then calling the inconclusive result "no regression".
- Analysing queries as independent when users were assigned, which makes the interval too narrow by the square root of 1.55.
- Measuring latency in staging and treating it as the production guardrail.
