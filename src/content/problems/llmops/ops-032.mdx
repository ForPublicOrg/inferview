## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 25 minutes">
"We run our own support agent. A vendor quotes $0.99 per outcome. What does one resolved conversation cost us today, all in, and is the quote cheaper?"
</Callout>

Inputs. List prices are published; everything else is scenario.

- Model: Claude Sonnet 4.6 at list price ($3 per million input, $0.30 per million cache hits, $15 per million output).
- A conversation averages 5 model calls. Each call sends a 3,500-token prefix (system prompt and tool definitions) that is a cache hit, 4,500 uncached tokens (history and retrieved articles), and returns 350 tokens.
- Retrieval, reranking and ticketing API calls: $0.006 per conversation.
- 120,000 conversations a month.
- From the last month: 55% of conversations end with the customer confirming the issue is resolved; 15% end with the customer going quiet after the agent's reply; 30% are handed to a person.
- A sample of 200 quiet conversations: 62 customers contacted support again about the same issue within 7 days.
- A conversation handled by a person, including a re-contact, costs $5.00.
- Engineers who build and run the agent (prompt changes, evals, on-call) cost $20,000 a month each, loaded. You choose how many it takes.
- Assume the vendor's agent would land the same split of confirmed, quiet and handed-off conversations on this traffic.

Give the in-house cost per truly resolved conversation, with the team size you chose, and compare it with the vendor's quote on the same footing. Name the term that dominates the total spend and the assumption that flips the comparison.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask how the vendor counts an outcome, and whether a customer who goes quiet and comes back is counted once or twice.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how many engineering hours went into the agent last quarter after launch, not during the build.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what the handed-off conversations would cost if the agent did not exist.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Variable cost is $0.105 per conversation: five calls at $0.0198 plus $0.006 of services. Truly resolved conversations are the 55% confirmed plus the 69% of the quiet 15% that did not come back, 65.35%, so the variable cost per resolved conversation is $0.161. With three engineers ($60,000 a month over 78,420 resolutions, $0.765), the all-in cost is about <b>$0.93</b>. The vendor charges $0.99 on 70% of conversations, because quiet ones count, which is $1.06 per truly resolved one. In-house is cheaper by about 13% at three engineers and more expensive at four. The dominant term in total spend is neither: it is the human handling of escalations and re-contacts, $1.73 per conversation, which is identical on both sides.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Per call.</strong> Prefix: 3,500 × $0.30 / 1M = $0.00105. Uncached input: 4,500 × $3 / 1M = $0.0135. Output: 350 × $15 / 1M = $0.00525. Total $0.0198. Five calls: $0.099. With services, <strong>$0.105 per conversation</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>What counts as resolved.</strong> Confirmed: 55%. Quiet: 15%, of which 62 / 200 = 31% came back, so 15% × 0.69 = 10.35% are real resolutions. Truly resolved: <strong>65.35%</strong> of conversations, 78,420 a month.</p>
<p><strong>Variable cost per resolved conversation:</strong> $0.105 / 0.6535 = <strong>$0.161</strong>. Counting only confirmed resolutions would give $0.191; counting every quiet one as resolved would give $0.150.</p>

<p>Two engineers is reasonable for an agent past launch with a stable scope; three is reasonable if the team also owns the eval set and an on-call rotation, which a production agent needs; four is reasonable if the agent's scope is still growing.</p>
<p><strong>The vendor on the same footing.</strong> Its outcome includes a customer who stops asking for help, so it bills 55% + 15% = 70% of conversations: 0.70 × $0.99 = $0.693 per conversation. Divided by the 65.35% truly resolved, that is <strong>$1.06 per resolution</strong>. The re-contacts are billed once as outcomes and then cost a person $5.00 each on both sides.</p>
<p><strong>Dominant term.</strong> Total spend per conversation in-house is $0.105 + 0.30 × $5.00 + 0.15 × 0.31 × $5.00 = $0.105 + $1.50 + $0.23 = $1.84 before engineers. Human handling is 94% of it, and it is the same whichever agent answers, as long as the resolution split is the same. A one-point change in resolution moves spend by $0.05 per conversation, half the whole model bill.</p>
<p><strong>The assumption that flips it.</strong> Equal resolution rates. If the vendor's agent truly resolves 3 points more on this traffic, it saves 0.03 × $5.00 = $0.15 per conversation, which at 120,000 conversations is $18,000 a month, close to one engineer. The comparison is then decided by a trial, not by the price sheet. Volume is the second lever: at 12,000 conversations a month the fixed cost per resolution is ten times larger and buying wins at any team size.</p>
<p><strong>Two defensible answers.</strong> Keep building at two or three engineers: cheaper per resolution at this volume, keeps the transcripts and the eval set in-house, and costs the engineers' attention. Buy: comparable cost at three to four engineers, frees the team for differentiating work, and costs control over the outcome definition and the data. I would keep building at this volume only if the team is honest that it is three engineers, and I would run the vendor on 10% of traffic for a month to measure the resolution rate rather than assume it.</p>
<p><strong>How the recommendation fails.</strong> The 31% re-contact rate comes from 200 conversations; its normal-approximation 95% interval is 0.31 ± 1.96 × √(0.31 × 0.69 / 200), roughly 25% to 37%, which moves the truly resolved share by about one point either way. More importantly, a re-contact window of 7 days misses customers who gave up and churned without contacting anyone. If the real failure rate of quiet conversations is higher, every per-resolution figure rises, and the vendor's definition, which bills quiet conversations, gets more expensive per real resolution.</p>

### Choices that are not reasonable

- One engineer. A production agent with an eval set, prompt changes and on-call does not run on one person's time without the on-call gap becoming the outage plan.
- Counting quiet conversations as resolved on your side and not on the vendor's, or the reverse. The comparison has to use one definition.
- Leaving fixed cost out. The vendor's price covers its engineers; yours have to be added to compare.

## Follow-ups

- The prefix stops being a cache hit after a deploy that puts the customer's name at the top of the system prompt. What does a call cost now, and does it change the decision?
- Support leadership wants the agent's resolution target raised from 65% to 75%. What is that worth per month, and how much model spend would it justify?
- The vendor's contract has a minimum monthly commitment. At what volume does it bind, and does it matter here?

## Common mistakes

- Dividing the model bill by conversations and calling it cost per outcome.
- Comparing $0.99 with $0.161 and stopping there.
- Taking the vendor's outcome count as the resolution rate.
- Treating human escalation as outside the AI feature's cost, when it is most of it.
- Reporting a single number without saying which team size and which definition of resolved produced it.
