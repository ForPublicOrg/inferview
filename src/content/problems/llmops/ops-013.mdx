## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Debug, 30 minutes">
"Our eval scores are flat after a model change, but users are complaining. What is happening, and what do you sample first?" Here is the gate the change passed and the first week of production. Pick the explanation, rank what else is wrong, and say what you do about the model this week.
</Callout>

The setup, as a scenario. A support assistant answers billing questions inside a SaaS product, with retrieval over the help centre and two read-only tools (plan lookup, invoice lookup). The provider retires the old model, M1, in 45 days, so last Tuesday the team moved to M2 with the same system prompt.

The release gate was the team's golden set: 300 cases built in January from support tickets, each one user message with the documents retrieval returned for it. A pinned LLM judge scores each answer from 1 to 5 against a correctness and completeness rubric, and 4 or more is a pass. Both models were scored on the same day with the same judge and rubric:

| Gate run | Passes | Pass rate | Mean judge score | Median answer length |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | 263 / 300 | 87.7% | 4.21 | 190 tokens |
| M2 | 265 / 300 | 88.3% | 4.34 | 290 tokens |

A 5% canary ran for 24 hours, watching error rate and latency, and passed. The first full week against the week before, per day unless stated:

| Metric | Week before (M1) | Week after (M2) |
|---|---|---|
| Conversations | 41,000 | 42,500 |
| Share with 3 or more user turns | 38% | 39% |
| Thumbs-down per 100 rated replies, 1 or 2 turns | 6.1 | 6.4 |
| Thumbs-down per 100 rated replies, 3+ turns | 7.0 | 13.8 |
| Escalation to a human, 3+ turn conversations | 9% | 15% |
| Median output tokens per reply | 210 | 330 |
| Refusal rate | 1.2% | 1.1% |
| Tool calls per conversation | 1.9 | 1.9 |
| p95 time to complete reply | 4.1 s | 4.6 s |

Support forwarded one complaint: the user said in their first message that they were on the annual plan, and at turn four the assistant gave the refund steps for monthly plans.

<p class="tiny">The setup, gate and dashboard are synthetic, built for this exercise.</p>

Pick the hypothesis that explains the symptom. Then rank the other defects in this release, say what you would sample and read before you are sure, and decide what runs in production this week.

## Answer

**Why the golden set's coverage.** Every row that moved is in one slice. Thumbs-down in short conversations went from 6.1 to 6.4 per 100 rated replies; in conversations of three or more user turns it went from 7.0 to 13.8, and escalations in that slice went from 9% to 15%. The 3+ turn slice is 39% of traffic and none of the golden set, because every case was built from a single message. A gate that holds still while one untested slice doubles its complaint rate is doing exactly what it was built to do. The forwarded complaint fits a multi-turn failure (a fact from turn one lost by turn four), but it is one transcript and proves nothing alone.

The cost of leaving it running: 42,500 × 39% is about 16,600 long conversations a day, and 6 more points of escalation is roughly 1,000 extra human escalations a day.

**The ranking of the other defects.**

1. *The judge and rubric.* M2's answers are 53% longer in the gate (290 against 190 tokens) and its mean score rose by 0.13 while passes moved by 2 of 300. Zheng et al. name verbosity bias in LLM judges, and a 1 to 5 scale with a pass line at 4 is where a length preference can move cases across the line. This did not cause this week's complaints, since the short-conversation slice held, but it means the gate cannot be trusted to catch the next regression either. Check it with length-controlled pairs: the same content padded and trimmed, scored by the same judge.
2. *The canary watched only errors and latency.* It ran 24 hours at 5% and could not have seen a quality regression; it needed thumbs-down and escalation by turn-count slice, which were available.
3. *Output length.* Median reply length went from 210 to 330 tokens. It may annoy users on its own and it costs output tokens, but it rose in both slices and only one slice complained, so it is a secondary finding.

**What to sample first, and what the evidence does not show.** The dashboard does not say what M2 does wrong in long conversations; dropped constraints, contradicted earlier answers, tool results ignored after several turns and repeated questions all fit. Sample first: 100 thumbs-down or escalated 3+ turn conversations from the M2 week, plus 100 random 3+ turn conversations from the same week as a control, read in full and tagged with a failure label. Then take each failing conversation's prefix up to the failing turn and replay it on M1 and M2 with the same retrieval results, so the comparison is paired and holds the conversation fixed. The read tells you the failure mode; the replay tells you whether M1 really did better on those exact turns, which the week-over-week numbers cannot.

**The decision this week.** The conflict is the 45-day retirement against a measured regression. Two defensible answers:

- *Roll back to M1 now and re-migrate with a multi-turn gate.* The regression stops today, and the team builds a 3+ turn slice for the golden set from the sampled transcripts, finds the fix (often a state block in the prompt that restates facts the user gave, such as plan and account, on every turn), re-gates and migrates again. Cost: the clock keeps running, and if the fix takes longer than about 30 days the second migration happens with less than two weeks of margin.
- *Stay on M2 and fix forward.* No second migration, and the team learns M2's behaviour under real traffic. Cost: about 1,000 extra escalations a day until the fix ships, and a fix tested against a slice built from a week of complaints.

I would roll back. M1 still answers for 45 days, the rollback is one configuration change, and every day on M2 has a measured price. The written plan: multi-turn gate slice ready by day 10; M2 re-gated by day 25; re-migration by day 30 with a canary that watches thumbs-down and escalation per turn-count slice and rolls back if the 3+ turn thumbs-down rate rises more than 2 points over M1's in 48 hours.

**How big the new slice is.** The size is a choice. To see a fall from about 88% to 80% judged passes between two models scored on different conversations takes about 325 conversations per model (two-proportion test at 95% confidence and 80% power: 7.84 × (0.88 × 0.12 + 0.80 × 0.20) / 0.08² ≈ 325). Scoring both models on the same conversation prefixes needs fewer, because only the conversations where they disagree carry information. Anything from about 150 paired to 400 unpaired conversations is defensible; 20 hand-picked complaints is not, since it can only confirm the failure already seen.

**Where the recommendation fails.** The slice is built from the failure this week exposed, so it guards against the last regression; the next model change will regress something the gate still does not hold, such as conversations with tool errors or a long pasted invoice. And the rollback assumes the fix lives in the prompt. If M2 simply does worse at carrying state over many turns, no prompt reaches parity by day 30, and the team ships M2 at retirement with a known regression. Write that fallback now: which slice ships degraded, by how much, and who signs it.

**Why the wrong picks are tempting.** Judge verbosity bias is a published failure, and M2's scores did rise while its answers grew. A traffic-mix shift is the most common reason an aggregate moves without any slice changing, and the 3+ turn share did move by a point. Latency did rise, and abandonment is a real escalation driver, but it would show in both slices and not in thumbs on replies that arrived.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the multi-turn slice needs 300 or more conversations labelled before re-migration, and the two people who can label are also on the retirement plan for two other prompts. Which labelling do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing for it?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in the paired replay to stay on M2 instead of rolling back?* M2 failing no more often than M1 on the replayed 3+ turn prefixes, with the complaints traced to something the rollback would not fix, such as a retrieval change that shipped the same week.
- *Operating: on day 25, M2 with the state block passes the new slice but its single-turn pass rate fell from 88% to 85%. Twenty days remain. Do you ship it, and who signs off on the single-turn loss?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants the judge replaced with pairwise comparison against M1, the other wants binary pass or fail per criterion. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Score the same 200 labelled conversations both ways and keep the judge whose disagreements with human labels are fewer and less tied to answer length.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the flat gate score as evidence that the model did not regress, instead of asking which traffic the gate contains.
- Reading the aggregate thumbs-down rate, which blends a slice that held with one that doubled.
- Blaming the judge because its scores moved, without checking that the complaints came from cases the judge never saw.
- Sampling complaints only, with no random control from the same slice, so every failure found looks like the cause.
- Fixing forward on M2 without pricing the escalations it costs per day.
