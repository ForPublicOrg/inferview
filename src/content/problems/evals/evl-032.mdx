## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"This launch plan was approved on Friday. Every number in it is green. Two of our expert testers say the model feels off and cannot say why. Review the plan: what is wrong, what would you change before launch, and does it ship?"
</Callout>

The plan below is constructed for this problem; its shape follows the failure OpenAI described in its GPT-4o postmortem of April 2025. Read it once as an approver would, list the flaws and rank them, and choose the A/B duration and the rollout stages you would require. Then open the checklist.

### Launch plan: assistant model update M-17

**Summary.** M-17 is the next post-trained version of the assistant model. It combines three improvements, each of which looked positive in its own experiment: a new reward signal from users' thumbs-up and thumbs-down ratings, better use of saved user memories, and a fresher data mix.

**Offline evaluation.** All launch evals pass. Capability benchmarks are flat to positive. Behaviour evals (refusals, policy compliance, tone) pass at or above M-16. Safety red-team pass rate is unchanged.

**Online evaluation.** A/B test on 1% of traffic for 4 days, M-17 against M-16. Primary metric: thumbs-up rate, +2.1% relative, significant at p below 0.01. Secondary: messages per session, +1.4%. Users with memory enabled were excluded from the test to reduce variance.

**Expert review.** Twelve expert testers used M-17 for a week. Two noted that the model "feels off, somehow more eager". The notes are recorded as qualitative feedback and are non-blocking, since no launch metric regressed.

**Rollout.** 100% of users on Tuesday. Rollback triggers: error rate, p95 latency or refusal rate above M-16 by the thresholds in the runbook. If a behaviour issue is reported, the runbook's first step is a system-prompt update, which ships within hours without retraining.

**Communication.** None to users; this is a quality update with no new features.

**Sign-off.** Metrics owner, Friday.

## Answer

Rank by how directly each flaw lets the risky change through, then by how hard it is to undo.

**1. The A/B test measures the reward, not the outcome.** The new reward is trained on thumbs-up ratings, and the primary metric is the thumbs-up rate. A model optimised on a signal will move that signal; the +2.1% is the training objective showing up in production, not evidence that users are better served. OpenAI wrote that this kind of user feedback can favour more agreeable responses. The test needs an outcome that the reward cannot reach directly: task success on audited conversations, or a measured behaviour such as agreeing with a user's stated plan regardless of its merit. It is easy to approve because each half, a reasonable reward and a standard A/B metric, is sensible alone. Approval at the moment of the answer and the outcome can also diverge without any training loop: in METR's study, developers estimated a 20% speed-up and were measured 19% slower.

**2. The expert notes were filed as non-blocking.** "Feels off, somehow more eager" from two of twelve experts is the only evidence in the plan that looks at behaviour the metrics do not cover. In OpenAI's case, offline evals and A/B tests looked good while some expert testers said the behaviour felt slightly off, and the launch went ahead; afterwards OpenAI said behaviour issues should be launch blocking. A vague note cannot block forever, so the rule is: an expert concern blocks until it is turned into a check, for example 100 prompts where the user states a bad plan or a weak idea, judged for agreement and validated on human labels, run on M-16 and M-17.

**3. No eval targets the risk the change creates.** The behaviour evals cover refusals, policy and tone. A reward from user approval pushes toward approval, and nothing in the suite measures flattery or endorsing a user's mistake. Passing evals that were not written for this risk is not evidence about it.

**4. Memory users were excluded.** OpenAI found memory can exacerbate sycophancy in some cases, and M-17 changes how memories are used. Excluding those users to reduce variance removes the population where the effect is likely largest, and then ships to them on Tuesday.

**5. Three changes in one update.** Each improvement looked positive in its own experiment; their combination was never tested separately, and a regression in M-17 cannot be attributed to one of them. Rollback is all or nothing.

**6. The rollback plan cannot fire for this failure.** The triggers are errors, latency and refusals. A model that agrees with everyone produces none of them. The runbook's first step, a system-prompt patch, is acceptable if it is a stopgap with a model rollback already scheduled; OpenAI's own sequence was a prompt update late on a Sunday and a full rollback the next day, and the prompt line it replaced turned out not to be the root cause.

**7. The rollout and the communication.** One step from 1% to 100%, and no release notes for a change in behaviour that users will notice. OpenAI committed afterwards to communicate updates proactively, subtle or not.

**The numbers you were asked to choose.** A/B duration: at least two weeks, so returning users meet the model across several sessions and the test sees behaviour that builds over repeated use; 4 days measures first impressions. Anything from 10 days to 4 weeks is defensible, and the choice should come from how long the effect takes to show in the testers' conversations (Hint 2). Rollout: 1%, 10%, 50%, 100%, with at least two days' hold at each and the sycophancy check and audited outcome read at every step.

### Does it ship?

Two defensible answers.

- **Hold M-17.** Build the agreement eval from the testers' conversations, rerun the A/B with an outcome metric and memory users included, and test the reward change on its own. Cost: two to four weeks, and the data-mix and memory improvements wait for the reward change they were bundled with.
- **Ship M-17 without the feedback reward.** Retrain or pick the checkpoint without the new reward, keep the memory and data improvements, and run the staged rollout. Cost: a retrain, and the new reward, which may be fine with a counterweight, is shelved without being measured.

I would hold, because the evidence that M-17 is better is the very signal the risky change optimises, and the only independent evidence, the testers, says something is wrong. The failure mode of holding: a sycophancy eval built in a week from twenty conversations becomes the new green light, and the next update that is off in a different way passes it. The check gets written down as one check among the launch criteria, not as the definition of behaviour quality.

## Follow-ups

- *The agreement eval costs three weeks of two researchers and delays every model update by a week from now on. Which half do you cut, and what risk do you sign for?*
- *What would you need to see to ship a thumbs-based reward at all?* An outcome metric that the reward cannot reach directly improving in the A/B test, with the agreement eval flat against the previous model.
- *The metrics owner says the expert testers are two people with a feeling and the A/B test is thousands of users with a p-value. Two senior people disagree; how do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *After launch, users post screenshots of the model praising bad decisions. The system-prompt patch reduces it by half overnight. Roll back the model anyway, or keep the patch while you investigate?*

## Common mistakes

- Treating the significant A/B result as independent evidence when it reads the signal the model was trained to raise.
- Dismissing the expert notes because they carry no number, or blocking forever on them without turning them into a check.
- Accepting "behaviour evals pass" without asking which behaviours they measure.
- Proposing more offline evals and leaving the rollback triggers blind to behaviour.
- Treating a system-prompt patch as the fix for a trained behaviour.
