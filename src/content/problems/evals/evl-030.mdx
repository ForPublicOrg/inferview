checklist:
  - Computes the cost of running every suite on every push and shows it cannot fit, before cutting anything
  - Places each suite on a trigger (push, merge candidate, nightly, release, sampled production) with a per-month cost that sums under the budget and leaves stated headroom
  - Keeps deterministic checks on every relevant push and moves judged suites to merge candidates or later
  - Caches outputs on case, prompt hash, model snapshot, parameters and judge version, and skips runs when none of those changed
  - Retires or slows the saturated red-team suite, or keeps it per pull request, and states the risk or cost of the choice in writing for the safety lead
  - Subsamples the multi-turn simulations or the capability trials instead of dropping them, and says what the subsample cannot detect
  - Sets the online sampling rate from the budget and filters before sampling, for example on low feedback or sensitive intents
  - Makes reruns idempotent so a retry after a rate-limit error neither double-charges nor double-counts a result
  - Meters month-to-date spend and says which suites degrade first when it runs hot, while gating suites never skip silently
  - Decides whether a gating suite that cannot run fails closed or open, with the cost of each
  - Names who approves an override of a blocked merge and where it is recorded
  - Writes down what the schedule gives up, and the signal that would move a suite back to a more frequent trigger

## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Our eval bill is out of control. Here are our suites, our volumes and our budget. Design what runs when, what you stop running, and how the pipeline behaves when the money runs low."
</Callout>

The scenario, given by the interviewer. A team of three runs evals for a customer-facing support agent. Model calls for evals, judges included, are capped at $8,000 a month. Prices and volumes are the team's own measurements, not vendor figures.

| Suite | Contents | Cost per run |
|---|---|---|
| A. Deterministic | 1,000 cases: schema, citations present, banned content; $0.003 per case | $3.00 |
| B. Regression, judged | 400 cases, 3 binary judges; $0.003 generation + 3 × $0.004 | $6.00 |
| C. Capability | 250 cases, 5 trials each for pass^k, judged as B; pass rate 62% | $18.75 |
| D. Multi-turn simulations | 150 scenarios with a simulated user, 3 trials; $0.50 per conversation | $225.00 |
| E. Red-team | 2,000 adversarial prompts with a judge; $0.005 each; 100% pass for 12 weeks | $10.00 |
| F. Online judges | 30,000 production conversations a day, 2 judges at $0.004 | $240.00 a day at 100% |

Volumes per month: 660 pushes to 220 pull requests that touch prompts, tools, retrieval or the model pin; 30 nights; 6 release candidates. Today every suite except F runs on every push, and F is off. The safety lead insists that E keep running on every pull request. The judge provider returns rate-limit errors on bursts above a few hundred calls a minute.

Design the workflow: triggers, state, retries, budgets, approvals and rollout. Choose the online sampling rate and defend it. Then open the checklist.

## Answer

**The current bill.** Every suite on every push: $3.00 + $6.00 + $18.75 + $225.00 + $10.00 = $262.75 a run, × 660 = $173,415 a month, about 22 times the budget, before any online judging. Suite D alone on every push is $148,500. Nothing is fixed by trimming; the triggers have to change.

**A schedule that fits.**

| Suite | Trigger | Runs | Month |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Every push that changes prompts, tools, retrieval or the model pin; cached otherwise | up to 660 | up to $1,980.00 |
| B | Merge candidate, baseline from cache | 220 | $1,320.00 |
| C | Nightly on main | 30 | $562.50 |
| D | Release candidate | 6 | $1,350.00 |
| E | Release candidate | 6 | $60.00 |
| F | 5% of conversations, after filters | 30 days | $360.00 |
| Total | | | $5,632.50 |

That leaves $2,367.50 for reruns of borderline flips, judge re-validation and the month a model upgrade forces two full extra cycles. The online rate: 5% of 30,000 is 1,500 conversations a day, enough to see a daily trend per major intent; I would put the filter first (low feedback, refunds, account access always evaluated) and spend the rest of the 5% on a random sample, so the filtered stream does not become the only view of production. Anything from about 2% to 10% is defensible against this budget; 100%, at $7,200 a month, is not.

**State and idempotency.** Each eval result is keyed by (case id, prompt hash, model snapshot, generation parameters, judge version). A push that changes none of them reuses the stored result, which is the same idea as promptfoo's cache keyed on the prompt and config files, applied per case. A run has a run id; retries after a rate-limit error re-request only the missing keys and write with the key, so a retried call neither charges twice in the ledger nor counts a case twice in the gate. Judge calls go through a client-side rate limiter sized below the provider's limit, so bursts from 400 cases do not trip it in the first place.

**Budget control.** A month-to-date spend meter per suite. At 70% of the budget by day 20, nightly C drops to every other night; at 85%, D runs on the final release candidate only. Gating suites (A, B) never skip silently. If a gating suite cannot run, because the meter is exhausted or the provider is down, the merge is blocked and the pull request says why: fail closed. The cost of failing closed is that a provider outage stops merges; the alternative, failing open, lets an outage turn the gate off without anyone deciding to.

**The safety lead's request.** Two defensible answers. Keep E per pull request: 220 × $10 = $2,200 a month, which still fits (total $7,772.50) but leaves $227.50 of headroom, so the first forced rerun cycle breaks the budget. Or run E per release candidate and nightly, $360 a month, and spend the difference on growing E: a suite that has passed 100% for twelve weeks measures nothing new, and the FAQ's advice is to run evals for critical errors often despite their cost, but first to confirm the error can actually be triggered; twelve weeks without a failure has not shown that. I would take the second, with the safety lead's agreement that every new attack found in red-teaming or production goes into a fast subset of E that does run per pull request. Written risk: an attack already in E can regress on a pull request and be caught at the next nightly run, up to a day later, before any release.

**What stops.** E per push. D per push and nightly. C's fifth trial: dropping to 3 trials cuts C to $11.25 a run, and pass^3 still separates consistent from inconsistent cases at this pass rate. B cases that duplicate A's deterministic checks.

**Approvals.** A merge blocked by B can be overridden by the eval owner and the product owner together, recorded on the pull request with the flipped cases listed. Nobody can override A.

**Rollout.** Run the new schedule for two weeks with the old per-push D and C still running on one pull request in five, to measure what the schedule misses: any regression that the per-push run caught and the new schedule did not catch until nightly or release.

**What you give up.** Authors no longer see capability, simulation or red-team results while iterating; the first time they see D is on a release candidate, where a failure is expensive. Multi-turn regressions are found up to a release cycle late.

**Failure mode of this schedule.** It is tuned to today's suites. Suites grow: D gains scenarios after every incident, and at 300 scenarios its release line doubles to $2,700 without anyone deciding it. The meter catches the overrun but not the cause. Review per-suite cost per month alongside the budget, and make adding cases to D a decision that carries its monthly cost.

## Follow-ups

- *D is $1,350 of a $5,632.50 plan and caught one regression last quarter. Which half of its scenarios do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *What would you need to see to put D back on every merge candidate?* A regression that reached a release candidate, or production, which only multi-turn simulation would have caught, more than once a quarter.
- *The provider retires your judge model next month. B's pass rate moves 4 points on the new judge with no change to the agent. How does the schedule handle a judge migration?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants fail closed, the other fail open with an alert. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Count last quarter's provider outages and the merges they would have blocked, against the escaped regressions from runs that did not complete.

## Common mistakes

- Cutting suites by feel without computing the per-trigger cost first.
- Keeping a saturated suite on the most frequent trigger because it feels safe, while never adding cases to it.
- Letting a budget overrun or a provider outage skip the gating suite silently.
- Caching on the prompt alone, so a model snapshot or judge change reuses stale results.
- Turning online judging on at 100% because production matters most, and spending the whole budget there.
