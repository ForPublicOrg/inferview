checklist:
  - Computes the expert budget (24 hours) against a measured or chosen time per trace, and shows that random sampling alone cannot supply enough failing examples of the rarer modes
  - Labels each trace once against every failure mode, as binary labels, rather than one mode per pass or a 1 to 5 scale
  - Moves the modes that a lookup can decide (plan and price facts, refund amounts) to code checks against the source tables, and spends expert hours on the modes that need judgement
  - Draws dev and test failures from a flagged pool and estimates prevalence only from a random sample, and says why the two are kept apart
  - Splits each judged mode into a few prompt examples, a dev set and a held-out test set, by conversation, with 30 to 50 failing and 30 to 50 passing examples in each of dev and test, and uses the test set once
  - Measures agreement with a second labeller on an overlap enriched for the rare modes, reports kappa per mode, and names the expert as the final word on disagreements
  - Re-labels a blind sample of the expert's own early labels late in the programme to detect criteria drift, with a stated action when agreement drops
  - Gives a week-by-week plan whose hours add up to 24
  - Refuses in writing to gate releases on any mode without a measured true positive and true negative rate on test, and on tone at all
  - Answers the support lead's week-3 request with something that ships by then and a date for judge gating, instead of a flat no
  - Writes rollback triggers for a gating judge, such as a weekly spot check against the expert with a demotion threshold set from the measured error rates
  - States a failure mode of the plan, such as flagged failures being the obvious ones, so the test set overstates the judge on subtle cases

## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Workflow design, 40 minutes">
Design the labelling operation behind the evals for our billing support assistant: what gets labelled, by whom, how you split it, how you know the labels agree, and what you do in each of the next six weeks.
</Callout>

The situation, constructed for this problem:

```text
Assistant      answers billing and account questions; tools: get_plan, get_invoice,
               issue_credit, escalate_to_human
Error analysis last month, 100 random traces, first upstream failure per trace:
  wrong plan or price fact          9
  tool error ignored                6
  invented product feature          5
  missed escalation                 4   (escalation policy: legal threats, fraud, account takeover)
  refund or credit policy misstated 3
  tone                              2
  no failure                       71
Expert         one billing specialist, 4 hours a week for 6 weeks
Pilot          she labelled 20 traces against all six modes in 30 minutes
Flag signals   thumbs-down, conversations escalated later by the customer, reopened tickets
Second person  the support lead, available for a few hours in total
Stakeholder    the support lead wants an LLM judge gating weekly prompt releases from week 3
```

Design the operation: the label set, which modes get a judge and which do not, how traces are sampled, the splits, the agreement checks, the six-week plan with hours, what you will and will not gate on, and your answer to the support lead.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what share of flagged conversations fail each mode; the error analysis only gives rates on random traffic.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Twenty-four expert hours label at most about 960 traces at the pilot's pace, and random traffic cannot supply enough failures of a 4% mode for a judge. So the plan spends no expert time on what code can check (plan and price facts, credit amounts), validates judges for the two modes that need judgement and cost most when missed (missed escalation, invented feature), samples their failures from flagged traffic and their prevalence from random traffic, checks agreement with the support lead on an enriched overlap and the expert against her own early labels, and gates on nothing that lacks a measured true positive and true negative rate on held-out labels. The support lead gets code-check gating in week 2, a manual escalation review per release until week 6, and judge gating in week 6 if the test numbers hold.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Budget.</b> 4 hours × 6 weeks = 24 hours = 1,440 minutes. The pilot ran at 30 / 20 = 1.5 minutes per trace for all six modes at once, so the ceiling is 1,440 / 1.5 = 960 traces. Labelling, adjudication, definition sessions and drift checks share that ceiling.</p>
<p><b>Why random sampling cannot do it.</b> A judge needs 30 to 50 failing examples in each of dev and test. For missed escalation at 4 per 100 random traces, 60 failures need about 60 / 0.04 = 1,500 random traces, 2,250 minutes, more than the whole budget for one mode.</p>
<p><b>What the label set becomes.</b> Wrong plan or price facts and misstated credit policy are lookups: compare the reply's price, plan limits or credit amount with the pricing table and the credit policy, as code. Tool errors ignored are half code (did a tool return an error) and half judgement (did the reply say the action happened), and a code pre-filter plus a narrow judge covers it later. Tone is labelled for monitoring only. The two judged modes are missed escalation, the costliest single failure, and invented feature, the most frequent judgement failure.</p>
<p><b>Sampling.</b> Dev and test failures come from the flagged pool, where both modes should be more common; week 1 measures how much more. Prevalence for the dashboard comes only from random traces, because a rate measured on flagged traffic describes flagged traffic.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why not label one mode at a time?</em> The expensive part is reading the conversation; once read, six binary labels cost seconds more than one.</li>
<li><em>Why binary labels?</em> A 1 to 5 tone score from one labeller drifts and invites the middle value; a pass or fail rule per mode can be checked against a second labeller.</li>
<li><em>Who breaks ties?</em> The billing specialist. Disagreements with the support lead are discussed, the written definition is revised, and her call stands.</li>
</ul>

### The six weeks

The hours assume 1.5 minutes per trace; the week-1 numbers replace that assumption.

| week | expert hours | work |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 | 1 h with the engineer turning the two judged modes into written pass/fail rules on 20 traces; 1.5 h labelling 60 random traces (prevalence, and a check of the pilot's pace); 1.5 h labelling 60 flagged traces to measure each mode's rate in the flagged pool |
| 2 | 4 | 4 h labelling 160 flagged traces; the support lead labels 40 of them independently, chosen so at least 15 are escalation-relevant |
| 3 | 4 | 1 h adjudicating the expert and support lead's disagreements and revising the definitions; 3 h labelling 120 flagged traces |
| 4 | 4 | 3 h labelling 120 flagged traces; 1 h reviewing dev cases where the engineer's first judge prompt disagrees with her labels |
| 5 | 4 | 45 min re-labelling 30 of her week-1 traces blind (drift check); 2 h labelling 80 random traces; 1.25 h on judge disagreements in dev |
| 6 | 4 | 1 h reviewing every test-set disagreement after the single test run; 2 h labelling 80 random traces; 1 h signing the gating memo |

Totals: 460 flagged traces from weeks 1 to 4 plus 220 random from weeks 1, 5 and 6, 680 labelled traces, inside the 960 ceiling because adjudication, definitions and the drift check take the rest. If week 1 finds escalation failures in 20% of flagged traces, 460 flagged traces hold about 92, enough for 40 in dev, 40 in test and a few prompt examples; at 10% they hold about 46, short of 30 in each of dev and test, and the escalation judge slips from gating to monitoring. That is the number to read at the end of week 1.

**Splits.** Per judged mode: a few failing and passing examples for the judge prompt (10 to 20%), a dev set for iterating the prompt, and a test set the judge sees once, in week 6. Split by conversation and by customer, so near-identical conversations from one account do not land on both sides.

**Agreement.** Kappa per mode on the 40-trace overlap, computed as (p_o − p_e) / (1 − p_e). On a mode that fails rarely, kappa swings with one or two disagreements, which is why the overlap is enriched; with 15 escalation-relevant traces, read every disagreement rather than trusting the coefficient. The drift check compares her week-5 labels with her own week-1 labels on the same 30 traces: if she now fails traces she passed, the definition moved, and the dev labels from before the move are re-checked against the revised rule before the judge is tuned on them.

**What the programme gates on, and what it refuses to.** Code checks for price facts and credit amounts gate from week 2, after the engineer hand-checks 30 cases against the pricing table and the credit policy. A judge gates only with a measured true positive and true negative rate on its test set and a written threshold for both. Tone never gates. Production rates for the judged modes are reported from random traffic, corrected for judge error: with observed fail rate p, the true rate is (p + TNR − 1) / (TPR + TNR − 1).

**The answer to the support lead, in writing.** Week 2: code checks gate releases for price and credit errors. Weeks 2 to 5: each release gets a manual review of 20 escalation-relevant conversations by the engineer, with the expert consulted on any doubtful case. Week 6: the escalation judge gates if its test TPR and TNR clear the thresholds agreed now; if the flagged pool is too thin, it monitors and the manual review continues. What you give up: three weeks in which an invented feature or a missed escalation can ship, caught only by the manual sample; the risk is accepted by the support lead and the product owner by name.

**Rollback triggers.** A gating judge gets a weekly spot check: 20 conversations it passed and 20 it failed, labelled by the expert. With a measured TNR of 0.9, about 2 false alarms in 20 passing conversations are expected; more than 5 in one week, or any missed escalation the expert finds among the passes, demotes the judge to monitoring until it is re-validated.

### The other defensible plan

Spread the 24 hours across all six modes: label 900 random traces, report prevalence for every mode with an interval, and gate on nothing but the code checks. It gives an honest picture of all six failures, avoids the flagged-pool bias, and needs no judge. The cost: no judge is validated at the end, missed escalation is measured at about 4% with a Wilson interval of about 2.9% to 5.5% on 36 of 900, too wide to see a one-point change, and the support lead's request is still unmet in week 6. The deep plan fits when one or two failures dominate the cost of being wrong; the broad one fits when the team does not yet know which failure matters most.

### Where this plan fails

Flagged conversations are the failures a customer noticed. A missed escalation the customer never complained about, such as an account-takeover attempt the assistant handled politely, is under-represented in the flagged pool, so the test-set TPR is measured on the visible failures and overstates the judge on quiet ones. Keep a small random slice in the test set, label every escalation found in random traffic, and report the judge's TPR on that slice separately even when it is small. The second failure is the single expert: her definitions are the ground truth, and the agreement check with the support lead covers 40 traces. If they disagree on more than a handful of escalation cases, the definition is not ready to automate.

## Follow-ups

- *The support lead says six weeks is too slow and the project loses its sponsor unless a judge gates in week 3. What do you give, and what risk do you write down?* Let an unvalidated judge flag releases for human review without blocking them, and record that its error rates are unknown until week 6.
- *What would you need to see in production to let the escalation judge gate without the weekly spot check?* Several consecutive months of spot checks within the expected false-alarm count and no missed escalation found among its passes, plus a random-traffic TPR estimate whose interval clears the threshold.
- *The billing specialist leaves in week 4. Who becomes the ground truth, and what happens to labels she made?* Her written definitions and adjudicated examples become the reference; the replacement labels 40 of her traces blind, and the programme continues only if their agreement matches the support lead's.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants all six modes judged shallowly, the other two modes judged deeply. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Agree on the cost of one missed escalation against one wrong price, and let the expected cost per thousand conversations under each plan decide.

## Common mistakes

- Planning the label count from the failure rate on random traffic and discovering in week 4 that a 4% mode cannot fill a test set.
- Spending expert hours on failures a lookup against the pricing table can decide.
- Reporting production prevalence from flagged traffic.
- Tuning the judge on the test set, or re-running it after every prompt change until the numbers look right.
- Trusting a kappa computed on 40 traces with two escalation failures in them.
- Giving the stakeholder a flat no, which ends in an unvalidated judge shipped by someone else.
