## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"This report is going to the support director on Thursday with a proposal to retire the weekly human review. Review it."
</Callout>

The team, the report and every number in it are a scenario.

```text
Judge validation: support-reply quality judge, prompt v3

Data
- 500 replies sampled at random from last week's production traffic.
- Labelled pass or fail by Priya (support lead, replies 1-250) and Tom (support agent, 251-500).
- 22 replies labelled fail: wrong refund amount, promises a credit we cannot issue,
  exposes another customer's data, or tone.

Judge
- Model: judge-model-latest (same model family as the assistant, so it knows our house style).
- Reads the customer message and the reply; returns a quality score from 1 to 5. Score >= 4 counts as pass.
- Few-shot: 12 examples from the labelled set, chosen as the clearest failures.
- Prompt iterated six times against the labels; v3 had the best agreement.

Result
- Agreement with human labels: 94.2% (471 of 500).
- 29 disagreements. In 20 the judge was stricter than the labeller; we re-read 11 of those
  and agree with the judge, so effective agreement is about 96%.

Proposal
- Retire the weekly human review of 150 replies.
- Release gate: judge pass rate >= 95% on a nightly sample of 1,000 replies.
```

List the flaws, ranked by how much each could change the decision, before opening the checklist. Then say what you would send the director instead.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what agreement a judge that returns 5 for every reply would get on these 500.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>For each of the 500 labels, ask whether it was also used to write or choose the prompt.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what judge pass rate last week's replies would have produced through this judge.</p></details>

## Answer

**1. The judge is below the trivial baseline.** 22 of 500 replies fail, so a judge that passes everything agrees on 478, 95.6%. The report's 94.2% is lower. The counts in the report are enough to split it: 29 disagreements, 20 where the judge failed a human pass and so 9 where it passed a human failure. TPR = (22 − 9) / 22 = 13 / 22 = 59%; TNR = (478 − 20) / 478 = 458 / 478 = 95.8%. A 95% Wilson interval on 13 of 22 runs from about 39% to 77%: the judge catches somewhere between two in five and three in four failures. The llm-judge write-up names exactly this trap: on imbalanced data, raw agreement rewards a judge for the class balance.

**2. There is no test set.** Six prompt iterations chose v3 by its agreement on the same 500 labels, so 94.2% is a development number, selected as the best of six. The FAQ's split exists for this reason: iterate on dev, measure once on a test set the prompt never saw.

**3. The failures are in the prompt.** Twelve few-shot examples were taken from the labelled set, "the clearest failures". If they are among the 22, more than half of the failures the TPR was measured on were shown to the judge with their answers. On the unseen failures the TPR could be far below 59%, and with 10 or fewer of them nobody can say how far. This and flaw 5 are the two a reviewer reads past, because both are described as diligence.

**4. Four failure modes, one score.** A 1 to 5 quality score thresholded at 4 is one classifier for four different failures. With 22 failures across four headings, each heading has a handful of examples; the report cannot say whether the 9 misses are credit promises, which cost money, or tone. The FAQ's recommendation is one binary judgement per failure mode.

**5. One-sided adjudication.** Re-reading only the disagreements where the judge was stricter, and only to see whether the judge was right, can raise agreement and never lower it. The 9 cases where the judge passed a human failure were not re-read, and neither were any of the 471 agreements. Either re-read a random sample of all 500 blind to the judge, or report the original labels.

**6. The gate cannot pass last week's replies.** Using the measured rates, with last week's true pass rate of 95.6%, the expected judge pass rate is 0.956 × 0.958 + 0.044 × (1 − 0.59) = 0.916 + 0.018 = 93.4%. A gate at 95% blocks a release identical to last week's. The fix the team will reach for is lowering the threshold, which hides the second problem: the judge's pass rate moves by TPR + TNR − 1 = 0.55 points for each point of true quality, and a nightly sample of 1,000 has a 95% half-width of about 1.5 points at that rate (1.96 × √(0.934 × 0.066 / 1,000)). A two-point drop in true quality moves the gate's input by about 1.1 points, inside its own noise.

**7. Nothing pins the judge.** `judge-model-latest` changes when the provider moves the alias, and nothing in the proposal re-measures TPR and TNR when it does. Anthropic's guide lists model-based graders as needing calibration against human graders; a calibration on a model you no longer run is not one.

**8. The labels themselves.** Two people labelled separate halves with no overlap, so their agreement with each other is unknown, and a support agent and a support lead may draw the credit-promise line in different places. This is a judgement call, not a flaw by itself: it is acceptable if a shared sample measures their agreement and each heading has a written criterion. The FAQ prefers one domain expert as the single labeller where that is feasible.

**9. Self-judging.** The judge shares a model family with the assistant. Panickssery and colleagues found evaluators favour their own generations and that self-recognition tracks self-preference. Also a judgement call: acceptable if TPR and TNR are measured on held-out human labels with this exact pairing, because the measured rates already include any self-preference on this traffic, and are re-measured when either model changes.

**What to send the director: a decision with two defensible answers.**

- *Hold the proposal until a proper validation exists.* Relabel a failure-enriched held-out set per heading, at least 30 failures each for the two money headings, with one labeller and written criteria, and measure TPR and TNR once. Cost: the human review continues for the weeks this takes, and the support lead's time goes to labels instead of reviews.
- *Run the judge now as a monitor, not a gate.* Show its corrected pass rate with an interval next to a smaller weekly human review, and add code checks for the mechanical failures (refund amounts against the order record). Cost: a dashboard number resting on a TPR whose interval spans 38 points, and the risk that people start treating it as a gate before it is validated.

I would send the second with the first as its exit condition: the judge becomes a gate when the held-out TPR on the money headings is measured and above a threshold the director signs off on in advance. It fails in a predictable way: monitors that run for a quarter acquire authority they were never given, so the memo names the date and the measurement that end the monitor phase, and says the gate stays human until then.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the director says the human review costs the support lead's time every week and the judge costs almost nothing. Which part of the review do you keep, and what risk do you accept in writing?* Keep a smaller weekly sample whose purpose is fresh labels for re-measuring the judge; the written risk is that failures in headings without a validated judge are measured only by that sample.
- *Reversal: the held-out measurement comes back with TPR 85% on credit promises and 40% on tone. What would make you gate releases on the judge anyway?* Gate on the heading that is validated and leave tone to the human sample; one judge per heading makes that possible, one 1 to 5 score does not.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one says 94.2% is good enough to start, the other says a judge below baseline should be scrapped. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Agree on the TPR and TNR that would make each position right, then measure on held-out labels; agreement is not one of the numbers.

## Common mistakes

- Accepting 94.2% because it sounds high, without comparing it with the always-pass baseline.
- Asking for "more data" without saying which class: the 478 passes are plenty, the 22 failures are the limit.
- Missing that the few-shot failures came out of the evaluation set.
- Treating the re-read of 11 disagreements as extra rigour.
- Setting the gate threshold on the raw judge pass rate without computing what last week's replies would have scored.
