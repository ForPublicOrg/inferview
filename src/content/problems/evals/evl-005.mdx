## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Debug round, 20 minutes">
"Our fine-tuned support model beat the production model on our golden set and on two public benchmarks. A week after launch, refund complaints are up and users are being told things that are not true. Which explanation fits the evidence, how do you rank the others, and what single run settles it?"
</Callout>

The system and every number are a scenario. Production runs an open-weights model with a system prompt and a `policy_lookup` tool that reads the current refund rules. The candidate is the same model fine-tuned on support transcripts. The golden set runs the assistant with its tools against a staging copy of the policy service. The refund window changed from 30 days to 14 days on 2026-03-01.

The fine-tuning export manifest:

```yaml
export: resolved_tickets
range: 2024-07-01..2026-06-30
rows: 50000
filters: [resolved == true, csat >= 4]
fields: [customer_messages, agent_messages]
dedup: exact_match(customer_messages)
```

The golden set card:

```yaml
dataset: support-golden-v4
cases: 300
sampled: 2025-09-12
source: support_logs 2024-01-01..2025-08-31
references: refs-2025-09
categories: [refunds, shipping, account, billing, other]
```

The two runs that went into the launch review:

```text
run         model                judge     date        refunds  shipping  account  billing  other  total
baseline    prod-2026-01         judge-v2  2026-04-08  29/48    47/61     55/72    52/66    40/53  223/300
candidate   ft-support-2026-07   judge-v3  2026-07-14  41/48    48/61     56/72    52/66    40/53  237/300
```

Public benchmarks reported by the training team: both up by about one point.

Production, the week before launch against the week after:

```text
refund conversations calling policy_lookup     58%   ->  31%
mean answer length (tokens)                     96   ->  131
complaints quoting a 30-day refund window        4   ->  57
```

You do not have the outputs of either run side by side, the baseline graded by judge v3, or any check of the golden set against the export. Which explanation accounts for both the score gain and the complaints? Rank the rest, say what you would collect, and what you would ship today without it.

## Answer

**The golden set's refund references are stale, and the fine-tune learned the same stale answer.** The references were written in September 2025 under the 30-day window. The baseline was scored in April 2026, after the change, so when it called `policy_lookup` it got 14 days, answered correctly, and was marked wrong against the old reference. The fine-tune was trained on transcripts from July 2024 to June 2026. Assuming roughly even volume, 20 of those 24 months, about 83 percent, predate the change, and the `csat >= 4` filter keeps conversations where customers were happy with the answer they got, the old one. The export kept only customer and agent messages, so the model never saw a lookup happen before the agent stated a window; it learned to state the window. It now answers "30 days" from memory, the stale reference agrees, and the refund category rises from 29 to 41 while the other four categories move by two cases between them.

Why each other choice is tempting, ranked by how much of the symptom it carries:

1. **The lookup rate fell (D).** This is the production half of the same failure and the reason users see the wrong window. Alone it would leave the refund score flat, because nothing in the set checks that the answer came from the tool. It is second because fixing it is the fastest mitigation.
2. **Eval conversations in the training export (C).** The set was sampled from logs running to August 2025 and the export starts in July 2024, so up to fourteen months overlap, and exact-match dedup on customer messages inside the export does nothing about the eval set. This inflates the score and has to be measured, but it would lift every category roughly in proportion to its overlap, not one.
3. **Judge v3 against judge v2 (B).** Comparing runs graded by different judges is a defect on its own, and Zheng et al. report verbosity bias, so a 36 percent rise in answer length is worth checking. It would also lift every category.

The public benchmarks say nothing here in either direction: a one-point move on general tasks is not evidence about a support assistant's refund answers, and public sets can be contaminated the same way, which is OpenAI's stated reason for no longer reporting SWE-bench Verified.

**The run that separates them.** One eval run over the stored outputs of both models: same judge (v3), references for the refund-window cases corrected to 14 days, results reported per category and split into cases with and without a near-duplicate in the export. If A is right, the candidate falls below the baseline on refunds once the references are fixed. If B is right, the gap shrinks evenly across categories when both share a judge. If C is right, the gain concentrates in the overlapping cases in every category. Use an LLM-based near-duplicate check, not only n-gram overlap; Yang et al. show paraphrased items pass n-gram decontamination.

**What to collect.** The run above. A sample of 50 refund conversations from the week after launch with tool calls, read by hand against current policy. The export pipeline's code, to confirm tool messages were dropped by design rather than by a bug.

**What to ship today without it.** Make the orchestration layer call `policy_lookup` for every refund intent and put the result in the context, whichever model runs. It is cheap, it is right under every explanation above, and it does not depend on the diagnosis. Rolling refund intents back to the baseline is the alternative: it fixes refunds today at the cost of losing whatever the fine-tune improved elsewhere, which the evidence does not yet show to be anything.

**Where this diagnosis can mislead you.** Correcting the refund-window references only fixes the cases someone thought of. Any reference that restates a policy, a price or a limit can go stale the same way, so the set needs every such case tagged with the policy it depends on and re-checked when that policy changes. Until then, a clean refund score after the fix proves less than it appears to.

## Follow-ups

- *The training team says re-exporting with tool messages and filtering pre-change transcripts costs three weeks. What do you ship in those three weeks, and what risk do you write down?*
- *After the regrade, the candidate is two cases ahead outside refunds and the overlap split shows those two are both overlapping cases. What do you tell the launch review?*
- *How would you tag 300 cases with the policies they depend on without reading all 300, and how do you keep the tags current?*

## Common mistakes

- Comparing two runs graded by different judges, a month apart, and reading the difference as a model change.
- Deduplicating inside the training export and assuming that also protects the eval set.
- Treating a reference answer as permanent, when it encodes the policy of the day it was written.
- Fixing the eval set and leaving the stale transcripts and the stripped tool messages in the next export.
