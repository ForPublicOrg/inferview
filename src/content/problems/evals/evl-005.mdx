## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As a public question bank phrases it">
"Your new model version scores higher on every benchmark, but users say it got worse. Why does this happen, and how do you find the problem?"
</Callout>

A synthetic version with evidence. A team fine-tunes an open-weights model on an export of 50,000 support transcripts from the last two years. Their internal eval is 300 questions sampled from "the same support logs" by a different engineer, graded by an LLM judge. After fine-tuning, the internal score and two public benchmarks all go up. A week after launch, users complain that answers are confident and wrong, and several quote last year's refund policy. You run a near-duplicate check between the eval questions and the training transcripts:

```text
                               before fine-tune   after fine-tune
eval cases overlapping training   22 / 41            39 / 41
eval cases with no overlap       201 / 259          198 / 259
total                            223 / 300          237 / 300
```

Pick the root cause.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Split the 14-case gain in the total by the two rows. Where did it come from?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>What did the model learn from two-year-old transcripts that a current user would notice?</p></details>

## Answer

The eval set leaked into the training data. 41 of the 300 questions have near-verbatim matches in the fine-tuning transcripts, and the whole improvement lives there: those cases went from 22 to 39 passes, a gain of 17, while the 259 clean cases went from 201 to 198, a loss of 3. The headline moved from 223/300 (74.3%) to 237/300 (79.0%), but on clean data the model went from 77.6% to 76.4%. The same training data explains the complaints: two years of transcripts include answers under a refund policy that has since changed, and the model learned them.

Why the wrong picks are tempting. Judge bias towards longer, more confident answers is real and worth testing, but it would lift both rows, not concentrate the gain in the overlapping cases. "Benchmarks measure general skills" is true and a good thing to say in the interview, but your internal set rose too, and that rise is what needs explaining. Novelty aversion exists, but complaints quoting an outdated policy are a correctness signal.

<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Contamination. The internal eval overlaps the fine-tuning data, so the score rise is memorised answers; on the clean subset the model got slightly worse. Training on old transcripts also taught it an outdated refund policy. I would deduplicate the eval set against training data before every run, report clean-subset scores, filter or relabel stale transcripts, and add cases that test the current policy.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Find it. Run near-duplicate detection between the eval set and the training data (normalised n-gram overlap or embedding similarity), and score the overlapping and clean subsets separately. A gain concentrated in the overlap is memorisation.</p>
<p>Fix the eval. Remove or quarantine overlapping cases, rebuild from logs dated after the training cutoff, and keep a held-out slice no one tunes against. Make the deduplication a CI step that runs whenever either dataset changes.</p>
<p>Fix the model. Filter transcripts that contradict current policy, or weight recent data, and retrieve the policy at answer time rather than relying on what the weights remember. Add golden cases that ask about the current refund policy, so this regression would have failed a gate.</p>
<p>Public benchmarks. Treat them as a sanity check on general ability, not as a release criterion for a support bot. A public set can be contaminated the same way, which is why OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified in February 2026.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *Why did nobody notice before launch?* The eval was sampled from "the same logs" with no check against the training export. Data lineage for eval sets is as important as for training sets.
- *How do you choose eval cases so this cannot happen?* Time-split: train on data before a cutoff and evaluate on data after it.
- *What else would you monitor after the fix?* Complaint categories and policy-related failures per week, and the clean-subset score on every model version.

## Common mistakes

- Celebrating a gain without asking where in the set it came from.
- Blaming users or "vibes" when complaints name a specific, checkable error.
- Fixing only the eval set and leaving the stale transcripts in the next fine-tune.
- Trusting public benchmark gains as evidence for a domain-specific product.
