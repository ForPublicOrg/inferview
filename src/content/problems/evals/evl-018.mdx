## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As a public interview guide phrases it">
"How do you know your system is good?" Then: "Show me an eval you built."
</Callout>

Prepare a five-minute story about one eval you built for an LLM feature. The interviewer will interrupt with "how did you know that?" at least once. Use your own work; the answer below is a structure and a checklist, not a story to borrow.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Start from the failure you were trying to catch, not from the tool you used.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>The strongest stories include a moment when the eval was wrong and you found out.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>End with a decision the eval changed.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A good answer names one product, one failure mode found by reading real traces, the check built for it (code if possible, a judge validated against labels if not), the evidence that the check itself was trustworthy, and one concrete decision it changed, such as a blocked release or a prompt that was reverted. Then one sentence on what you would do differently.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Situation.</b> The feature, its users, and why quality was in question. Keep it to two sentences.</p>
<p><b>How you found the failure.</b> The traces you read, how many, how you grouped them, and the category that mattered most. This is where "how do you know your system is good?" gets its real answer: you know because you looked, and you can say what bad looks like.</p>
<p><b>What you built.</b> The dataset (where cases came from, how many, how labelled), the grader (an assertion, or a binary judge with its prompt design), and where it runs (on every pull request, nightly, online on sampled traffic).</p>
<p><b>Why you trusted it.</b> For a judge, TPR and TNR on a held-out labelled set, with failure as the positive class. For a dataset, how you checked it resembled production. Mention a time the eval was wrong, such as a grader that rejected correct outputs, and how you caught it.</p>
<p><b>What it changed.</b> A release blocked, a regression caught, a model choice made, or a cost cut with quality held. Numbers from your own work, with their sample sizes.</p>
<p><b>Reflection.</b> What you would change: earlier error analysis, fewer judges, a held-out slice, better logging.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>How did you know the judge was right?</em> Have the confusion matrix ready: how many labelled failures, how many it caught, how many good outputs it wrongly failed.</li>
<li><em>What did the eval miss?</em> Name a failure that reached users anyway and what you added afterwards.</li>
<li><em>How much did it cost to run?</em> Calls per run, runs per week, and what you did to keep it affordable.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

### A skeleton to fill with your own details

The bracketed parts are placeholders, not facts to repeat.

"On [product], users reported [symptom]. I read [number] recent traces and found [category] was the most common failure, in [number] of them. I built [assertion or judge] over [number] cases taken from those traces, and validated the judge against [number] labels from [expert]: it caught [x of y] failures and passed [x of y] good outputs. It runs [where]. The first week it blocked [change], because [cases] flipped. Since then [outcome]. If I did it again I would [change]."

### What interviewers listen for

Concrete nouns and numbers from your own work, said with their sample sizes. A failure mode named in plain words. Evidence that the eval was checked against human judgement. And judgement about cost: you did not build twelve judges when two assertions would do. Weak answers name a framework, a metric like "helpfulness 4.2 out of 5" and nothing that changed because of it.

## Follow-ups

- *You have never built a formal eval. What do you say?* Describe how you decided a change was safe to ship, what you looked at, and what you would build now. Do not invent a project.
- *Your team disagreed with what the eval said. What happened?* A good story shows you read the flipped cases with them and either fixed the eval or changed their mind with examples.
- *How would you scale this to ten features?* Shared tracing, a shared dataset format, and one owner per golden set, with the same gate structure across features.

## Common mistakes

- Leading with tools ("we used framework X") instead of the failure and the decision.
- Quoting a single average score with no sample size and no validation.
- Telling a story where the eval was never wrong. Every eval is wrong somewhere, and the follow-ups will find where.
- Borrowing a story. Follow-up questions about labels and numbers expose it quickly.
