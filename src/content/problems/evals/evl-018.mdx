## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As a recruiter's guide phrases it">
"How do you know your system is good?" Then: "Show me an eval you built."
</Callout>

Five minutes on one eval from your own work. Then the interviewer takes the other side: "That eval measured the wrong thing. Your users did not care about what it scored." They have read your account closely enough to pick the weakest link and will hold the position for several minutes.

Prepare both halves from your own work. The answer below is the shape of a strong account and of the defence, with no story to borrow.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which part they think is wrong: the cases, the grader, or the decision the number was used for.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Before the round, find your judge's confusion matrix on held-out labels and the date it was last rechecked.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what they would have measured instead, and what labelling it would have cost.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The account names one failure found by reading traces, with how many traces and how often the failure appeared; the check built for it, code where code could see it and a binary judge validated against held-out labels where it could not; and one decision the eval changed. The defence first locates the objection, because "the wrong thing" means one of four different faults, each with its own evidence. Then it either narrows the claim to what the eval did measure and names the check that was missing, or shows the measured link between the eval and the outcome users care about. Pick by evidence, not by attachment.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The account.</b> Product, users and the decision at stake in two sentences. How the failure was found: traces read, open notes, categories with counts, and why this category came first (frequency, cost per occurrence, or both). What was built: where the cases came from, how they were labelled and by whom, the grader, and where it runs. Why it was trusted: for a judge, true positive and true negative rates on a held-out labelled set, with failure as the positive class; for cases, how their mix was compared with production. A time it was wrong and how that surfaced. The decision it changed: a blocked release, a reverted prompt, a model choice, with sample sizes on any number quoted.</p>
<p><b>Locating the objection.</b> <em>Construct:</em> the eval measures a proxy, such as answer correctness, while users care about something else, such as resolution without a second contact. <em>Sample:</em> the cases no longer resemble production. <em>Instrument:</em> the grader passes failures or fails good outputs. <em>Use:</em> the number was used for a decision it cannot support, such as ranking two prompts inside its noise. Each has different evidence: outcome data for construct, a mix comparison for sample, the confusion matrix for instrument, the interval for use.</p>
<p><b>Then decide.</b> Concede and narrow, or defend with the link. The section below gives both with their costs.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>How did you know the judge was right?</em> The held-out confusion matrix: labelled failures, how many it caught, how many good outputs it failed, and when that was last rechecked.</li>
<li><em>What did the eval miss?</em> A failure that reached users anyway, and what changed in the eval afterwards.</li>
<li><em>What did it cost to run and to keep labelled?</em> Calls per run, runs per week, and the expert hours per month spent relabelling.</li>
</ul>

### The decision under pushback

Both answers are defensible; the evidence decides.

**Concede and narrow the claim.** Say what the eval does measure, relabel it accordingly, keep it as a regression gate for that failure, and name the check that should sit beside it for what users care about. The cost: the eval loses its standing as a quality number, months of trend lines stop meaning what the dashboard said, and the team that relied on it now has a gap until the new check exists, which needs labelling time nobody budgeted.

**Defend with the link.** Show that the failure the eval scores moves the outcome users care about: traces the eval failed had more repeat contacts, escalations or refunds than traces it passed, on the same period, with counts. If that evidence exists, the objection is answered on data. The cost: it takes outcome data joined to traces, which many teams do not have, and without it the defence is assertion, and continuing to gate on a number with no demonstrated link is a choice to accept that risk.

The constraint that usually forces the choice is time against labels: the eval gates a release next week, and the replacement check needs a few hundred labelled traces from an expert who has a few hours a week. The plan for that week goes in writing: keep gating on the narrow check under its narrow name, add a manual review of a fixed number of outcome-relevant traces per release, and date the replacement.

**Where conceding goes wrong.** Narrowing every claim until it is unassailable produces a suite of checks so specific that no one reads them and a release gate that passes everything users still complain about. Criteria drift cuts the other way too: Shankar et al. found that grading outputs changes the criteria people grade by, so an eval defined before anyone read outputs is likely to need revising, and one revised after every objection never settles. The guard is a written definition per check, revised on a schedule from error analysis, not in the meeting where it was challenged.

### Signals to rate your answer against

- One failure, found in traces, with counts and a sample size.
- A check whose trustworthiness was measured, not assumed.
- A decision the eval changed, and a time it was wrong.
- The objection located before it was answered.
- A choice between narrowing and defending, made on evidence, with what was given up stated.

## Follow-ups

- *Your eval costs more to run and keep labelled than the feature it gates earns. Which half do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?* Name the checks whose failure mode has not fired in months and move them from every pull request to a nightly or weekly run; keep the ones with a recent catch; write down which regressions can now reach users for up to a week.
- *What would you need to see in production to retire this eval?* Outcome data showing that its failures do not predict repeat contacts or escalations once the newer checks exist, over enough traces that the comparison has an interval narrow enough to act on.
- *Two senior engineers disagree about whether the eval measured the wrong thing. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Agree beforehand on what result would settle it, such as the outcome difference between traces the eval passes and fails, pull that data, and let it decide; if the data cannot be pulled, run a blind labelling session on 50 traces with both definitions and compare agreement.
- *You built the eval two years ago and the person challenging it now owns the product. Who changes the definition?* The owner of the outcome decides what matters; the owner of the eval decides how it is measured and says what the measurement can and cannot support.

## Common mistakes

- Leading with the framework or the dashboard instead of the failure and the decision.
- Quoting an average score with no sample size and no check of the grader.
- Telling an account in which the eval was never wrong.
- Answering "the wrong thing" before asking which of cases, grader, construct or use is meant.
- Defending the eval because it took months to build; the cost of building it is not evidence that it measures the right thing.
- Borrowing someone else's project. Follow-ups about labels and counts go deeper than a borrowed account can.
