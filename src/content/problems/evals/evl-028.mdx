## Question

The company and its numbers are a scenario. An online retailer wants its support agent to issue refunds on its own. Today a person handles every refund ticket at a loaded cost of about $6 a ticket; the agent costs about $0.40 a conversation in tokens. Launch is in eight weeks, with three engineers.

Two stakeholders disagree. The VP of support wants refunds up to $150 issued without a person, because refund tickets are the largest queue. The finance controller wants a person to approve every refund and says the agent must "never issue an unauthorised refund". Refund policy has eleven rules (window, condition, receipt, payment method, prior refunds on the account and so on), written for people.

Design the evaluation that decides whether, and how far, the agent is allowed to refund: the task set, how the customer side is played, the graders, the reliability metric and its k, cost per resolved task, and the release gate. Write the rollout note you would send both stakeholders, with rollback triggers.

<Callout kind="interview" label="The pushback">
"Finance said never. Your eval runs a few thousand conversations. How do you put 'never' in a release gate?"
</Callout>

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask finance what a wrong refund costs on average, and how often people on the current team issue one.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask support how many times a customer typically contacts them about the same refund before it is resolved.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Request a hundred transcripts of real refund conversations and count how often the customer, not the agent, has to do something: find a receipt, return an item, confirm in the app.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Grade the end state, and grade the path only where the path is the requirement. Each task carries the goal database state (the right refund, of the right amount, on the right order, or no refund) plus a small trajectory check: no refund before identity verification and an order lookup, no second refund on an order, nothing above the cap. Run each task eight times and gate on pass^4 per refund intent, because a customer who comes back four times must get the same answer four times. Report cost per resolved ticket including the human fallbacks. "Never" is not something an eval can certify: zero violations in n runs only bounds the rate near 3 in n. So the cap and the verification rule are enforced in the refund tool, and the eval measures how often the agent attempts what the tool blocks. Launch with autonomy below a $50 cap and human approval above it; widen the cap only on production evidence.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Tasks.</b> Start from real refund tickets: about 60 tasks covering every one of the eleven rules at least twice, once where it permits and once where it forbids, plus tasks where the right outcome is no refund, a partial refund, or an escalation. For each, write the goal state the way tau-bench does: the database after a correct conversation, compared field by field, with the information the customer must be told checked separately. Tasks where several outcomes are acceptable list all of them; Anthropic reports a tau2-bench task that a model failed as written while finding a better solution for the user, which is what a single goal state does to a policy with discretion in it.</p>
<p><b>The customer side: the decision with two defensible answers.</b> An LLM-simulated customer, as in tau-bench and tau2-bench, gives breadth and repeatability: thousands of conversations, personas that lie about receipts or push past a refusal, and a customer who can act on a shared environment, which matters because in tau2-bench's dual-control setting pass^1 fell by 18 points for gpt-4.1 and 25 for o4-mini when the user had to act. Its cost is the simulator's own errors: the tau2-bench authors found task-critical simulator errors in 13 of 100 airline conversations they annotated, and a simulator that hands over the order number unprompted makes the agent look better than it is. Scripted replays of real conversations, or support staff role-playing customers, give fidelity at a few dozen conversations a week and break as soon as the agent asks something the script did not anticipate. I would run the simulator for the volume and the gate, and spend the role-play budget on calibration: each week, compare outcomes on twenty tasks played by staff and by the simulator, and read every transcript where they disagree. What I give up is the confidence that the gate's number reflects real customers; the calibration sample tells me how far off it is, not that it is right.</p>
<p><b>Graders.</b> The end-state check carries the score. The trajectory checks are few and each is a requirement on its own: refund only after successful verification and lookup, at most one refund per order, amount at or under the cap, no refund on a forbidden payment method. A natural-language check, judged by a model, covers whether the customer was told the refund amount and timeline; it is validated against a hundred labelled transcripts before it counts. Partial credit (verified and looked up but did not refund) is recorded for diagnosis and never counts as a pass.</p>
<p><b>Reliability: the k I choose.</b> pass^k should use the number of times a customer meets the agent about one problem. If support's answer to Hint 2 is "two to four contacts" (a scenario figure), k = 4 is the defensible choice: a policy the agent applies in three of four conversations is a policy customers learn to retry against. k = 1 hides exactly that; k = 8 is defensible for the highest-value refunds but needs at least eight trials per task and doubles the run cost. With eight trials per task, 60 tasks are 480 conversations a run.</p>
<p><b>What the eval cannot show.</b> If n runs show zero unauthorised refunds, the 95% upper bound on the rate solves (1 − q)^n = 0.05, which is about 3 / n. At 480 conversations that is 0.6%; to bound it at 1 in 1,000 takes about 3,000 clean conversations, roughly $1,200 at $0.40 for the agent alone, before the simulator's tokens. That is affordable once, and still says nothing about one in a hundred thousand. So "never" moves out of the eval and into the tool: the refund API rejects calls without a verified session and an order lookup in the same conversation, rejects amounts above the cap, and is idempotent per order. The eval then measures attempts the tool blocked, which is the leading indicator the controller can watch.</p>
<p><b>Cost per resolved task.</b> If the agent resolves 70% of refund tickets and hands the rest to a person (scenario figures), a ticket costs $0.40 + 0.30 × $6 = $2.20 against $6 today, and the cost per ticket the agent resolves is $2.20 / 0.70 = $3.14 when the fallbacks are charged to it. Add the expected cost of wrong refunds from the gate's measured rate and Hint 1's average loss; at a $50 cap that term is bounded per ticket, at $150 it is three times larger for the same rate.</p>
<p><b>The failure mode of this design.</b> The simulator is the weakest component and it fails in the agent's favour more often than against it: simulated customers rarely lie well, rarely change their story mid-conversation and never escalate to a chargeback. An agent can pass this gate and be talked into refunds by the first persistent customer. The adversarial personas, the tool-level enforcement and a production rollback trigger on blocked-attempt rate exist because the gate will be optimistic.</p>
</Fragment>

### The rollout note

> **Decision.** The agent issues refunds up to $50 on its own from week 9; refunds above $50 are drafted by the agent and approved by a person. The refund tool enforces verification, order lookup, the cap and one refund per order, so the agent cannot exceed them even when it tries.
> **Gate for week 9.** pass^4 on each refund intent at or above the human team's measured accuracy on the same tasks; zero executed violations across all runs; blocked attempts below 1% of refund conversations; simulator and role-play outcomes agree on at least 18 of 20 calibration tasks.
> **Rollout.** Two weeks in shadow mode (the agent drafts, a person executes, every disagreement is logged), then 10% of refund tickets, then 50%, each step held for a week.
> **Rollback triggers.** Any executed refund without verification found in the nightly trajectory audit; blocked attempts above 2% of refund conversations on any day; re-contact rate on agent-resolved refunds above the human baseline for three days. Rollback means drafting mode for all refunds, which the support team can switch without a deploy.
> **What we give up.** Refunds between $50 and $150 keep a person in the loop, so the largest queue shrinks by less than the VP planned. In return, the worst case per wrong autonomous refund is capped at $50.
> **Owners.** Finance signs the cap and any change to it; support owns the re-contact metric; the agent team owns the gate and the audit.

## Follow-ups

- *The model vendor ships a new version in week 6. What reruns, and what can you skip?* The full gate reruns, because pass^k and blocked-attempt rates are properties of this model on these tasks; the calibration against role-play can reuse last week's sample if the simulator did not change.
- *A customer finds that saying their receipt was emailed to an old address gets a refund. Where should that have been caught?* In an adversarial persona in the task set; after the fact, it becomes a task with a forbidding goal state and a red-team persona that tries it in every run.
- *Your simulator's outcome disagrees with staff role-play on 6 of 20 tasks. Do you fix the simulator or stop trusting the gate?* Read the six transcripts first; if the simulator is too cooperative, the gate is optimistic and stays closed until it is fixed.

## Common mistakes

- Grading the agent's final message ("I've processed your refund") instead of the refund record in the database.
- Checking the whole step order against a golden path, which fails correct conversations.
- Reporting pass^1 for a policy customers can retry against.
- Promising "never" from an eval run instead of enforcing the rule in the refund tool.
- Computing cost per resolved ticket from the agent's token cost alone, without the human fallbacks and wrong refunds.
- Trusting a simulated customer without measuring how often it makes the agent's job easier than a real one would.
