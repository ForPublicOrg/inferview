## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Debug round">
A billing-support assistant ships behind an LLM-judge release gate. Compliance keeps finding bad replies in production, and the product lead points out that the judge "agrees with our analyst 92% of the time". Using the evidence below, which explanation accounts for the symptom? Rank the other defects, say what you would collect next, and say what you would change first, before the evidence comes in.
</Callout>

The system and its numbers are a scenario. The assistant sends about 15,000 replies a day. The quarterly compliance review is in three weeks; the compliance analyst can give the team two days before it.

```text
Release gate (in force since 2026-04-14)
  Judge: policy-accuracy, prompt v2, model alias judge-large
  Rule: ship if the judge passes >= 95% of a nightly sample of 2,000 replies

Validation summary (2026-04-14)
  400 replies, random sample of March traffic, labelled pass/fail by the compliance analyst
  Human-labelled failures: 30
  Agreement: 92.0% (368 of 400)
  Replies and labels kept; per-item judge verdicts not kept

Changes since validation
  2026-07-01  refund window for annual plans changed from 30 to 14 days;
              prompt v2 still contains the policy text of 2026-03-01
  2026-09-02  provider moved alias judge-large to a new snapshot

Dashboard, nightly judge pass rate, May to October
  weekly means between 96.1% and 97.0%; no step in July or September

Compliance audit (2026-10-10)
  200 random September replies, analyst-labelled: 15 failing (7.5%)

Complaint sample
  50 replies customers escalated, May to October, all labelled failing by the analyst
  Judge verdicts today: pass 41, fail 9
    promises a credit the agent cannot issue   31   (judge passed 26)
    states the wrong account status            12   (judge passed 9)
    quotes the wrong refund window              7   (judge passed 6)
  Of the 41 passes, 28 are replies sent before 2026-09-02
```

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask what nightly judge pass rate a judge that catches most failures would produce at the audit's failure share, and compare it with the dashboard.</p></details>

## Answer

**The cause: a judge that rarely says fail, hidden by the base rate.** The April set had 30 failures in 400, so an always-pass judge would have agreed on 370, 92.5%. The judge's 92.0% is below that. Agreement alone cannot fix its TPR: 32 disagreements fit a judge that missed 24 failures and wrongly failed 8 passes (TPR 0.20, TNR 0.978) as well as one that missed 6 and wrongly failed 26 (TPR 0.80, TNR 0.930).

The dashboard decides between them. At the audit's 7.5% failure share, the expected judge pass rate is (1 − f) × TNR + f × (1 − TPR). For the high-recall judge: 0.925 × 0.930 + 0.075 × 0.20 = 0.875, a dashboard near 87.5%. For the low-recall judge: 0.925 × 0.978 + 0.075 × 0.80 = 0.965, which is what the dashboard shows. The complaint sample agrees: the judge passed 41 of 50 known failures, a miss rate of 82%. Complaints are a skewed sample of failures, the ones customers noticed, so this is supporting evidence, not a measurement of TPR.

**Why the gate never fired.** With TPR 0.20 and TNR 0.978, the judge's pass rate moves by TPR + TNR − 1 = 0.18 points per point of true quality. If true quality fell from 92.5% passing to 85%, the judge's pass rate would go from 96.5% to 0.85 × 0.978 + 0.15 × 0.80 = 95.2%, still above the 95% gate. The same 0.18 is why correcting the judge's pass rate with judgy's formula will not rescue it: dividing by 0.18 multiplies the nightly sample's noise by more than five.

**The ranking.**

1. **Low recall hidden by imbalance.** Explains the symptom from April onwards, the flat dashboard and the complaint passes.
2. **The stale refund policy in the prompt.** Real and certain to recur: every policy change silently invalidates the judge's reference. It explains at most 6 of the 41 misses and nothing before July.
3. **The unpinned alias.** Real: the judge the team validated in April is not the judge running today, and nothing triggered a re-measurement. But 28 of the 41 passed complaints predate the move and the dashboard has no step, so it is not the cause. It does mean any re-measurement must use today's snapshot.
4. **The labeller's definition.** Not supported here: the same analyst labelled the complaints as failures.

**What to collect.** First, judge the 400 April replies again with today's judge and compute TPR and TNR directly against the kept labels: 30 failures is thin, so add the 15 audit failures and their 185 passes for 45 failures. That is machine time and no analyst time. Second, split the result by heading, because 31 of 50 complaints are credit promises. Third, re-run the April replies on the old snapshot if the provider still serves it, which separates the alias move from everything else.

**What to change first, without that evidence: a decision with two defensible answers.**

- *Freeze releases until the judge is re-measured and fixed.* Nothing ships under a gate that is known to be blind. Cost: every product change waits; the re-measurement is fast, but a fixed judge needs a prompt rewrite and a fresh validation, which competes with the compliance review for the same two analyst days.
- *Take the release decision away from the judge today and keep shipping.* Replace it with a deterministic check for the dominant failure, credit promises compared against what the agent is entitled to issue, plus a 200-reply analyst audit before each release. Cost: analyst time on every release, and a weak audit: at a 7.5% failure share, 200 replies give a standard error of about 1.9 points, so a regression from 7.5% to 10% is inside the noise.

The constraint conflict is the compliance deadline against the analyst's two days. I would take the second path, because the code check removes most of the complaint volume without any labels, and spend the analyst's two days on per-heading labels for a rewritten judge rather than on audits. How it fails: a credit-promise check that looks for amounts and the word "credit" misses paraphrased promises such as "we will take that off your next bill", so it needs its own measured recall on the 31 complaint examples before it is trusted, and the audit stays as the backstop for everything the check does not cover.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the product lead says the judge passes 96.5% of replies and compliance's own audit says 92.5% pass, which is close enough. What do you say, with numbers?* The two numbers are close because the judge almost never says fail, not because it measures quality. The number to show is how far the judge's rate moves when quality moves: 0.18 points per point, so a fall from 92.5% to 85% true passes leaves the gate open.
- *Reversal: the re-run on the April set shows TPR 0.75 on today's snapshot. What does that change in your diagnosis, and what do you look at next?* It would contradict the dashboard arithmetic, so something differs between April's replies and today's: the mix of headings, or credit promises that did not exist in March. Split by heading and by month before trusting either number.
- *Operating: the provider will retire the judge-large snapshot in sixty days, and the rewritten judge is not ready. Do you keep the old judge until retirement, switch to the new snapshot unvalidated, or run without a judge, and who signs off?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants to fix the judge, the other wants to replace it with per-heading code checks and a human audit. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Measure the code check's recall on the complaint examples for each heading. Where code reaches the recall the team needs, code wins on cost and determinism; the judge stays only for the headings code cannot decide.

## Common mistakes

- Picking the alias change because it is the most recent change, without checking the dates on the misses.
- Fixing the stale policy text and declaring the incident closed.
- Re-thresholding the gate at a higher judge pass rate, which moves a blind instrument's cut-off.
- Reading the complaint sample's 82% miss rate as the judge's TPR, when complaints are the failures customers noticed.
