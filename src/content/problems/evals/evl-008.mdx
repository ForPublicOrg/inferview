## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As a public question bank phrases the follow-on">
"Pairwise comparison or absolute scoring?"
</Callout>

The setup and its numbers are a scenario. You are choosing between two system prompts for a ticket summariser. A teammate ran a pairwise LLM judge over 300 tickets from last month and reports that prompt A won 192 of 300, 64%. The judge runs at temperature 0. This is the loop:

```python
JUDGE_TEMPLATE = """You are reviewing two summaries of the same support ticket.
Rubric: {rubric}

Summary 1:
{summary_1}

Summary 2:
{summary_2}

Answer with the number of the better summary, 1 or 2, and nothing else."""

wins_a = 0
for ticket in tickets:                       # 300 tickets, last month
    a = summarise(ticket, PROMPT_A)
    b = summarise(ticket, PROMPT_B)
    reply = judge_model.complete(JUDGE_TEMPLATE.format(rubric=RUBRIC, summary_1=a, summary_2=b))
    wins_a += reply.strip() == "1"
print(wins_a / len(tickets))
```

Before anyone ships prompt A, which experiment tells you whether the 64% comes from the prompts or from the judge?

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask for the judge's answers on the tickets where both prompts produced the same summary, word for word.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how different the two summaries are on a typical ticket: a few words, or a different structure?</p></details>

## Answer

Read the loop: `summary_1=a, summary_2=b` on every ticket. A judge that prefers slot 1 produces a win for A even if the prompts are equal, so the 64% is preference and position bias added together, in unknown shares. Judging every ticket a second time with B in slot 1 and counting A's win only when both orders agree, B's only when both agree and the rest as ties, separates them. MT-Bench measured how large the effect can be: with the default prompt and the order swapped, GPT-4 gave the same verdict in 65.0% of cases and favoured the first position in 30.0%; Claude-v1 was consistent in 23.8%. A study of 15 judges over more than 150,000 instances found the bias depends on the quality gap between the two answers, which is the case for two prompts for the same summarisation task. The control costs one extra judge call per ticket.

Why each wrong option is tempting. Temperature 0 feels like rigour, and a stable result feels like a real one, but a deterministic rerun reproduces the confound exactly. A larger judge feels like a better instrument; MT-Bench's strongest judge still had the bias, and in this loop any judge sees A first. More comparisons shrink the interval around a quantity that may be measuring the slot.

### Pairwise or pointwise, after the swap

If A still wins clearly on consistent verdicts, the preference is real. The next decision is whether pairwise was the right format at all. Pairwise with both orders suits a relative question such as which tone readers prefer. A pointwise binary judge per failure mode suits a release gate, because a pairwise win cannot say whether either summary is good enough. Tripathi and colleagues (COLM 2025) found pairwise preferences flipped in about 35% of cases when distractor features were added, against about 9% for absolute scores, which is an argument for pointwise gates even when pairwise comparisons are kept for preference.

## Follow-ups

- *After swapping, 40% of tickets are ties. What do you report?* The consistent wins for each prompt and the tie share. A high tie share says the judge cannot separate the prompts on those tickets; it is not evidence for either.
- *Your teammate proposes randomising the order once per ticket instead of judging both orders, to halve the cost. What do you gain and lose?* The aggregate win rate becomes unbiased by position, at half the calls. You lose the per-ticket consistency signal, so you can no longer see which tickets the judge cannot decide.
- *How would you check for length bias in the same data?* Compare the lengths of the winning and losing summaries on consistent verdicts, then re-judge a sample with the lengths equalised.

## Common mistakes

- Fixing the order in the loop, which is the default in most quick scripts.
- Treating a pairwise win for A as evidence that A is good rather than better than B.
- Reading temperature 0 as a control for bias.
