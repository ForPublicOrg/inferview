## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As a public question bank phrases it">
"Your eval reports 82% pass on 100 examples. What does that number not tell you?"
</Callout>

A worked version, with scenario numbers constructed for this problem. Your assistant's regression suite has 100 cases drawn from production traces. The current prompt passes 82. A candidate prompt passes 86 on the same 100 cases: 7 cases went from fail to pass and 3 from pass to fail.

1. Say what 82 of 100 tells you and what it does not, starting with its 95% interval.
2. The team wants a suite that detects a real 5-point improvement, two-sided at the 5% level. Choose the power you want and the rate of cases that change outcome between two prompts, and **estimate how many cases the suite needs**. Then say whether it is worth building, given that a reviewer writes and labels about 20 cases an hour (scenario figure).

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether both prompts ran on the same cases, and how many cases changed outcome between them.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request a rerun of the current prompt against itself and count the cases that flip with nothing changed.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what size of improvement would change the ship decision.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>82 of 100 has a Wilson 95% interval of about 73% to 88%, so the candidate's 86 sits inside it, and the paired view agrees: 7 fixes against 3 breaks has an exact two-sided p of 0.34. To detect a 5-point gain, size the suite from the share of cases that flip between prompts, not from the pass rate: with the observed 10% flip rate and 80% power that is about 310 cases, and with a more cautious 15% and 90% power about 630. The number also says nothing about which failures make up the 18, whether the cases still look like production, or whether the grader is right.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The dominant term is the flip rate. Pairing removes everything the two prompts agree on: a case both pass or both fail carries no information about the difference. Only the cases that change outcome do, so the variance of the per-case difference is about the flip rate, and the suite size scales linearly with it and with one over the square of the difference you want to see.</p>
<p>The assumption that flips the conclusion is pairing. Treat the two runs as independent samples and the same 5-point goal needs about 820 cases for each prompt at 80% power, more than twice the paired figure, and a team that does that arithmetic decides the suite is unaffordable when it is not.</p>
<p>The second assumption is that flips are signal. With temperature above zero, the same prompt run twice flips some cases on its own. If the self-rerun flips 6 of 100, the noise floor is most of the 10 flips seen, and the planning flip rate should be the measured one between prompts, with several samples per case to shrink the noise before adding cases.</p>
<p>What the number hides beyond the interval: the 18 failures could be one bug or 18; the cases were drawn from production at some date and drift from it after; and a pass is whatever the grader says, so an unvalidated judge moves the centre of the interval, which no sample size fixes.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Thirty of the 100 cases are paraphrases of five questions. What changes?</em> Cluster standard errors on the question, or count each cluster once; the effective sample is smaller than 100 and the interval wider.</li>
<li><em>Why not just run the 100 cases five times each?</em> Repeats shrink per-case noise, which lowers the flip rate between prompts; they do not add new inputs, so they cannot narrow uncertainty about cases the suite does not contain.</li>
<li><em>The product lead asks for one number on the dashboard.</em> The pass rate with its interval and n, and the per-category failure counts beneath it.</li>
</ul>

### Part 1, the interval

Normal approximation: standard error √(0.82 × 0.18 / 100) = 0.0384; half-width 1.96 × 0.0384 = 0.075; interval 74.5% to 89.5%.

Wilson score interval with z = 1.96, z² = 3.8416, n = 100: centre (0.82 + 3.8416 / 200) / (1 + 3.8416 / 100) = 0.8392 / 1.0384 = 0.808; half-width (1.96 / 1.0384) × √(0.82 × 0.18 / 100 + 3.8416 / 40,000) = 1.8875 × 0.03965 = 0.0748; interval **73.3% to 88.3%**. Wilson is the default for pass rates because it stays inside 0 to 100% near the edges, where regression suites live.

The paired comparison on the candidate: 10 cases flipped, 7 of them towards the candidate. Under no difference each flip is a coin toss, so P(7 or more of 10) = (120 + 45 + 10 + 1) / 1,024 = 0.172, and the two-sided p is 0.34. The candidate's 4 points are not evidence of anything yet.

### Part 2, the suite size

Score each case +1 if only the candidate passes, −1 if only the current prompt passes, 0 otherwise. With a flip rate f and a true difference d, the mean of that score is d and its variance is f − d², close to f when d is small. The standard error over n cases is √(f / n), and detecting d at two-sided 5% with power 1 − β needs d ≥ (z₀.₉₇₅ + z₁₋β) × √(f / n), so

n = (z₀.₉₇₅ + z₁₋β)² × f / d²

with z₀.₉₇₅ = 1.96, z₀.₈ = 0.84, z₀.₉ = 1.28, and d = 0.05:

| power | f = 0.10 (observed) | f = 0.15 | f = 0.17 (top of its interval) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 80%: (1.96 + 0.84)² = 7.85 | 7.85 × 0.10 / 0.0025 = **314** | 471 | 534 |
| 90%: (1.96 + 1.28)² = 10.51 | 420 | **630** | 715 |

The accepted band, 300 to 750, covers 80% or 90% power with any flip rate from the observed 10% up to 17%, the top of the Wilson interval on 10 flips in 100. Planning at the high end is reasonable because 10 flips is a small sample of the flip rate itself.

Unreasonable choices, and why. The unpaired formula, (z₀.₉₇₅ + z₀.₈)² × (0.82 × 0.18 + 0.87 × 0.13) / 0.0025 = 7.85 × 0.2607 / 0.0025 = 819 cases per prompt, ignores that both prompts answer the same cases. Using the net difference as the flip rate (f = 0.04, 126 cases) is impossible: the flip rate can never be below the difference, and 10 of 100 flipped. Power of 50% (z = 0, 154 cases at f = 0.10) means a real 5-point gain is missed half the time.

**Is it worth building?** At 20 cases an hour, growing from 100 to about 450 cases is roughly 18 reviewer-hours. The defensible alternative is to keep 100 cases and only act on differences of 10 points or more: 7.85 × 0.10 / 0.01 = 79 cases suffice for that at 80% power. The cost of that choice is that 5-point improvements and 5-point regressions ship unmeasured, so it fits a suite that gates releases only on large regressions and leaves small ones to online metrics. Labelling budget against coverage is the trade: the same 18 hours could instead add 360 cases of a failure type the suite does not cover at all.

**Where this estimate fails.** It assumes cases are independent and the flip rate is a property of the suite. Neither holds when cases cluster around a few questions or when the next candidate prompt changes a different behaviour than this one did; a prompt that rewrites the tool-use instructions can flip 30% of cases, and the 450-case suite is then underpowered for it. Re-measure the flip rate on every comparison and report the interval of the paired difference, not a pass/fail verdict from the plan.

## Follow-ups

- *You have a week before launch and 100 cases. The candidate is 4 points better and the product lead wants it shipped. What do you sign off?* That 7 fixes against 3 breaks is no evidence either way, that the 3 broken cases were read and are acceptable or not, and that the decision is made on the reading, with the interval recorded next to it.
- *What would you need to see to stop maintaining the 450-case suite?* Six months of candidate comparisons where every shipped change moved the pass rate by 10 points or more, or online metrics that catch 5-point regressions before the suite does.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants 500 cases, the other wants 100 cases sampled five times each. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Run the current prompt against itself both ways and compare the flip rates; the design with the lower noise per labelled case wins, and the answer differs by product.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing two pass rates as if they came from independent samples when both prompts ran on the same cases.
- Sizing the suite from the pass rate instead of from the flip rate.
- Using the normal approximation near 100% and reporting an upper bound above 100%.
- Treating the interval as covering grader error or an unrepresentative set; it covers sampling noise only.
- Answering only the statistics. Composition, drift and the grader matter as much as the width.
