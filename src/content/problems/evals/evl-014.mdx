## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"The merge gate on this prompt change is borderline and the release train cuts on Thursday. Is the regression real, what number does the gate report, and what do you do?"
</Callout>

The data, constructed for this problem. A 300-case regression suite, one binary judged outcome per case, run once on the current prompt and once on the candidate with the same model snapshot, temperature and judge.

- Baseline passes 246 of 300, candidate passes 238 of 300.
- Case by case: 12 cases passed on the baseline and fail on the candidate, 4 failed and now pass, and 284 have the same result in both runs.
- Last week the team ran the baseline prompt against itself on the same suite, twice, to measure noise. 7 cases flipped in that run. 4 of today's 16 flipped cases are among those 7: 3 of the 12 regressions and 1 of the 4 fixes.
- The gate policy in the repository says: "Block when the one-sided exact sign test on flipped cases gives p below 0.05." It says nothing about known-flaky cases.

Decide how the known-flaky cases are treated, estimate the p-value the gate should report under that choice, in percent, and say whether the change merges before Thursday.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether the 7 flaky cases from last week's self-run were identified before or after anyone looked at today's result.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask what it costs in CI minutes and judge calls to rerun only the 16 flipped cases several times on both prompts before Thursday.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask the owner of the gate policy which question it answers: "is the candidate worse" or "is the candidate different".</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Two treatments are defensible and they land on opposite sides of 0.05. Keep all 16 flips: P(X ≥ 12 | n = 16, p = 0.5) = 2,517 / 65,536 = 3.8%, and the gate blocks. Drop the 4 flips on cases that last week's self-run had already marked flaky: 9 regressions of 12 flips, P(X ≥ 9 | n = 12) = 299 / 4,096 = 7.3%, and the gate passes. Both are valid tests, because the flaky set was chosen from an independent run. The policy never said which one applies, so I would not let either number decide alone: rerun the 16 flipped cases five times on each prompt, take the majority per case, and run the pre-agreed test on that. It fits before Thursday and settles the question with evidence instead of with a choice made after seeing the result.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Why the flips and nothing else.</b> The 284 agreeing cases cancel. Comparing the two pass rates as if they came from independent samples throws the pairing away: a two-proportion z-test on 246/300 against 238/300 gives z = 0.83 and a one-sided p of about 20%, and the two 95% Wilson intervals (77.3% to 85.9% and 74.4% to 83.5%) overlap. Both readings say "noise", and both are the wrong test for runs on the same cases.</p>
<p><b>Treatment 1, keep every flip.</b> Under no effect, each flip goes either way with probability 0.5, including the flaky ones: noise flips are symmetric under the null, so keeping them does not bias the test, it only dilutes it. One-sided p = 3.84%. The dominant term is C(16, 12) = 1,820 of the 2,517 in the tail, 72% of it, so the answer is decided by the exact split at 12 and moves fast if one regression turns out to be noise: 11 of 15 would give 5.9%.</p>
<p><b>Treatment 2, drop the known-flaky cases on both sides.</b> 9 of 12: (220 + 66 + 12 + 1) / 4,096 = 7.30%. This is valid only because the flaky set was fixed last week, before today's result existed, and because the fix on a flaky case is dropped along with the three regressions. It has less power, and it assumes last week's single self-run found every flaky case, which with one run it cannot have done.</p>
<p><b>Choices that are not reasonable.</b> Dropping the 3 flaky regressions but keeping the flaky fix, which gives 9 of 13 and 13.3%: the selection now depends on the direction of the flip. Switching to two-sided after seeing the result (7.7%, inside the band for the wrong reason). The uncorrected normal approximation, (12 - 4)² / 16 = 4.0, z = 2.0, one-sided 2.3%: with 16 discordant cases, under the traditional threshold of 25, the exact test is the one to use; the continuity-corrected version, (|12 - 4| - 1)² / 16 = 3.06, z = 1.75, one-sided 4.0%, lands close to the exact value.</p>
<p><b>The boundary decision.</b> Rerun the 16 flipped cases 5 times on each prompt: 160 generations and 160 judge calls, a few minutes of CI. A case counts as a pass for a prompt when it passes at least 3 of its 5 runs; the sign test then runs on the cases whose majority differs. Write the rule into the gate policy before the rerun starts. The cost of this answer: the reruns cover only cases that flipped once, so a case that is flaky in a way that showed agreement on both single runs stays invisible, and the rerun cannot raise the regression count, only confirm or dissolve it. If Thursday cannot absorb even that, block: the policy as written says 3.8% blocks, and the author can merge on the next train.</p>
</Fragment>

### Worked calculation

Upper tail of Binomial(16, 0.5) from 12: C(16, 12) + C(16, 13) + C(16, 14) + C(16, 15) + C(16, 16) = 1,820 + 560 + 120 + 16 + 1 = 2,517; 2,517 / 65,536 = 0.0384.

Upper tail of Binomial(12, 0.5) from 9: C(12, 9) + C(12, 10) + C(12, 11) + C(12, 12) = 220 + 66 + 12 + 1 = 299; 299 / 4,096 = 0.0730.

The accepted band, 3 to 8%, covers both defensible treatments and the continuity-corrected approximation. The assumption that flips the conclusion is the treatment of the 4 known-flaky flips, and nothing in the data settles it; the policy had to.

## Follow-ups

- *Why not just run the whole suite three times on every merge candidate?* It triples judge spend on 284 cases that carry no information about this change; rerunning only the flips puts the spend where the uncertainty is.
- *A teammate says the 3 flaky regressions prove the change is noise. What does the self-run actually tell you about those 3 cases?* That they can flip without a change, not that they did this time; they are evidence of lower power, not of no effect.
- *How would you size the suite so the next borderline call is rarer?* From the flip rate of past changes, since power depends on the number of discordant cases, not on the suite size.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing two pass rates or two intervals as if the runs were independent.
- Choosing the flaky-case treatment or the sidedness after seeing which side of 0.05 it lands on.
- Dropping flaky regressions while keeping flaky fixes.
- Using the uncorrected normal approximation on 16 flips.
- Treating the gate's p-value as the whole decision without listing the 12 cases that broke for the reviewer to read.
