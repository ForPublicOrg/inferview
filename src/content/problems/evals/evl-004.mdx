## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As a public interview guide phrases it">
"You have 100K user interactions. How do you build a 500-item eval set from them?"
</Callout>

Assume the interactions are a month of logs from a customer-support assistant, with full traces, thumbs-up and thumbs-down feedback on some of them, and a record of which conversations were escalated to a human. You have one domain expert for about a week of labelling. Walk through how you go from the logs to a versioned set of 500 labelled cases that the team will gate releases on.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>A uniform random sample of 500 will be mostly easy, repeated questions. What do you want over-represented?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>You cannot label 500 cases well without knowing what failure looks like. What comes before labelling?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Think about what makes the set trustworthy six months from now, not only today.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Error analysis first, on a few hundred traces, to learn the failure categories. Then clean the 100K (remove personal data, deduplicate near-identical conversations), stratify by intent, user segment and outcome, and sample 500 on purpose: a stratified slice of normal traffic, over-sampled failures from thumbs-down and escalations, rare but severe cases, and some cases where the right behaviour is to refuse or hand off. Label each with binary pass or fail per failure category and a short critique, check agreement on a shared sample, split into a regression and a capability suite with a held-out slice, and version it with a datasheet.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>1. Learn what failure looks like.</b> Read at least 100 traces, open-code the first failure in each, and cluster into categories. Those categories are both the labelling rubric and the strata for the sample. Without this step the labels are opinions.</p>
<p><b>2. Clean.</b> Strip or mask personal data before anyone else sees the traces. Cluster near-duplicates (embedding similarity or normalised text) so the 500 are not 200 copies of "where is my order".</p>
<p><b>3. Stratify and allocate.</b> Tag every conversation with intent, segment, language, turn count and outcome (thumbs-down, escalated, abandoned, resolved). Then allocate deliberately. One defensible split, which is a design choice and not a standard: about 200 stratified by traffic share, so the set still resembles production; about 150 from thumbs-down, escalations and the categories found in step 1; about 100 rare, high-severity or long-tail cases (refund promises, legal or medical topics, other languages); about 50 where the correct behaviour is to decline or hand off, so a system that answers everything cannot score well.</p>
<p><b>4. Label.</b> For each case, the expected properties and a binary verdict per relevant category, plus a one-line critique. Budget it: at 2 minutes per case, an assumption to replace with a measured pace, 500 cases is 1,000 minutes, about 17 hours, which fits the week with time left for a second labeller to re-label a shared sample of 50 and measure agreement. Disagreements become rubric fixes.</p>
<p><b>5. Structure.</b> Split into a regression suite (cases the system passes today, expected near 100%) and a capability suite (cases it fails, tracking progress). Hold out a slice nobody tunes prompts against. Mark must-pass cases that block a release on their own.</p>
<p><b>6. Version and maintain.</b> Store it with a content hash, a datasheet (source month, sampling scheme, rubric version, known gaps) and the labeller. Every score records the set version. Each error-analysis cycle adds new failures, and on a schedule someone re-reads a sample to retire labels that policy changes made wrong.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why not use thumbs-down as the label?</em> Only some users click, they are not a random sample, and a thumbs-down can mean slow, rude or wrong. It is a filter for finding cases, not a label.</li>
<li><em>How would you use an LLM to speed this up?</em> To pre-cluster conversations and propose categories after you have labelled about 30 yourself, and to draft synthetic variants for thin strata, all reviewed by the expert.</li>
<li><em>What breaks first?</em> Coverage, when a new feature or customer segment appears. Compare the set's strata with production's every cycle.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

### What the interviewer is listening for

Three things separate strong answers. Error analysis before labelling, so the rubric comes from real failures. Deliberate over-sampling of failures and refusals, with an explicit reason, so the set is not dominated by easy traffic. And a maintenance story: versioning, a held-out slice, and a loop that keeps adding production failures. A strong staff-level answer also names the cost: who labels, how long it takes, and how agreement is checked.

## Follow-ups

- *The set is mostly English but 15% of traffic is Spanish. What do you do?* Make language a stratum, allocate at least enough Spanish cases to see each failure category, and have a Spanish-speaking labeller.
- *How do you keep the held-out slice honest?* Restrict access, run it only at release time, and record every time it is looked at.
- *How would you know the set has gone stale?* Its strata drift away from production's, its regression suite sits at 100% for months while complaints rise, or labels start to contradict the current policy.

## Common mistakes

- Sampling 500 at random and calling it representative, then discovering the hard cases are a handful.
- Using Likert scores for labels, which makes agreement hard to measure and thresholds arbitrary.
- Skipping de-identification because the set is "internal".
- Building the set once. A golden set without an owner and a refresh loop drifts away from the product it is meant to test.
