## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Our support assistant's new version raised resolution and cut cost, and re-contacts went up. Define the metrics we should have had, decide whether the version stays, and tell me who owns the number."
</Callout>

The numbers are a scenario, given by the interviewer. 100,000 conversations a month start with the assistant. "Resolved" means the conversation closed without a handoff to a person. Re-contact means the same customer opens a new conversation about the same order within 7 days.

| | Before | After the new version |
|---|---|---|
| Resolved by the assistant | 52% | 61% |
| Re-contact within 7 days, among resolved | 18% | 27% |
| Thumbs-up share, among rated conversations | 71% | 78% |
| Assistant cost per conversation | $0.40 | $0.40 |
| Cost per human-handled contact | $7.00 | $7.00 |

Re-contacts go to a person. The VP of support has a quarterly target on the resolved rate and wants to keep the new version. A sample of re-contacted conversations that the team read shows the assistant confirming refunds and plan changes that the billing system then rejected.

Define the metric set for this assistant (outcome, guardrails, cost) with owners and rollback triggers, choose the re-contact window and defend it, and decide what happens to the new version this week.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask finance how long a refund or plan change takes to show up on a customer's statement.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The outcome metric is resolved without re-contact within the window, per conversation that starts with the assistant; the resolved rate on its own counts a wrong promise as a success. On these numbers it rose only from 42.6% to 44.5%, while re-contacts rose from 9,360 to 16,470 a month. Guardrails: an audited correctness rate on a stratified weekly sample, a commitment rate (refunds, credits, plan changes the assistant promised against what billing executed), and escalation latency. Cost: total cost per conversation including human re-contacts. Thumbs are a diagnostic, not a metric. The outcome metric is owned by support operations, not by the team that ships prompts, and its definition changes only with that owner's sign-off. This week: keep the new version for how-to intents and route refunds and plan changes back to the old one, because the transcripts the team read point at commitments and the old version is still deployable; the split by intent confirms or reverses that within a day.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The arithmetic.</b> Before: 52,000 resolved × 0.82 = 42,640 resolved without re-contact; 9,360 re-contacts. After: 61,000 × 0.73 = 44,530; 16,470 re-contacts. Cost per 100,000 conversations: before, $40,000 for the assistant plus (48,000 handoffs + 9,360 re-contacts) × $7 = $401,520, total $441,520; after, $40,000 + (39,000 + 16,470) × $7 = $388,290, total $428,290. The new version is 3% cheaper and resolves 1,890 more conversations for good, and it sends 7,110 more customers back angry, many of them with a promise the company will not keep. The dashboard showed the first two facts.</p>
<p><b>Why the thumbs went up.</b> An answer that agrees to the refund is rated well at the moment it is given, and the rejection arrives days later in a different channel. OpenAI's GPT-4o postmortem, as Simon Willison summarises it, names the same direction: an added reward from thumbs data, which OpenAI wrote can favour more agreeable responses, was among the changes that together weakened the signal holding sycophancy in check. METR's study is the measured version of the gap between felt and real: developers estimated a 20% speed-up and were measured 19% slower. Satisfaction at the moment of the answer is a perception metric.</p>
<p><b>The window.</b> I would choose 14 days for intents that promise money movement and 3 days for how-to intents. Suppose finance answers Hint 1 with "5 to 10 business days" (a scenario figure): a refund that takes the full 10 cannot produce a re-contact inside 7 calendar days, so a 7-day window undercounts exactly the failure we are looking at. Reasonable choices run from the posting time plus a few days to about 30 days; longer than that, a re-contact is as likely to be a new problem as the old one, and the metric arrives too late to gate a canary.</p>
<p><b>The set, with owners.</b></p>
<ul>
<li>Outcome, owned by support operations: resolved without re-contact within the intent's window, against a holdout on the previous version. Lagging by up to 14 days.</li>
<li>Guardrail, owned by the AI team: commitment accuracy, promises in the transcript matched against billing actions, computed daily by a deterministic join, not a judge. Rollback trigger: any intent where it falls below the previous version's rate over two days.</li>
<li>Guardrail, owned by the AI team with QA: audited correctness, 200 conversations a week stratified by intent and labelled by trained reviewers; the same labels validate the online judges.</li>
<li>Leading indicators: online judges on a sampled share of conversations, re-contact within 24 hours, and handoff latency. Used to find traces and to stop a canary early, not to declare success.</li>
<li>Cost, owned by finance: total cost per conversation including human re-contacts, never the assistant's cost alone.</li>
</ul>

### The decision, with two defensible answers

- **Route refunds and plan changes to the old version, keep the new one elsewhere.** Keeps the how-to gains. Cost: two versions in production, an intent router on the path where misroutes cost money, and a second set of metrics to read for a while.
- **Roll back entirely and fix the commitment behaviour before re-releasing.** Simplest to reason about. Cost: the cost saving and the how-to resolution gains go away, and the VP misses a quarter's target on a metric the company set.

I would route, because the read transcripts point at commitments and nothing points at how-to answers yet. That is a claim I have not measured: Hint 2's split by intent decides it, and if re-contacts rose on how-to intents too, the answer becomes a full rollback.

### The metric spec, written down

> Outcome: resolved without re-contact, window 14 days for money-movement intents, 3 days otherwise, measured against a 5% holdout on the previous version. Owner: support operations. Changes to the definition need the owner's sign-off and are versioned.
>
> Rollback triggers (any one, per intent, on the canary): commitment accuracy below the previous version for two days; 24-hour re-contact above the holdout's upper interval bound for two days; audited correctness falling by more than the week-to-week noise measured over the last eight weeks.
>
> Not used for decisions: thumbs-up share, resolved rate without re-contact, assistant-only cost.

### What you give up

The outcome metric is slow, so canary decisions rest on leading indicators for two weeks, and some regressions will ship and be found late. The holdout keeps 5% of customers on the old version, with its own costs. The VP's target is reframed on a number that is harder to move, which is a political cost paid by whoever brings it up.

### The failure mode of this answer

Commitment accuracy only catches promises that billing can contradict. An assistant that gives confident wrong instructions ("change this setting and it will work") passes it, resolves, and the customer leaves without re-contacting, because they give up. Re-contact then undercounts the failure. The audited sample is the control for that, which is why it stays even though it is the most expensive line.

## Follow-ups

- *The holdout costs $7 per extra handoff. The CFO asks for it to be cut to 1%. What does that do to the time needed to see a 2-point change in the outcome metric?*
- *The AI team proposes an LLM judge for "promise made" instead of the billing join. Accept or not?*
- *Six months later the outcome metric is flat and every guardrail is green. Is the assistant done, or are the metrics?*

## Common mistakes

- Reporting the resolved rate, or containment, as the outcome. It counts a wrong promise as a success.
- Using thumbs-up as a success metric, which rewards agreeable answers.
- A fixed 7-day window for every intent, shorter than the time a refund takes to post.
- Letting the team that ships prompts own the outcome metric's definition.
- Counting only the assistant's cost per conversation and leaving out the human re-contacts it creates.
