## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As a public interview guide phrases it">
"How would you define success metrics for an AI-oriented feature or product?"
</Callout>

Make it concrete: a search product is launching AI answers, a generated answer with citations above the usual list of links. Define how you will know whether the feature is succeeding, what you can measure on day one before any labels exist, what would make you roll it back, and how the metrics connect to the evals the team runs before shipping.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Separate the outcome you want, the things that must not get worse, and the signals you can read early.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>For each metric, ask how it could go up while users are worse off.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Some quality properties of an answer can be checked on every response without a human. Which?</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Three layers. An outcome metric tied to the user's goal, such as the share of searches where the user's need is met without reformulating the query, measured by A/B test against the links-only page. Guardrail metrics that must not get worse: answer error rate on audited samples, citation accuracy, latency, cost per answer, complaints. And leading indicators readable on day one: online judges for groundedness and citation support on sampled traffic, reformulation and abandonment, and clicks on citations. Each metric has a counter-metric, and the offline eval suite is built from the same failure modes the production metrics track.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Outcome.</b> Users search to get an answer or reach a page. Candidate outcome metrics: queries resolved without a reformulation within the session, return usage over weeks, and time to a satisfied end state. Measure against a control group without AI answers, because absolute numbers mean little.</p>
<p><b>Counter-metrics, so the outcome cannot be gamed.</b> Fewer reformulations could mean users trust wrong answers. Pair it with answer accuracy from an audited sample, and with complaints and corrections. Fewer clicks on links could mean satisfied users or a broken ecosystem for publishers; decide in advance which reading you accept.</p>
<p><b>Guardrails with rollback thresholds.</b> Latency to first token and full answer, cost per answer, rate of answers with an unsupported citation, rate of answers in sensitive categories (medical, legal, financial) that fail policy, and error rates. Crossing a threshold in the canary rolls back automatically.</p>
<p><b>Before labels arrive.</b> Reference-free checks on every answer or a sample: does each cited source support the sentence that cites it (a judge with the source text), are the citations reachable, did the answer respond to the question. Behavioural signals: reformulations, scroll past the answer, copy events, thumbs. These are noisy, so they are for finding traces to read and for trends, not for absolute claims.</p>
<p><b>Connection to offline evals.</b> Error analysis on early traffic produces failure categories: unsupported claims, stale facts, wrong entity, answering a different question. Each category gets an offline check in the CI suite and an online signal in production, so a failure seen in one place is measurable in the other.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Which single metric would you report to leadership?</em> The outcome against control, always shown next to the accuracy guardrail, never alone.</li>
<li><em>How do you measure accuracy at scale?</em> A stratified weekly sample labelled by trained reviewers, used both as a metric and to validate the online judges.</li>
<li><em>Thumbs-up rate rose after a change. Good news?</em> Not by itself. Only some users click and they are not a random sample; check the audited accuracy and the outcome metric before celebrating.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

## Follow-ups

- *The feature improves the outcome metric but accuracy on medical queries drops. What do you do?* Treat the sensitive category as a separate gate: suppress answers there or route them differently, rather than averaging it away.
- *How long do you run the A/B test?* Long enough to cover weekly usage cycles and novelty effects, and sized from the expected effect and baseline rate, decided before starting.
- *What does success look like for the eval suite itself?* Escaped regressions trend down, and the offline suite's failure mix resembles what production audits find.

## Common mistakes

- Picking a generic quality score (BLEU, a 1 to 10 "helpfulness") as the success metric.
- Defining only an outcome metric with no counter-metric, so a harmful change can look like a win.
- Treating thumbs as ground truth.
- Leaving the metrics disconnected from the offline evals, so CI and production disagree about what quality means.
