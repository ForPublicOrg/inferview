## Question

All inputs below are a scenario. A support agent handles three intents and can move money on one of them. Before a model upgrade, the eval suite ran each intent 16 times in fresh environments:

```text
intent             trials   successes   share of tickets this quarter
reset password       16        16          60%
change address       16        13          the rest
partial refund       16         9          between 10% and 20%
```

Finance has not settled the refund share: it was 10% last quarter and a pricing change could push it to 20%. Pick the share you believe and say why.

The release gate is written as "traffic-weighted pass^4 of at least 70%": the probability that a customer who brings the same intent four times is served correctly all four times, weighted by ticket share.

1. Estimate the traffic-weighted pass^4 in percent.
2. Say whether the upgrade passes the gate, and which of your inputs could flip that.
3. Say how many trials per intent you would need before you trust a pass or a fail, and what that costs if a trial costs $0.30 in tokens (scenario figure).

<Callout kind="interview" label="The pushback that follows">
"The reset intent passed 16 of 16. Why are you asking for more trials on the one intent that never fails?"
</Callout>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>About 69 to 74%, depending on the refund share and the estimator. Per intent, the unbiased estimate is C(c, 4) / C(16, 4): 1.000 for reset, 715 / 1,820 = 0.393 for address, 126 / 1,820 = 0.069 for refund. At a 15% refund share that is 0.6 × 1.000 + 0.25 × 0.393 + 0.15 × 0.069 = 70.9%, a pass by under one point. The gate fails if the refund share is above about 17.6%. Neither verdict is trustworthy on 16 trials: zero failures in 16 still allows a reset failure rate up to 17% at 95% one-sided, which would cut the weighted pass^4 by up to 32 points, and the reset intent is the dominant term because it carries 60% of the weight. To hold that uncertainty to 5 points you need about 138 clean reset trials; to 2 points, about 354.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Per intent, then weight.</b> pass^k is a per-task quantity averaged over tasks, here with ticket share as the weight. Averaging first and raising to the fourth power afterwards gives a different and wrong number: the weighted per-trial rate is 0.6 × 1.000 + 0.25 × 0.8125 + 0.15 × 0.5625 = 0.8875, and 0.8875^4 = 62.0%, which fails a gate the agent passes. Pooling all 48 trials, 38 / 48 = 0.792, gives 0.792^4 = 39.3%. Both shortcuts understate, because the mean of p^4 is at least the fourth power of the mean, and the gap grows with the spread between intents.</p>
<p><b>Which estimator.</b> The plug-in (c / n)^4 is the obvious choice and overstates p^4 on small n, for the same reason the pooled number understates: it raises a noisy estimate to a power. tau-bench's C(c, k) / C(n, k) is unbiased for independent trials, and it is the same construction as the Codex paper's pass@k estimator, which that paper uses after showing that the plug-in form is biased. On these counts the two differ by 1.5 to 1.6 points: plug-in gives 74.1% at a 10% refund share and 70.7% at 20%; unbiased gives 72.5% and 69.2%.</p>
<p><b>The band.</b> The accepted range, 69 to 75%, covers any refund share from 10% to 20% with either estimator. Outside it on purpose: the unweighted three-intent average of 48.7%, the weighted-rate shortcut of 62.0% and the pooled 39.3%. The unweighted average is not wrong as a number; it answers a different question, which comes back below.</p>
<p><b>What flips the verdict.</b> With the unbiased estimator the gate is 0.6 + (0.4 − r) × 0.393 + r × 0.069 ≥ 0.70, so it passes only while the refund share r is below 0.0571 / 0.324 = 17.6%. With the plug-in it passes up to 22.1%, beyond the stated range. A gate that sits within two points of its threshold and changes verdict with an estimator choice and a finance forecast is not deciding anything yet.</p>
<p><b>The dominant term is the intent that never failed.</b> With 16 of 16 successes, the largest reset failure rate q still consistent with that result at 95% one-sided solves (1 − q)^16 = 0.05, so q = 1 − 0.05^(1/16) = 17.1%. At q = 17.1% the reset intent's pass^4 is 0.829^4 = 47.3%, and its contribution falls by 0.6 × (1 − 0.473) = 31.6 points. The refund intent, the one everyone is looking at, moves the weighted number by at most 0.15 to 0.20 of a point per point of its own pass^4. Measurement effort goes where weight times uncertainty is largest, and that is the reset intent.</p>
<p><b>How many trials.</b> Choose a tolerance t for the reset intent's possible contribution error. Solve 0.6 × (1 − (1 − q)^4) ≤ t for q, then find the n for which zero failures bounds the failure rate at q: n = ln 0.05 / ln(1 − q). For t = 5 points, q = 2.15% and n ≈ 138; for t = 2 points, q = 0.84% and n ≈ 354. The address intent fails often enough for an ordinary interval: its contribution's 95% half-width is about 1.96 × 0.25 × 4 × 0.8125³ × √(0.8125 × 0.1875 / n) ≈ 0.41 / √n, so 5 points needs about 67 trials and 3 points about 187. At $0.30 a trial, 354 reset trials cost $106 and a full 354-trial run of all three intents about $319, which is cheap next to one wrong refund policy shipped. The wall-clock time and the simulator's own error rate are the real limits, not the token bill.</p>
<p><b>The failure mode of this answer.</b> Every number above assumes independent trials. If the 16 trials shared a warm cache, a reused container or leftover state, successes are correlated and every estimate here is optimistic, the zero-failure bound most of all. Anthropic reports an internal eval inflated by an agent reading git history left by earlier trials. Before buying 354 trials, confirm that each one starts clean, or the extra trials only make a biased number look precise.</p>

### Worked calculation at a 15% refund share

| Intent | Weight | Successes | C(c, 4) / C(16, 4) | Plug-in (c / 16)^4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reset password | 0.60 | 16 | 1,820 / 1,820 = 1.000 | 1.000 |
| Change address | 0.25 | 13 | 715 / 1,820 = 0.393 | 0.436 |
| Partial refund | 0.15 | 9 | 126 / 1,820 = 0.069 | 0.100 |
| Weighted pass^4 | | | 70.9% | 72.4% |

For scale on what pass^k does to a published agent: tau-bench reports gpt-4o with function calling at a pass^1 of 61.2 on its retail domain and a pass^8 there under 25%. C(n, k) needs n ≥ k, so a pass^8 figure needs at least eight trials of every task; the paper's main results ran at least three.

## Follow-ups

- *A product manager points out that the weighted pass^4 rose two points since the last release. How much of that would you believe on 16 trials per intent, and what would you show them?* The zero-failure bound alone spans 32 points; a two-point move is inside it.
- *When would you gate on pass^1 instead of pass^4?* When customers rarely bring the same intent twice and a failure is cheap to retry. For an intent that moves money, the k should come from how many times a customer meets the agent before trust is lost, and that number should come from support data, not from the eval.
- *Refunds above a threshold already go to a person. Does that change the weight you give the refund intent?* It changes what a refund failure costs, which is the argument for a separate refund gate rather than a heavier weight.

## Common mistakes

- Averaging the per-trial rates and raising the average to the fourth power.
- Treating 16 of 16 as a measured 100% and spending every extra trial on the intent that visibly fails.
- Quoting the plug-in estimate without saying it runs high on small n.
- Reporting a gate verdict without the input (here the refund share) that flips it.
- Adding trials without checking that trials are independent.
