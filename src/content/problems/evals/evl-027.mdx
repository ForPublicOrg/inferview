## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 15 minutes">
"Compliance will accept our judge-corrected failure rate if we can state the judge's TNR to within plus or minus five points at 95%. How many replies does the analyst need to label, and how long is that?"
</Callout>

Scenario inputs, not measurements:

- The team's eval tooling is judgy. Labels are 1 for pass and 0 for fail, and the team reports TPR and TNR in that convention.
- Last month's audit found 9 failing replies in 300 random ones.
- The current judge's TNR was last measured at 0.8 on 20 labelled failures.
- The analyst labels about 50 replies an hour and has other work.
- Replies are sampled at random from production unless you propose otherwise.

Give the number of replies the analyst must label, then say what you would do instead.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which human label the outputs behind the team's TNR carry.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how precise the 9-in-300 failure share is before you divide by it.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask which signals in production flag a likely failure without asking the judge.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>In judgy's convention pass is positive, so the team's TNR is the share of real failures the judge fails, and its sample is failures, not replies. A ±5-point interval at TNR near 0.8 needs about 1.96² × 0.8 × 0.2 / 0.05² ≈ 246 labelled failures. At 3% failures, random sampling finds them at 1 in 33: about 8,200 replies, 164 analyst hours. The dominant term is the failure share. I would not label at random: I would find candidate failures with signals independent of the judge, label those, and accept the bias risk that comes with it, in writing.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Which rate this is.</strong> judgy's quick start labels pass as 1, and its correction (p_obs + TNR − 1) / (TPR + TNR − 1) only holds with pass as the positive class. So the team's TNR is P(judge fails | human fails): what the FAQ, with failure positive, calls TPR. Whatever it is called, it is estimated from human-labelled failures only, and the passes in the sample add nothing to it.</p>
<p><strong>Failures needed.</strong> For a proportion near p, a 95% interval of half-width E needs n ≈ 1.96² × p(1 − p) / E². The one assumption you choose is p:</p>
<ul>
<li>p = 0.8, the last measurement: 3.84 × 0.16 / 0.0025 ≈ 246 failures.</li>
<li>p = 0.5, the worst case, because 20 failures say little: 3.84 × 0.25 / 0.0025 ≈ 385.</li>
<li>p = 0.9, if the judge is believed to have improved: 3.84 × 0.09 / 0.0025 ≈ 139.</li>
</ul>
<p>For comparison, the FAQ's floor of 30 to 50 per label gives, at 50 failures and p = 0.8, a half-width of 1.96 × √(0.16 / 50) ≈ 11 points. Compliance's ±5 costs about five times the floor.</p>
<p><strong>Replies needed.</strong> Divide by the failure share f. At f = 3%: 246 / 0.03 ≈ 8,200 replies (164 hours at 50 an hour); 385 / 0.03 ≈ 12,800 (256 hours); 139 / 0.03 ≈ 4,600 (92 hours). The second choice you make is f. 9 in 300 is itself uncertain: a 95% Wilson interval runs from about 1.6% to 5.6%. Planning at f = 2% to protect the schedule gives 385 / 0.02 ≈ 19,200 replies at the worst-case p. Answers from about 4,500 to 20,000 follow from choices the inputs allow.</p>
<p><strong>The dominant term and the assumption that flips it.</strong> 1/f dominates: halving the failure share doubles the labelling, while moving p from 0.8 to 0.5 multiplies it by about 1.6. The conclusion flips if failures can be found without random sampling. TNR in this convention is conditional on the true label being fail, so a sample of failures does not have to come from a random sample of replies, as long as the way they were found does not depend on the judge's verdict.</p>
<p><strong>What I would do instead.</strong> Draw candidate failures from signals that do not consult the judge: customer escalations, thumbs-down, agent hand-offs, and deterministic checks for mechanical failures. If one candidate in three is a real failure, about 740 candidate labels yield 246 failures, about 15 hours instead of 164. Label a random sample of replies alongside, to keep measuring f and the other rate.</p>
<p><strong>How that fails.</strong> Failures found by escalation are the ones customers noticed. If the judge is better at obvious failures, the TNR measured on them is too high, and no interval width shows it. The mitigation is to keep a random-sample stratum, even if it only yields a few dozen failures a quarter, and compare the judge's rate on the two strata; a large gap means the enriched estimate cannot stand alone. That is the trade: labelling budget against how representative the failures are, and it belongs in the note to compliance.</p>
</Fragment>

<ul>
<li><em>Compliance asks for the same ±5 points on each of four failure headings. What happens to the estimate?</em> The failure count is needed per heading, about 246 each at p = 0.8, and the rarest heading sets the cost.</li>
<li><em>Why not label only the replies the judge failed, since those are cheap to find?</em> Every failure found that way was caught by the judge, so the measured rate is 100% by construction.</li>
</ul>

### Choices that are not reasonable

- About 250 replies. That is the failure count, read as a reply count, which ignores the 3% base rate.
- Assuming p = 0.95 or above to shrink the sample. The normal approximation is poor near 1, the last measurement was 0.8, and the assumption is the conclusion you are trying to test.
- Reading the team's TNR as the share of good replies passed, as the FAQ's naming would suggest. Its sample would then be passes, which are 97% of traffic, and the answer would be a few hundred replies for the wrong rate.
- Finding failures by the judge's own verdicts.

## Follow-ups

- *The analyst can give 20 hours, not 164. What do you tell compliance, and which interval width do you promise?* At about 1,000 random labels you expect roughly 30 failures, and a half-width near 14 points at p = 0.8. Promise that, or the enriched design with its bias note.
- *What would make you trust the enriched estimate without a random stratum?* Evidence that the screen's recall does not depend on how obvious the failure is, for example a screen that is a deterministic check whose rule is unrelated to anything the judge reads.
- *The judge's prompt changes next month. Which of these labels can you reuse?* All of them: labels belong to the replies, not the judge. Re-run the new judge on the same labelled replies and recompute; only the verdicts are new.

## Common mistakes

- Using the FAQ's TPR and judgy's TNR as if they were the same number with different names, when they are the same number only with the classes swapped.
- Sizing the sample in replies without dividing by the failure share.
- Treating the 3% as exact when it rests on 9 failures.
- Enriching with the judge itself.
