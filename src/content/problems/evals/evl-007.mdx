## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As a public question bank phrases the core of it">
"How do you calibrate an LLM judge against humans?"
</Callout>

The setting and its numbers are a scenario. A support assistant sends about 30,000 replies a week. Today one support lead reviews 150 sampled replies every Friday; over the last six weeks she reviewed 900 and marked 36 as failures, under four headings: wrong refund amount, promises a credit the agent cannot issue, leaks account data, and tone. A vendor offers a hosted judge that returns pass or fail per reply, and their sales deck says it "agrees with human raters 92% of the time". The head of support wants to stop the Friday review at the end of the month and use the vendor's pass rate on a dashboard instead. The support lead can give you ten hours over the next two weeks; she labels about 40 replies an hour.

Can the team trust the judge? Say what you would measure, with how many labels, what you would decide by the end of the month, and how you answer the pushback: "The vendor already measured it. 92% is good."

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask the vendor what share of the 92% set was failures, who labelled it, and for which criterion.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask whether the 36 failures from the Friday reviews were kept with the reply text and the heading she gave them.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask which of the four failure headings the head of support would accept missing for a month, and which none.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Not yet, and the 92% cannot tell us. At our failure share of 36 in 900, 4%, a judge that passes every reply agrees with the support lead 96% of the time, so a 92% judge could be worse than no judge. I would measure the vendor's judge on our labels as a classifier, failure as the positive class, and report TPR and TNR per failure heading. The ten hours buy about 400 labels; spent at random they would contain about 16 failures, which pins nothing, so I would spend most of them relabelling the 36 known failures and a matched set of passes, held out from any prompt work. By the end of the month the judge runs as a monitor next to a smaller Friday review, not instead of it.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The pushback, answered with arithmetic.</strong> Agreement is (true positives + true negatives) over all items, so it mostly measures the class balance. On our traffic, with 4% failures, the always-pass judge scores 96%. On a set with 50% failures, the same judge scores 50%. The vendor's 92% is uninterpretable until we know three things: the failure share of their set, whose definition of failure it encodes, and the task. Published agreement figures carry their conditions with them: in the MT-Bench paper, GPT-4 agreed with experts on 85% of first-turn pairwise votes against 81% between humans, which is a result about choosing the better of two chat answers, not about catching a credit promise in a support reply.</p>
<p><strong>What to measure.</strong> The judge is a classifier written in English, so it gets a confusion matrix on labels it was never tuned on. With failure as positive, TPR is the share of real failures it fails and TNR the share of good replies it passes. Both, per heading: a judge with TPR 0.9 on tone and 0.2 on credit promises averages to a number that hides the failure that costs money.</p>
<p><strong>How many labels: the quantity you choose.</strong> Ten hours at 40 an hour is about 400 labels. At random, 400 replies contain about 16 failures, four per heading. A TPR measured on four failures moves 25 points per miss, and the llm-judge write-up warns that below 60 examples the intervals are often too wide to support a conclusion. So the budget goes where the information is: the support lead relabels the 36 known failures pass or fail under one written criterion per heading (about one hour; they were labelled once already), labels about 160 passes drawn from the same weeks (four hours), and spends the rest on fresh replies from this week, which also tells us whether the failure share has moved. TPR and TNR are rates conditional on the true label, so sampling failures and passes separately does not bias them. Reasonable choices sit between 30 and 50 failures and at least as many passes, matching the FAQ's dev and test sizes; with 36 failures total, there is no separate dev set, which is acceptable only because we are not tuning the vendor's prompt.</p>
<p><strong>The decision, and its two defensible answers.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><em>Adopt the vendor's judge after this validation.</em> Cheapest to run and nothing to build. Cost: its rubric is the vendor's, a single pass or fail that we cannot split by heading or edit when the refund policy changes, and the vendor can change the model under it; each change needs a re-measurement we pay for.</li>
<li><em>Build our own binary judge per heading from the support lead's critiques.</em> Criteria match ours and each can be re-measured alone. Cost: her time to write critiques, engineering time to build and measure four judges, and four judges to maintain; the FAQ counts ongoing weekly maintenance as part of what a judge costs.</li>
</ul>
<p>The constraint conflict is labelling budget against coverage: ten hours can validate one judge in aggregate or two headings properly, not four. I would validate the vendor's judge first, because it costs nothing extra to measure and the result decides the build question: if its TPR on the money headings (refund amount, credit promises) is high, adopt it for those; if not, write our own judge for those two headings, and use code checks where the failure is mechanical, such as comparing a quoted refund amount with the order record.</p>
<p><strong>What changes at the end of the month.</strong> The Friday review shrinks from 150 replies to about 50 and changes purpose: it now produces fresh labels to re-measure the judge each week, rather than being the only check. The dashboard shows the judge's pass rate corrected for its measured TPR and TNR, with an interval, the way judgy computes it: (p_obs + TNR − 1) / (TPR + TNR − 1) in judgy's convention, where pass is the positive class. A raw judge pass rate is never shown alone.</p>
<p><strong>How the recommendation fails.</strong> The 36 failures were found by a human reading replies, so they are the failures a human notices on a Friday afternoon. A TPR measured on them describes those, and may overstate the judge's recall on subtle failures, such as a refund amount that is plausible but wrong, which neither she nor the judge flagged. The correction formula also assumes TPR and TNR measured on this mix hold on production traffic; a shift in the share of each heading changes the judge's aggregate recall without any change in the judge.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Pushback: the head of support says ten hours is all she gets this quarter, and asks which heading you leave unvalidated.</em> Tone. A tone miss costs a worse reply; a credit promise costs money the company may have to honour. The written risk: tone failures go unmeasured until next quarter's labels.</li>
<li><em>Reversal: what would you need to see to stop the Friday review entirely?</em> Several weeks in which the corrected rate's interval stays narrow enough to detect the regression size the team cares about, TPR on the money headings re-measured on fresh labels without a drop, and a code check in place for refund amounts.</li>
<li><em>Two senior engineers disagree: one wants the vendor judge now, the other wants four in-house judges first. How do you decide without deciding by rank?</em> Agree in advance on the TPR that would make the vendor judge acceptable for each money heading, then let the measurement decide.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>

### When a judge is the wrong tool here

A wrong refund amount is mechanical once the order record is in reach: compare the amount in the reply with the record. Code is cheaper per item, deterministic, and needs a handful of pass and fail examples rather than a hundred labels. A leaked account number is the same: a pattern check plus an allow list. The judge is for what code cannot decide, such as whether a reply promised a credit in so many words.

## Follow-ups

- *Operating: the vendor announces a new judge model next quarter and says agreement improved. What do you rerun, on which labels, and who signs off before the dashboard switches?* The same per-heading measurement on the held-out labels plus the latest Friday labels, before and after, on the same items; the support lead signs off on the money headings.
- *The vendor offers you their 92% evaluation set to save labelling time. Do you use it?* Only to read their criterion. It cannot measure the judge on our definition of failure or our failure share.
- *Your relabelling shows the support lead now disagrees with her own Friday label on 5 of the 36. What does that tell you?* The criterion was not written down. Fix the written criterion first; the judge cannot be more consistent than the labels it is measured against.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing the vendor's 92% with an agreement figure from a paper, as if agreement were a property of the judge rather than of judge, labels and class balance together.
- Spending the whole labelling budget on a random sample and getting a TPR from a dozen failures.
- Reporting one TPR across four failure headings with very different costs.
- Stopping the human review on the day the judge launches, which removes the only source of fresh labels for re-measuring it.
