## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"How do you handle model updates and deprecations from providers?" Make it concrete with the situation below.
</Callout>

A customer-support product sends all traffic to one provider's model through a thin internal client: a ticket classifier, a tool-using assistant with four tools, and a structured-output extraction job that writes to a billing system. The provider has just announced that the model retires in 60 days, its stated minimum notice, and names a replacement from the same family. Leadership uses the moment to ask for a second provider, both as failover during outages and as a migration path next time. Two engineers and one evaluation analyst are available. Each feature has a golden set; the assistant's is 300 conversations and takes two analyst-days to grade by hand.

Design the migration and the second-provider path. Users must not notice which model answered.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>How many full grading passes of the assistant's golden set fit in 60 days with one analyst, and how many will each candidate model need?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask which of the three features has a downstream system that rejects malformed output, and which has a human who would notice tone.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask for the secondary provider's rate limits at the tier you would buy, against peak traffic.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Treat the retirement and the second provider as two projects with different deadlines. The retirement is fixed: move every feature to the named replacement before day 60, qualified per feature on its golden set with an agreed margin, through shadow traffic and a canary with rollback triggers. The second provider is not on the critical path: qualify it feature by feature after the migration is safe, and use it for failover only on features that pass. "Users don't notice" becomes a measured margin per feature, not a promise.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The decision.</b> Two defensible plans. Plan A: migrate to the same provider's replacement first, then add the second provider. Cost: no failover for another quarter, and the next retirement may find you single-homed again. Plan B: qualify the replacement and the second provider in parallel and keep whichever passes each feature first. Cost: double the qualification load on one analyst, inside a fixed deadline. Sixty calendar days are about 60 × 5 / 7 ≈ 42 working days, so one analyst grading nothing else completes at most 21 passes of the assistant's set at two days each. Each candidate model needs several (a baseline, one per prompt revision, a final canary check), and Plan B's extra passes come out of the room for a failed first attempt. I choose A for the assistant and B for the classifier and extraction job, whose golden sets grade automatically.</p>
<p><b>The interface.</b> The thin client becomes the contract: messages and tools in, text, tool calls, stop reason and normalised usage out. Per-provider adapters translate tool-call formats, schema subsets, stop reasons and errors, and drop parameters a model rejects (Claude's API version 2026-10-21 returns 400 for temperature, top_p and top_k). Prompts are versioned per (feature, model), because one prompt does not behave the same on two models. A gateway such as LiteLLM can hold the routing (retries on a model group, then fallbacks in listed order, separate fallbacks for context-window and content-policy errors), but the behaviour behind it is still yours to qualify.</p>
<p><b>Margins.</b> The reader chooses them. For the extraction job, zero tolerance on schema-valid output and a field-level accuracy margin small enough that the billing team accepts it in writing; for the classifier, a margin on macro accuracy against the current model; for the assistant, a paired comparison on graded conversations with a margin set before seeing results. A margin chosen after the results is not a margin.</p>
<p><b>Failover, later.</b> Trigger on overload and sustained server errors, never on 400s, auth errors or spend-cap 429s. Fail over whole conversations, because history carries one provider's tool-call format. Accept that failover traffic misses the prompt cache and that encrypted reasoning does not carry across deployments.</p>
<p><b>What you give up.</b> Failover for the assistant this quarter under Plan A; one analyst's entire quarter; and the option to pick the cheaper provider per feature until both are qualified.</p>
<p><b>Failure mode of this plan.</b> The secondary provider is qualified on a few hundred requests and then receives full production volume during the first outage, where its rate limits return 429s and the failover itself fails. Size its limits for failover volume and test with a forced breaker before declaring it ready.</p>
</Fragment>

### The written artefact

A rollout plan the team can hold itself to:

| Day | Step | Rollback trigger |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 10 | Adapters and per-model prompts; golden sets run on the replacement for classifier and extraction | none, no traffic |
| 11 to 20 | Shadow traffic on all three features; first graded pass of the assistant | n/a |
| 21 to 30 | Classifier and extraction canary at a small share, then full | schema failures above zero on extraction; classifier below margin |
| 31 to 45 | Assistant canary, widened in steps | complaint rate or graded score below margin; tool-call error rate above baseline |
| 46 to 55 | Assistant at full traffic; second-provider qualification starts for classifier and extraction | same triggers |
| 56 to 60 | Buffer; nothing new ships | n/a |

The buffer matters more than any step: a rollback on day 58 must land on a model that is still served.

### Checklist

- Separates the retirement migration (a reviewed rollout with a hard date) from runtime failover (an automatic reaction in seconds) and gives each its own trigger
- Qualifies each feature separately on its own golden set, and lets a feature ship on one provider while another waits
- Chooses and defends the regression margin per feature, and says which margins would be unreasonable and why
- Decides between migrating to the same provider's replacement model first and adding the second provider in the same window, with the cost of each
- Names what differs between providers beyond the endpoint (tool-call formats, strict-schema subsets, rejected sampling parameters, stop reasons, prompt caches)
- Keeps whole conversations on one provider and does not fail over mid-conversation without converting history
- Excludes 400s, auth errors and spend-cap 429s from failover triggers
- Writes a rollout plan with dates, traffic shares and rollback triggers that end before the retirement date
- States a failure mode of the plan inside the answer, such as the secondary's rate limits being sized for qualification traffic rather than failover volume
- Says what ships degraded if a feature does not qualify by the deadline, and who signs off

## Follow-ups

- *The provider moves the retirement date earlier on a partner cloud you also use. What breaks first?* The fallback route that points at that platform's copy of the model. Track lifecycle per (model, platform) pair; the deprecations page notes that partner platforms set their own schedules.
- *The extraction job's billing team refuses any change to field accuracy. What do you do with ten days left?* Ship the replacement with a validation layer that sends low-confidence extractions to a human queue, and size that queue from the shadow results.
- *Why not let the gateway pick the cheapest qualified provider per request?* Two behaviours in production at once for one feature, which doubles the slices every metric needs. Do it per feature, not per request, and only after both are qualified.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the second provider as a prerequisite for the retirement and putting both on one deadline.
- One prompt for both models, with no per-model version.
- Choosing the margin after looking at the graded results.
- Failing over on every error, including your own 400s.
- A plan whose last step lands on the retirement date, with no room to roll back.
