## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
A benefits-eligibility check returns one of three answers: eligible, not eligible, or needs review. Two designs are on the table. How many times more does the reasoning-model design cost per request than the voting design? Then say which assumption would change the choice between them.
</Callout>

Scenario inputs (sizes are the team's measurements, prices are list prices checked 2026-10-28):

- Each request sends 1,500 input tokens: the rules and the applicant's record. 200,000 requests a day.
- **Voting design:** Claude Haiku 5.5 with thinking disabled, five parallel calls, each writing its reasoning and answer in about 400 output tokens, majority vote. No cache hits, since the calls go out together. $0.10 input and $0.50 output per million tokens.
- **Reasoning design:** one Claude Sonnet 5.5 call at `medium` effort, with a final answer of about 150 tokens plus however many thinking tokens it spends. $2 input and $10 output per million tokens.

You choose the thinking-token count and defend it. Give the ratio of reasoning-design cost to voting-design cost per request.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask how thinking tokens are billed when the thinking text is not returned to you.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask for <code>usage.output_tokens</code> from a sample of real Sonnet calls at medium effort on this task, not the length of the visible answer.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what a wrong "not eligible" costs the business, per case.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Voting: 5 × (1,500 × $0.10 + 400 × $0.50) per million = $0.00175 per request. Reasoning: (1,500 × $2 + (T + 150) × $10) per million, which is $0.0145 at T = 1,000 thinking tokens. Ratio about 8x, and anywhere from about 5x to 20x for thinking between 500 and 3,000 tokens. The dominant term is Sonnet's output, thinking included, at $10 per million. The choice flips on error cost, not token cost: at $0.01275 a request difference, the reasoning design pays for itself if it avoids one wrong decision in about 400 requests when an error costs $5.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Voting design.</b> Per call: 1,500 × $0.10 / 1M = $0.00015 input and 400 × $0.50 / 1M = $0.0002 output, so $0.00035. Five calls: $0.00175 per request, $350 a day at 200,000 requests.</p>
<p><b>Reasoning design.</b> Input: 1,500 × $2 / 1M = $0.003. Output: (T + 150) × $10 / 1M. Thinking tokens are billed as output whether or not the text is returned, so T is in the bill even though the application sees 150 tokens.</p>

<p><b>Dominant term.</b> 10 × T in the reasoning cost. Input and the visible answer are a fixed $0.0045; every 1,000 thinking tokens adds $0.01.</p>
<p><b>Which choices are reasonable.</b> Effort is a behavioural signal, not a budget, so T has to come from measured <code>usage</code> on this task. A few hundred to a few thousand tokens for a 1,500-token rules check is the defensible range until measured. T = 0 is unreasonable: Sonnet 5.5 at medium effort thinks by default and rejects disabling it. Counting only the 150 visible tokens, which gives about 2.6x, is the common wrong answer.</p>
<p><b>The assumption that flips the choice.</b> The cost gap at T = 1,000 is $0.01275 a request, $2,550 a day. If a wrong eligibility decision costs the business $5 (scenario), the reasoning design breaks even when it is right on 0.01275 / 5 = 0.255% more requests, about one in 400. Whether it is depends on the eval, especially on the needs-review slice where a majority vote over three classes can be split.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>The votes come back 2-2-1. What does the voting design do, and what does that cost?</em> Route to needs review; the cost is human review time, which belongs in the comparison.</li>
<li><em>What changes if the five votes run on Sonnet 5.5 instead?</em> Even if each spent nothing on thinking, five Sonnet calls at 400 output tokens cost 5 × (1,500 × $2 + 400 × $10) / 1M = $0.035 a request, more than the single reasoning call for any T below 3,050, so voting stops being the cheap option.</li>
<li><em>Which design has the better p95 latency?</em> Voting's critical path is the slowest of five 400-token generations on a small model; reasoning's is T + 150 tokens on a larger one. Measure both under production concurrency before deciding on latency grounds.</li>
</ul>

The estimate settles the price of each design, not the choice. At this volume the voting design saves a few thousand dollars a day, which matters only if its accuracy on the expensive error is within a fraction of a percentage point of the reasoning design's. The failure mode of picking voting on cost alone is that its savings are visible on the invoice while its extra wrong denials are visible only in appeals, weeks later.

## Follow-ups

- *Finance wants the voting design. Product wants the reasoning one for accuracy. What do you put in front of them?* The per-day cost of each, the measured accuracy gap on the not-eligible slice, and the scenario error cost, as one table with the break-even line on it.
- *What would make you run both?* Voting first and escalating only split votes to the reasoning model, if the eval shows split votes are where voting is wrong; the blended cost is voting plus the split fraction times the reasoning cost.
- *Next year's model halves Sonnet's thinking at the same effort level. What in your analysis survives?* The formula and the break-even method; the token count has to be re-measured, since effort levels do not mean the same thinking across models.

## Common mistakes

- Pricing the reasoning call on its visible answer and ignoring thinking tokens.
- Assuming the five votes hit the cache when they are sent in parallel.
- Comparing designs on cost per request without the cost of a wrong decision.
- Quoting a vendor effort level as if it fixed a token count.
