## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 25 minutes">
A teammate reproduces the Large Language Monkeys setup on SWE-bench Lite and reports that 250 samples per issue solve 56% of the 300 problems, against 15.9% with one sample. They want to ship "56%" as the agent's solve rate. You point out that 56% is coverage: a correct patch exists somewhere among the 250. Estimate the share of problems the system actually solves when it has to pick one patch, and say how much of the gap from 15.9% to 56% selection recovers.
</Callout>

Published inputs (Brown et al., arXiv 2407.21787, v3 revised 2024-12-30): DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct in Moatless Tools solves 15.9% of SWE-bench Lite with one sample, 29.62% with five and 56% with 250.

Scenario inputs, not measurements. The paper does not publish how many of the 250 samples are correct per problem, so use this distribution, chosen to match the three published points:

| Share of the 300 problems | Correct samples out of 250 |
|---|---|
| 44% | 0 |
| 20% | 3 |
| 12% | 25 |
| 18% | 125 |
| 6% | 225 |

Check: the expected one-sample rate is 0.20 × 3/250 + 0.12 × 25/250 + 0.18 × 125/250 + 0.06 × 225/250 = 0.0024 + 0.012 + 0.09 + 0.054 ≈ 15.8%; the five-sample coverage, 1 − (1 − c/250)<sup>5</sup> summed over the rows, is about 29.5%; and 56% of problems have at least one correct sample.

The picker, modelled on the filter Anthropic describes for Claude 4's high-compute numbers but without its scoring model: discard every patch that fails the repository's visible tests, then pick one of the survivors at random. A correct patch survives with probability 0.95 (some suites are flaky). The probability that a wrong patch survives is yours to choose. Give the solve rate at 250 samples.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>With a wrong patch surviving the visible tests 30% of the time, the system solves about 23% of problems, not 56%. Selection recovers about 7 of the 40 points between one sample and coverage. A row with <em>c</em> correct samples of 250 is solved with probability 0.95<em>c</em> / (0.95<em>c</em> + 0.3(250 − <em>c</em>)), which is 76% where half the samples are correct and under 4% where 3 are. The problems that 250 samples newly cover are mostly the 3-correct and 25-correct rows, and a random pick among survivors cannot find them. The dominant term is the 125-correct row; the assumption that flips the conclusion is the filter's rate on wrong patches.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The choice: a wrong patch survives with probability <em>f</em>. Visible regression tests reject patches that break existing behaviour; they say nothing about whether the issue is fixed, so most wrong patches that are not destructive pass them. I choose <em>f</em> = 0.3.</p>
<p>Per row, the expected survivors are 0.95<em>c</em> correct and 0.3(250 − <em>c</em>) wrong, and a random pick is correct with the ratio of the first to the sum:</p>
<ul>
<li>3 correct: 2.85 / (2.85 + 74.1) ≈ 0.037</li>
<li>25 correct: 23.75 / (23.75 + 67.5) ≈ 0.260</li>
<li>125 correct: 118.75 / (118.75 + 37.5) ≈ 0.760</li>
<li>225 correct: 213.75 / (213.75 + 7.5) ≈ 0.966</li>
</ul>
<p>Weighted by the shares: 0.20 × 0.037 + 0.12 × 0.260 + 0.18 × 0.760 + 0.06 × 0.966 ≈ 0.007 + 0.031 + 0.137 + 0.058 ≈ 0.233. About 23% solved, against 56% covered and 15.8% at one sample. The 125-correct row contributes 13.7 of the 23.3 points; the 3-correct row, a fifth of all problems and over a third of the covered ones, contributes 0.7.</p>
<p>The band. At <em>f</em> = 0.1, a filter that catches most wrong patches, the rate is about 30.4%; at <em>f</em> = 0.7, about 17.8%. Anything from roughly 17% to 31% follows from a defensible <em>f</em>. Unreasonable choices: <em>f</em> = 0, which treats regression tests as the oracle and returns the full 56%; and no filter at all, a random pick among all 250, which returns the one-sample rate of 15.8%.</p>
<p>What more samples buy. Under this picker, success depends on the ratio of correct to wrong survivors, not on their number. Computed over the binomial draw of correct samples (the script below), five samples give about 22.8% and 25 give about 23.3% at <em>f</em> = 0.3, the same as 250. The paper's own cost table puts five samples at $10.8 for the 300 problems ($0.0072 per attempt), so 250 samples cost 50 times that, $540, and buy nothing over 25 with a random picker. The paper reports the same shape on MATH with Llama-3-8B-Instruct: coverage rose from 82.9% to 98.44% between 100 and 10,000 samples while majority voting and a reward model moved from 40.50% to 41.41%.</p>
<p>The decision with two defensible answers. Spend the next month on a better filter: an agent-written reproduction test per issue, required to fail before the patch, which attacks <em>f</em> directly; at <em>f</em> = 0.1 the rate is 30.4% with no other change. Its cost is that reproductions are themselves wrong some of the time, which lowers the 0.95 and can reject correct patches. Or spend it on a scoring model that ranks survivors, as Anthropic did for Claude 4's high-compute numbers; it is the only thing that can pull a 3-in-250 patch out of 77 survivors, but it has to be trained and calibrated, and the paper found reward models plateau without an automatic verifier. With fixed compute, I would cut sampling to 25, which loses nothing under this picker, and put the saving into the reproduction-test filter, because its effect is measurable on the existing 300 problems in a day.</p>
<p>Failure mode of that answer. The filter and the scoring model only see what the visible tests and reproductions check. If the reproductions share the agent's misreading of the issue, <em>f</em> drops on paper while the system solves no more problems. Measure solve rate on the hidden tests, never on the agent's own checks.</p>
</Fragment>

### The computation

```python
from math import comb

ROWS = [(0.20, 3), (0.12, 25), (0.18, 125), (0.06, 225)]  # share, correct of 250
TP = 0.95

def at_250(f):
    return sum(s * c * TP / (c * TP + (250 - c) * f) for s, c in ROWS)

def at_k(k, f):  # draw k samples; filter; random survivor, or random sample if none survive
    total = 0.0
    for share, c in ROWS:
        q = c / 250
        for n_ok in range(1, k + 1):
            p_n = comb(k, n_ok) * q**n_ok * (1 - q) ** (k - n_ok)
            n_bad, acc = k - n_ok, 0.0
            for a in range(n_ok + 1):
                pa = comb(n_ok, a) * TP**a * (1 - TP) ** (n_ok - a)
                for b in range(n_bad + 1):
                    pb = comb(n_bad, b) * f**b * (1 - f) ** (n_bad - b)
                    acc += pa * pb * (a / (a + b) if a + b else n_ok / k)
            total += share * p_n * acc
    return total

print(round(at_250(0.3), 3), round(at_k(5, 0.3), 3), round(at_k(25, 0.3), 3))  # 0.233 0.228 0.233
```

### Choices that are not reasonable

- Reporting 56% as the solve rate. It is pass@250, a property of the sample set, not of a system that returns one patch.
- Treating visible regression tests as an oracle. They reject breakage; a wrong patch that breaks nothing passes them.
- Assuming the 40-point gap is spread evenly across problems. It is concentrated in problems with a handful of correct samples, which is exactly where random selection fails.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing a 250-sample coverage number with another system's single-sample number without saying so. The paper states both sample counts when it sets 56% against 43%; a slide that drops them compares different quantities.
- Picking by majority vote over patches. Different correct patches rarely match textually, so voting favours a common wrong patch over several distinct correct ones.
- Forgetting the filter's effect on correct patches. Flaky tests discard correct fixes too, and in rows with few correct samples that can remove all of them.
