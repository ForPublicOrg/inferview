## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 30 minutes">
A developer on your team ran Claude Code with an API key for three hours. Before you open the usage report, estimate the session's prompt-cache hit rate and its input-side bill from the events below. Then decide whether to set <code>promptCacheTtl</code> to <code>1h</code> for this developer, and say what would change your answer.
</Callout>

Published inputs, from the sources below (checked 2026-10-28). Opus 5.5 costs $4 per million input tokens, $5 for a five-minute cache write, $8 for a one-hour write and $0.20 for a cache read. Sonnet 5.5 costs $2, $2.50, $4 and $0.10. Both bill a 260K-token request at the same per-token rate as a short one. With an API key, Claude Code gives the main conversation the five-minute TTL unless you choose otherwise.

Scenario inputs, constructed for this problem:

- The fixed layers (system prompt, tool definitions, CLAUDE.md) are 20K tokens.
- While the developer works, one request goes out per minute, and each adds g tokens to the conversation: tool results, the model's output, the developer's messages. A week of this developer's traces puts g between 1.5K and 2.5K. Choose a value.
- The timeline:
  - 0:00 to 0:40, Opus 5.5, 40 requests.
  - 0:40 to 1:00, a meeting; the terminal sits idle.
  - 1:00 to 1:20, Opus 5.5, 20 requests.
  - 1:20, `/model sonnet`, then 40 requests on Sonnet 5.5 until 2:00.
  - 2:00, `/model opus`, then 20 requests until 2:20.
  - 2:20, `/compact`; the summary is 10K tokens.
  - 2:20 to 3:00, 40 requests.

Treat a warm request as reading the previous request's input from the cache and writing its g new tokens at the write price. Hit rate here is cache-read input tokens over all input tokens. Price the input side only: output tokens cost the same with or without caching. Give the hit rate, and the bill in dollars.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>With g = 2K the hit rate is about 96 percent and the input-side bill about $6.64; the same session with no caching would cost about $66. The hit rate hides where the money goes: the 4 percent of tokens written cost $3.50, more than the 19.2M tokens read ($3.14). Four full rewrites (cold start, the meeting, each model switch) are $2.09 of it, and switching back to Opus alone is $1.11. Keep the five-minute TTL for this developer: the one-hour TTL would cost $7.96 here, because it raises every write from 1.25x to 2x to save one idle gap.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The events that miss. The first request writes the fixed layers and its own tokens. The meeting lasts 20 minutes, longer than the five-minute TTL, so request 41 rewrites all 102K tokens. <code>/model sonnet</code> moves to a model with its own cache, so request 61 writes 142K on Sonnet. <code>/model opus</code> misses too: under the five-minute TTL the Opus entry expired at about 1:25, and under the one-hour TTL it is still alive but sits 40 turns behind the new breakpoint, past the 20-position lookback, so treat it as a miss either way (at best the 20K fixed layers hit, a 16-cent difference). Request 101 writes 222K on Opus. The compaction request reads the warm 260K prefix; the request after it reads the 20K fixed layers and writes the 10K summary plus 2K.</p>
<p>The warm stretches are arithmetic series. Requests 2 to 40 read 22K, 24K and so on up to 98K: 39 reads averaging 60K, or 2.34M tokens, and they write 39 × 2K = 78K. The other stretches follow the same pattern; the table below has each one.</p>
<p>Totals at g = 2K: 19.21M tokens read and 0.81M written, a hit rate of 19.21 / 20.02 = 96.0 percent. Reads cost 12.19M × $0.20 + 7.02M × $0.10 = $3.14 (the Opus and Sonnet shares). Writes cost 590K × $5 + 220K × $2.50 = $3.50. The bill is $6.64. With no caching, every token at base price, it would be $65.60.</p>
<p>The band. At g = 1.5K the bill is $5.24 and at g = 2.5K it is $8.04; the hit rate stays between 95.9 and 96.0 percent across the range. The hit rate barely moves because reads and writes both grow with g; the bill grows with it almost linearly.</p>
<p>The dominant term is the steady reads of a growing conversation, $3.14, because every request rereads everything before it. The decisions sit in the second term: the four full rewrites cost $2.09, almost a third of the bill, from four events the developer chose or could have timed.</p>
<p>The TTL decision. The one-hour TTL charges 2x instead of 1.25x on every written token. This session writes 488K Opus tokens and 220K Sonnet tokens it cannot avoid, so the longer TTL adds 0.488M × $3 + 0.22M × $1.50 = $1.79. It saves one rewrite: request 41 becomes a 100K read and a 2K write ($0.04) instead of a 102K write ($0.51), $0.47 saved. Net, $1.32 more: $7.96 instead of $6.64. Each idle gap of five to sixty minutes at about 100K of context saves about $0.48, so the one-hour TTL pays off for a developer with four or more such gaps per session at this context size, or fewer at larger contexts. That count, not the hit rate, is the assumption that flips the decision; measure it from the request timestamps.</p>
<p>The constraint conflict is cost against latency. Claude Code's docs note that the first turn back after a break longer than the TTL can be noticeably slower, because the whole input is processed again; a developer who notices that wait every time they return from a meeting will ask for the hour, and on this session the hour costs $1.32 more. Two further facts belong in the decision. The one-hour TTL applies to the main conversation only; subagents and compaction requests keep five minutes unless set separately. And on the one-hour TTL, Claude Code can compact a long conversation left idle (the <code>idleCompaction</code> setting, v2.1.290 or later), which changes what the developer comes back to.</p>
<p>Failure mode of the recommendation. Five-minute caches punish exactly the habit this developer has: stepping away mid-task with a large context. If the session shape changes (longer meetings, review waits between turns, a 200K context at each pause), the same decision costs more every day without any alert firing, because the hit rate still reads 96 percent. Track cache-write dollars per session, not the hit rate.</p>

The cheapest change the developer controls is the model switch. Requests 62 to 100 cost $0.90 on Sonnet against $1.79 on Opus, a saving of $0.90. The two switches cost $0.36 and $1.11 in rewrites, where staying on Opus would have made requests 61 and 101 ordinary warm requests at about $0.09 together. The round trip lost about $0.48: the same session entirely on Opus comes to $6.16. A switch pays off only when the stretch on the cheaper model is long, or when it comes right after `/compact`, when the context being rewritten is small.

### Choices that are not reasonable

- Treating cache reads as free. The bill becomes the writes alone, $3.50, and the dominant term disappears.
- Pricing Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 reads at 0.1x, the multiplier for most other models. The reads double and the bill comes to about $9.78.
- Counting the switch back to Opus as a hit because Opus already cached this conversation. The entry is either expired or out of lookback range; the $1.11 rewrite is the largest single line.
- Assuming `/compact` reprocesses the whole history at full price. While the cache is warm, the summarization request reads the prefix at the read price; it costs about five cents here.
- Judging the session by its hit rate. 96 percent sounds healthy and still leaves writes as more than half the bill.

## Common mistakes

- Pricing the session as one cold write plus 160 reads, which misses all four rewrites.
- Assuming the one-hour TTL is free insurance. It raises the write premium on every request, including the ones that never idle.
- Forgetting that each model keeps its own cache, so a switch and a switch back are two misses, not one.
- Reporting the hit rate as the answer. Two sessions with the same 96 percent can differ by more than a dollar in rewrites.
