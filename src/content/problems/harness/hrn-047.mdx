## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimation">
"Harness B came in three points above harness A on Terminal-Bench 2.0. How many trials do you need before you would believe a three-point difference?"
</Callout>

The pilot and the budget are a scenario; the benchmark size is published. Terminal-Bench 2.0 has 89 tasks. A pilot ran harness A five times on every task:

```text
passes out of 5    0    1    2    3    4    5
tasks             28    8    9   10   10   24
```

Harness B will run on the same 89 tasks, interleaved with A, with the same model snapshot and resource configuration. The team wants a 3-point true difference in mean pass rate to be detected at the 5% significance level, two-sided, at least 80% of the time. This week's budget is 3,000 trials across both harnesses.

Estimate k, the trials per task for each harness if every task gets the same number. State the noise assumption you chose, then say whether the budget covers it and what you would do if it does not.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which of the 89 tasks can contribute noise to a comparison between two harnesses run on the same tasks, given the pilot.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how much you trust a per-task pass rate measured from five trials, especially one of 0 or 5.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask whether the launch claim is about these 89 tasks or about coding tasks in general.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>About 21 trials per task per harness, with the pilot's per-task variances corrected for being measured from five trials; 17 if they are taken at face value; 49 if every task is assumed to be a coin flip. The dominant term is the Bernoulli variance of the 37 tasks whose pilot results varied; the 52 that always passed or always failed add almost nothing to a paired comparison. Uniform allocation at 21 needs 2 × 89 × 21 = 3,738 trials, over the budget, so put the trials on the varied tasks: 2,074 trials. The assumption that flips the conclusion is the scope of the claim: if it is about coding tasks in general, variation of the effect across tasks enters and a plausible amount of it uses up the whole three points whatever k is.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The quantity.</b> The estimate of the difference is D, the mean over the 89 tasks of B's pass rate minus A's on each task. Both arms run the same tasks, so task difficulty cancels and only trial noise is left. With per-task success probability p<sub>i</sub> and k trials per arm, each arm's per-task rate has variance p<sub>i</sub>(1 − p<sub>i</sub>) / k. Assuming B's per-task variance is close to A's:</p>
<p>Var(D) = 2 × Σ p<sub>i</sub>(1 − p<sub>i</sub>) / (89² × k)</p>
<p>Detecting 3 points at a two-sided 5% level with 80% power needs (1.96 + 0.84) × SE(D) ≤ 0.03, so SE(D) ≤ 0.0107 and Var(D) ≤ 1.148 × 10⁻⁴. Then k ≥ 2 × Σv / (7,921 × 1.148 × 10⁻⁴) = 2.20 × Σv, where Σv is the sum of per-task variances.</p>
<p><b>The assumption you choose: Σv.</b></p>
<ul>
<li><em>Plug in the pilot rates.</em> Tasks at 1 or 4 of 5 have 0.2 × 0.8 = 0.16, tasks at 2 or 3 of 5 have 0.24, tasks at 0 or 5 have 0. Σv = 18 × 0.16 + 19 × 0.24 = 2.88 + 4.56 = 7.44, so k ≥ 16.4: 17 trials.</li>
<li><em>Correct for the small sample.</em> p̂(1 − p̂) from n trials underestimates p(1 − p) by a factor (n − 1) / n, so multiply by 5/4: Σv = 9.30, k ≥ 20.5: 21 trials. This is the estimate to defend.</li>
<li><em>Assume nothing.</em> Every task at p = 0.5 gives Σv = 89 × 0.25 = 22.25 and k ≥ 49. Safe, and more than twice the trials the pilot says are needed.</li>
</ul>
<p>The accepted band, 15 to 50, covers these. Choices outside it are not reasonable. Using 1.96 alone and no power term gives k of about 10 at Σv = 9.30, and a design with 50% power misses half of the real three-point gains it was built to find. Reading the published Table 2 intervals (±1.4 to ±4.6 points per pair) and asking whether two entries overlap is not a test of a difference at all: overlap of two separate intervals neither confirms nor rules out a paired difference.</p>
<p><b>The budget conflict.</b> Uniform allocation at 21 costs 2 × 89 × 21 = 3,738 trials against a budget of 3,000. Two answers are defensible. Accept a larger detectable gap: at the budget, k = 16 per arm detects about 3.4 points (SE = √(2 × 9.30 / (7,921 × 16)) = 0.0121; × 2.8 = 0.034), which is honest and simple. Or allocate: tasks that always passed or always failed add no variance, so give the 37 varied tasks 21 trials per arm (2 × 37 × 21 = 1,554) and the 52 stable tasks 5 more per arm to catch a flip (520), 2,074 in total, which keeps the 3-point target inside the budget with about 900 trials spare for infra-error reruns.</p>
<p><b>How the allocation fails.</b> "Stable" was decided from five trials. A task that truly passes 80% of the time shows 5 of 5 with probability 0.8⁵ = 0.33, so some of the 24 "always passes" tasks are flaky and their variance is missing from Σv. The five top-up trials per arm catch most of them: any stable task that fails a top-up trial is moved to the varied group and topped up to 21. A harness change can also flip a stable task outright (a timeout change, a tool that now crashes), and that is signal, not noise; it shows up as a per-task difference of 1.0 and dominates D.</p>
<p><b>The assumption that flips it.</b> Everything above conditions on these 89 tasks. If the claim is "B is better on coding tasks like these", the per-task true effect varies across tasks, with some standard deviation τ, and Var(D) gains τ² / 89, a term that more trials do not shrink. At τ = 0.10, √(0.01 / 89) × 2.8 = 0.030: that term alone uses the whole three points. The fix is more tasks, not more trials.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: B costs twice as much per trial as A. Do you still give both arms the same k?* Not necessarily: for a fixed spend, the variance of the difference is smallest when trials per arm are proportional to the arm's standard deviation divided by the square root of its cost, so with equal variances B gets about 1/√2 of A's trials. Write down that this saves money at the price of a slightly wider band than equal allocation at the same trial count.
- *Reversal: what would make you abandon the targeted allocation and go back to uniform trials?* Top-up trials showing that a large share of the "stable" tasks fail now and then, so the pilot's split between stable and varied tasks was wrong; at that point the classification costs more than it saves.
- *The run comes back at +2.1 points with a paired 95% interval of ±2.6. What goes in the launch note?* That B is not shown to be better and not shown to be worse by more than about half a point, which supports shipping B if it is cheaper or simpler, and does not support a claim that it is better. Attach the configuration, because a gap under 3 points is where Anthropic says results stop transferring across setups.
- *The pilot ran last month on a different cluster. Can you still use it for Σv?* Only as a rough guide: infra errors and resource limits change which tasks are flaky. Run a short pilot on the cluster you will measure on, or inflate Σv and say so.

## Common mistakes

- Computing the band from a pooled pass rate, which charges the comparison for task difficulty that pairing removes.
- Designing for significance and forgetting power, so half of the real effects are missed.
- Treating a task that passed 5 of 5 in the pilot as certain to pass.
- Adding trials when the claim is about tasks in general, where only adding tasks helps.
- Reading overlapping published intervals as proof that two entries are tied.
