## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 30 minutes">
Your coding agent fixes bug tickets that come with a failing reproduction test. You can run it two ways: five attempts in parallel with a mid-size model, keeping the candidate your test-based verifier ranks first, or one attempt with a larger model and three times the turn budget. Price each per resolved ticket, decide which one to run for the next quarter, and name the assumption that flips your answer.
</Callout>

Published inputs, from the paper below (2024; models and prices of that time). On SWE-bench Lite, DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct inside the Moatless Tools agent solves 15.9 percent of issues with one attempt; Table 1 reports 29.62 percent with five attempts, and the paper reports 56 percent with 250. The paper also reports that 34 of the 300 problems have flaky test suites.

Scenario inputs, measured on your own 200-ticket backtest (constructed for this problem):

- **Best-of-five.** One attempt costs $0.50 and resolves 25 percent of tickets on its own. Running the verifier (the repository's tests plus the reproduction test) costs $0.04 per candidate. When at least one of the five candidates is correct, the verifier's top pick is correct 85 percent of the time.
- **One large attempt.** It costs $2.00, resolves 40 percent of tickets, and runs the verifier once.

What you do not have is the share of tickets where at least one of five attempts is correct. Choose it, and defend the choice. Give the cost per resolved ticket for best-of-five in dollars.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask for the backtest's per-ticket results across attempts: do the same tickets fail every time, or do failures move around between attempts?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the gap between one attempt and five in the paper's own numbers, and ask whether your tickets look more like its problems or less.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask how many tickets the backtest has and what interval that puts around the 40 percent.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Best-of-five costs $2.70 per ticket and resolves 0.85 × c of them, where c is the share with at least one correct candidate. With c around 0.5 that is about $6.35 per resolved ticket, against $5.10 for the single large attempt ($2.04 / 0.40). Run the single attempt by default and measure c before switching: best-of-five is cheaper per resolved ticket only above c = 0.62, and the paper's own numbers put five-attempt gains well below what independent attempts would give.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Cost per ticket. Five attempts at $0.50 is $2.50 and five verifier runs are $0.20, so $2.70; the attempts are 93 percent of it, so the dominant term is the attempt price, not the verifier. The single attempt costs $2.04 with its one verifier run.</p>
<p>Resolved share. Best-of-five resolves a ticket when some candidate is correct and the verifier picks a correct one: 0.85 × c. The single attempt resolves 0.40.</p>
<p>Choosing c. If attempts failed independently, c = 1 − 0.75<sup>5</sup> = 0.76. The paper says otherwise: its one-attempt rate is 15.9 percent and its five-attempt rate 29.62 percent, a factor of 1.86, where independence would predict 1 − 0.841<sup>5</sup> = 58 percent, a factor of 3.6. Attempts on the same ticket fail together because the hard tickets stay hard. Applying the paper's factor to 25 percent gives c = 0.47; your base rate is higher than the paper's, which leaves less room for a multiplier and argues for a little above that rather than far above. Anything from about 0.45 to 0.60 is defensible.</p>
<p>Per resolved ticket. At c = 0.45, $2.70 / 0.383 = $7.06. At 0.50, $6.35. At 0.55, $5.78. At 0.60, $5.29. The single attempt is $5.10. Best-of-five breaks even on cost at c = 2.70 / (0.85 × 5.10) = 0.62, and resolves more tickets than the single attempt from c = 0.40 / 0.85 = 0.47.</p>
<p>The decision has two defensible answers. If cost per resolved ticket rules, run the single attempt: at any c below 0.62 it is cheaper, its pipeline has one candidate and one verifier run, and reviewers see one patch per ticket. If backlog throughput rules, best-of-five resolves up to 51 percent of tickets at c = 0.60 against 40 percent, for $0.19 to $1.96 more per resolved ticket across the band. The constraint conflict is cost against recall, with reviewer time as the third party: every ticket where the verifier passes a wrong candidate sends a reviewer a patch that looks finished.</p>
<p>The assumption that flips it is c, which nobody has measured on this repository. Measuring it costs a backtest: five attempts on the 200 tickets is 200 × $2.70 = $540. At c = 0.5 the two configurations differ by $1.25 per resolved ticket, so the backtest pays for itself after about 430 resolved tickets ($540 / $1.25).</p>
<p>Failure mode of the recommendation. The 40 percent is a measurement on 200 tickets; its 95 percent interval is about ±6.8 points (1.96 × √(0.4 × 0.6 / 200)), so the single attempt's true cost per resolved ticket sits somewhere between about $4.36 and $6.14. That overlaps most of best-of-five's band: the recommendation can be wrong for a reason that has nothing to do with c. Run the backtest on both configurations with the same tickets and compare them paired.</p>
</Fragment>

<li><em>Run the five attempts one after another and stop at the first candidate the verifier passes. What does that do to cost per ticket, to wall-clock time, and to how much the verifier's mistakes matter?</em></li>
<li><em>Your verifier has flaky tests, as 34 of the paper's 300 problems did. Which configuration does flakiness hurt more, and how would you see it in the backtest?</em> Best-of-five, because a flaky pass on a wrong candidate wins the selection and a flaky failure discards a correct one; it shows up as candidates whose verifier result changes on rerun.</li>
<li><em>What would you need to see after a month in production to reverse the choice of the single attempt?</em> A measured c above 0.62 on paired tickets, or a throughput target that values the extra resolved tickets (7 to 11 per hundred at c between 0.55 and 0.60) above the extra cost.</li>

### Choices that are not reasonable

- Assuming independent attempts (c = 0.76). Best-of-five then costs $4.17 per resolved ticket and wins easily; the paper's own numbers show a five-attempt gain about half of what independence predicts.
- Using the paper's 56 percent at 250 attempts as the five-attempt figure. That is coverage after 50 times as many samples.
- Dropping the verifier's 85 percent selection accuracy. A correct candidate that the verifier does not pick resolves nothing; c alone overstates resolved tickets by about 18 percent.
- Comparing cost per attempt instead of cost per resolved ticket: the $0.50 attempt is a quarter of the large attempt's price, and five of them with selection still cost more per resolved ticket below c = 0.62.

## Follow-ups

- *Finance accepts best-of-five only if total spend stays flat. Which tickets would you route to it, and which signal available before the first attempt would you route on?*
- *A cascade runs the single large attempt first and sends its verifier failures to best-of-five. Price it per resolved ticket with your chosen c, and say what you assumed about how the two configurations' failures overlap.*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one says the paper shows repeated sampling beats frontier models on cost, the other says it was measured on 2024 models and does not transfer. How do you settle it without deciding by rank?*

## Common mistakes

- Quoting the paper's headline (five cheap attempts beat one frontier attempt at under a third of the price) as if it applied to these prices and pass rates. Its cheap attempt cost about one eighteenth of a GPT-4o attempt; here the cheap attempt is a quarter of the price of the large one.
- Treating the verifier as an oracle because it runs the real tests. Tests decide only what they check, and selection among five candidates amplifies whatever they miss.
- Reporting one point estimate without the range of c that changes the decision.
