## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt, as a candidate report describes it">
"Why did your team build its own coding agent when Claude Code exists?" A candidate reported this from a Tencent first round, followed by questions on how the product differs, layered context, summary quality, subagent communication and loop prevention.
</Callout>

Answer it as the engineer who owns the decision, speaking to a director who thinks the project should stop. Use your own project. If you have none, use this scenario and its numbers, which are invented for the exercise:

- Five engineers have spent two quarters on an in-house agent that fixes failing tests in CI. Two of them now spend most of their time keeping it working across model and API changes.
- It talks to an internal test-impact service directly. The same engineers also use Claude Code on their laptops.
- On 60 recorded CI failures, the in-house agent fixed 38. Claude Code, run without a terminal with an MCP server for the test-impact service and the team's hooks, fixed 41. Both fixed the same 33; 5 were fixed only in-house and 8 only by Claude Code.
- The director wants a decision this quarter.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which of the five fixed-only-in-house failures depended on something the rented harness cannot reach through its extension points.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask for cost per fixed failure for both, from the API usage records rather than estimates.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask the director what result would change their mind, before you present yours.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Scenario answer: "I recommended we stop building the loop and keep building what only we can. On our 60 recorded failures the two agents differ by 3 fixes, and the 13 failures only one of them fixed split 5 to 8; if the two were equally good, a split at least that uneven would happen more than half the time, so our eval cannot tell them apart. What it can tell us is cost: two of five engineers maintain a loop that a vendor maintains for free. So we moved to the Agent SDK with our test-impact service behind MCP and our policy in hooks, kept the five in-house-only cases as regression tests, and put the two engineers on the eval set and the sandbox. I would reverse it if a larger recorded set showed our loop ahead by the margin we agreed on, or if a harness upgrade broke the MCP path and we could not pin around it."</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Situation and the decision, in two sentences.</strong> Who uses the agent, what it costs to keep, and the question on the table: keep building the loop, or rent it.</p>
<p><strong>The alternatives you weighed, with their costs.</strong> Keep building: full control of request assembly, model choice and telemetry, at the price of two engineers tracking every model and API change. Adopt Claude Code or the Agent SDK: the loop, caching, compaction and tool ergonomics come maintained, at the price of control over the request and over when it changes, since a new version can change the system prompt and tools. The honest test of "build" is what the rented harness cannot do through its extension points: MCP for the internal service, hooks and managed settings for policy, the SDK for embedding the loop in CI. Reasons that survive that test are narrow: a model family the vendor's harness does not support, an agent that is itself the product you sell, or an eval that shows your loop wins on your tasks by more than noise.</p>
<p><strong>The measurement.</strong> With paired results on the same 60 failures, only the 13 discordant ones carry information. Under "equally good", the split of 13 is binomial with p = 0.5; the chance of 5 or fewer for one side is (1 + 13 + 78 + 286 + 715 + 1,287) / 8,192 = 2,380 / 8,192, about 0.29, and about 0.58 counting either side. A gap of 3 on 60 is not evidence either way. The quantity you choose and defend is the gap that would justify two engineers: for example, at least 10 more failures fixed per 60, which is a decision about value, not a statistic, and it tells you how many more recorded failures you need before the eval can show it.</p>
<p><strong>Influence.</strong> Agree with the director on the decision rule before showing results (Hint 3), so the meeting is about the rule, not about whose project it is. Bring the cost per fixed failure for both. Write down what the team loses, and say it first: the people who built the loop lose ownership of it.</p>
<p><strong>The result and what you would reverse it on.</strong> The decision, the date, and the triggers. The failure mode of the recommendation belongs in the same breath: the rented harness can change under you on upgrade, and its compaction may drop your internal tool's output in ways you cannot fix, so pin the version, run the eval before every upgrade, and keep the adapter boundary that lets the in-house loop come back.</p>
</Fragment>

### What the story has to show

Rate your answer against these signals.

- **Ownership.** You owned the decision, not just the code; "I recommended" with the date.
- **Alternatives.** At least two options with a cost each, including the one you argued against.
- **A measured result.** Paired numbers on the same tasks, the gap you would have needed, and the cost per fixed task.
- **Influence.** How the director and the team that built the loop came along, and what you conceded to them.
- **A reversal condition.** What evidence would make you change your mind.

The scenario was built so that "stop building the loop" wins. With your own project the honest answer may be "keep building", and it holds up when it passes the same test: name the capability the rented harness cannot reach, show it in your own eval, and price it.

## Follow-ups

- *Layered context: how does your agent order what goes into each request, and what breaks the cache?* Static instructions and tools first, project guidance next, the conversation last; configuration changes appended as messages.
- *Summary quality: how do you know your compaction summary keeps what matters?* Force compaction at a fixed point in recorded runs and compare completion against the same runs without it.
- *Subagent communication and loop prevention: what does a subagent return, and what stops two agents handing a task back and forth?* A bounded summary rather than the transcript; a turn and spend cap shared across the tree; a repeat detector on delegations.
- *Two of your senior engineers disagree with the decision and say the vendor will deprecate the features you depend on. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*

## Common mistakes

- Defending the build with features the rented harness already has through MCP, hooks, skills or the SDK.
- Quoting the 41 against 38 as a win without the paired view of the 13 failures that differ.
- Leaving out the cost of the people who maintain the loop.
- Treating the question as loyalty to your team's project; recommending against your own build is part of owning the decision when the data says so.
- No reversal condition, which makes the decision sound like a preference.
