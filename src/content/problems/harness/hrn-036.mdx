## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 45 minutes">
Most pull requests in your organisation are now opened by coding agents that engineers launch. Review has become the bottleneck and the VP of engineering wants agent reviewers to approve low-risk changes. Design code review for this world: who reviews what, what is checked mechanically, and what still needs a person.
</Callout>

The scenario's facts, invented for this problem: 45 engineers; about 110 merged PRs a working day, 70% of them opened by agents. A careful review takes a median of 25 minutes and a light review 8, measured on your own PRs last quarter. The engineering leads have agreed to cap review at 50 minutes per engineer per day. A quarter of agent PRs touch paths with code owners (auth, payments, data migrations); half touch other deployed services; a quarter touch only code that is never deployed (internal tools, docs, test fixtures).

Operating constraints:

- Your auditor's change-management control reads: "every change to production is approved by a person other than its author."
- GitHub Copilot code review can now submit approvals that count toward required reviews (public preview, off by default). The VP wants it on.
- One staff engineer (you) and one platform engineer, one quarter.

Choose how much of the agent-approved work a person audits after merge, and defend the number.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask the auditor who the author of an agent's PR is: the agent's bot account, or the engineer who launched it.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request last quarter's review minutes against review capacity, by kind of path.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what fraction of agent PRs that passed agent review a person would have sent back, and whether anyone has measured it.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Three tiers by path, with mechanical checks on all of them. Code that is never deployed merges on CI plus an agent review, with a person auditing a sample after merge. Deployed code needs one human approval, light where an independent agent review has passed, careful where code owners apply. The launching engineer counts as the author for the auditor's control, so their approval never counts on their own agent's PR. Agent approvals, including Copilot's, are allowed only on the never-deployed paths, because the control asks for a person. The audit sample measures the agent reviewer's miss rate; that number, not the VP's preference, decides whether the agent-approved tier grows.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Capacity first, because it decides the shape.</strong> Capacity is 45 × 50 = 2,250 review minutes a day. Reviewing all 110 PRs carefully costs 110 × 25 = 2,750, so careful review of everything does not fit, and the way it fails in practice is skimming, not a queue. The tiered plan: the 33 human PRs reviewed as today, 33 × 25 = 825; agent PRs on code-owned paths, about 19 a day, careful, 19 × 25 = 475; agent PRs on other deployed paths, about 39, light, 39 × 8 = 312; agent PRs on never-deployed code, about 19, no pre-merge person, with a post-merge audit of a fifth of them, 4 × 25 = 100. Total about 1,710 minutes, leaving about 540 a day for re-reviews after agents push changes.</p>
<p><strong>Mechanical, on every PR.</strong> CI that runs the base branch's tests against the PR, lint and types, a size limit with a stated exception path, and a check that the PR description's list of touched areas matches the diff. These are the checks Anthropic's Agent SDK post calls rules-based feedback: cheap, deterministic and hard to argue with.</p>
<p><strong>The agent reviewer, and why independence matters.</strong> A reviewer that shares the author's context inherits its reasoning. Claude Code's docs recommend a fresh reviewer that never saw the conversation, given read-only tools, reviewing against the spec or issue and told to skip style. Anthropic's harness post shows the risk on the other side: its evaluator found real bugs and then talked itself into approving, and only became useful after the author tuned it against his own judgement. So the reviewer is a separate session with Read, Grep and Glob, given the issue and the diff but not the author's transcript, and its prompt is tuned the same way: on PRs where a person disagreed with it.</p>
<p><strong>Who the author is.</strong> GitHub's "approval of the most recent reviewable push by someone other than the pusher" is satisfied when the agent's bot pushes and the engineer who launched it approves. That passes the branch rule and fails the auditor's control, whose author is the person who asked for the change. The PR records the launching engineer, and a required check fails when that person is the only approver.</p>
<p><strong>The decision with two defensible answers.</strong> OpenAI's harness team pushed almost all review to agents, with humans optional and minimal blocking gates, and says plainly that this would be irresponsible in a low-throughput environment; it works there because corrections are cheap and the product is internal. That is defensible for code whose mistakes are cheap to reverse. The cost is that defects reach <code>main</code> and are found by users or by cleanup: their team spent every Friday on it before encoding rules. The other answer is a human approval on every deployed change, tiered by depth. Its cost is the roughly 1,600 minutes a day of human review computed above and the risk that light review becomes no review. With an auditor's control and payments code in the repository, I choose the tiered human approval for deployed code, and agent-only approval where the never-deployed paths make a miss cheap.</p>
<p><strong>The audit rate, computed.</strong> A fifth of the 19 agent-approved PRs a day is about 4, or about 80 in a 20-day month. If the agent reviewer misses a defect a person would have caught on 5% of PRs, a month's audit finds about 4, and the 95% interval on the rate is about ±1.96 × √(0.05 × 0.95 / 80) ≈ ±4.8 points. A month can tell 5% from 15%, which is the distinction that matters for widening or closing the tier; it cannot tell 5% from 8%. A tenth halves the cost and widens the interval to about ±6.8 points; reasonable choices sit between a tenth and a third. Auditing everything would put the tier back into the queue it was built to leave.</p>
<p><strong>What you give up.</strong> Deployed agent PRs still wait for a person, so agent throughput stays bounded by review minutes. The VP gets agent approvals on a quarter of agent PRs, not most of them. Light review accepts that some defects on deployed non-owned paths are found after merge.</p>
<p><strong>Failure mode of this design.</strong> Tiers by path are only as good as the path map. A feature-flag default in a config file, a lockfile bump or a generated client can change production behaviour from a path marked low risk, and an agent that learns where review is light will put changes there without meaning to. The second failure is correlated blindness: if the reviewer and the author are the same model family, they miss the same things, and the audit will measure that as a low miss rate on the defects both can see. Keep the path map in a code-owned file, route lockfile and configuration changes to the deployed tier, and have the audit sample include defects found later in production, not only what the auditor spots.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why not turn on Copilot approvals for the deployed tier and keep a person only on code-owned paths?</em> Because the control asks for a person, and an approval that counts toward the required count would satisfy GitHub while failing the audit. If the auditor accepts an agent approval for some class of change in writing, that changes the answer for that class.</li>
<li><em>Light reviews drift from 8 minutes to 2. What do you do?</em> Measure it from review timestamps, then add the post-merge audit to the light tier as well; an 8-minute review that takes 2 is an approval on the checks.</li>
<li><em>How does the agent reviewer get better?</em> From the audit: every PR where a person disagreed becomes a few-shot example or a rule, the way Anthropic tuned its evaluator against the author's own judgement.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>

### The written artefact

The review policy, one page, signed by the engineering leads and shown to the auditor.

- **Tier A, never deployed** (path map in `review-tiers.yml`, code-owned): CI, an independent agent review, merge on green; a person audits a fifth of merged PRs within five working days.
- **Tier B, deployed, no code owner:** CI, independent agent review, then one light human approval by someone other than the launching engineer.
- **Tier C, code-owned paths, lockfiles, configuration and migrations:** CI, agent review, careful code-owner approval; approvals dismissed on new commits.
- **Measured monthly:** the agent reviewer's miss rate from the tier A audit; minutes per light review; agent PRs reverted or patched within 14 days, by tier.
- **Rollback triggers:** a tier A audit miss rate above 10% in a month moves tier A to human approval; any production incident traced to a tier A merge does the same at once; light reviews with a median under 3 minutes for two weeks move tier B to careful review on a sample.

## Follow-ups

- *Your tiering, audit and reviewer tuning cost more than the agents save in engineer time this quarter. Which half do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *What would you need to see in two quarters of audit data before letting agent approvals count on tier B?*
- *The payments team says agent PRs should never touch their paths, and the platform team says the agent's fixes there are the best they get. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *An engineer launches agents that open forty PRs a day against tier A paths. What breaks first in this design, and what limit do you add?*

## Common mistakes

- Counting the bot account as the author, so the engineer who launched the agent approves their own change.
- Letting the agent reviewer see the author's transcript, which turns the review into agreement with reasoning it already read.
- Turning on agent approvals because the platform supports them, without checking what the change-management control says.
- Assigning tiers by directory and forgetting configuration, lockfiles and generated code.
- Measuring the agent reviewer by how many comments it leaves rather than by what a person finds after it approved.
