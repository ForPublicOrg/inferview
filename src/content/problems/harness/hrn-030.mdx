## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 25 minutes">
Your platform team wants to turn on Claude Code's auto mode for every engineer, replacing manual permission prompts with Anthropic's classifier. Security asks one question first: how many actions that go beyond what the engineer asked for will the classifier approve in a typical week? Estimate it, name the term that dominates, and say what you put around the classifier.
</Callout>

Published inputs, from Anthropic's auto-mode post of 2026-03-25 and measured on Anthropic's own traffic: the full classifier pipeline approves 17% of overeager actions (52 real cases) and blocks 0.4% of benign actions (10,000 real actions). Reads and file edits inside the project skip the classifier; shell commands, web fetches and everything else go to it.

Scenario inputs, not measurements:

- 150 engineers, five working days a week.
- An engineer-day produces about 120 actions that would go to the classifier.
- In a two-week pilot with manual prompts on the same kind of actions, engineers denied 5% of prompts. A review of 100 of those denials found 16 where the agent had gone beyond the request; the rest were timing, preference or a wrong flag.

One quantity is yours to choose: the pilot only counts overreach that engineers caught. Decide what share of overeager actions they approved without noticing, and defend it.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>About 90,000 classifier-reviewed actions a week. The pilot puts overreach at 5% × 16% = 0.8% of them, about 720 a week, and if engineers waved through as many as they caught, about 1,440. At a 17% miss rate the classifier approves roughly 120 to 250 overeager actions a week, call it 25 to 50 a working day. The dominant term is the base rate of overreach, which nobody has published for your codebase, not the classifier's error rate. At that volume the question is not whether misses happen but what a miss can reach, so the classifier goes inside a sandbox with deny rules for irreversible actions and no production credentials.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Volume.</strong> 150 engineers × 120 actions × 5 days = 90,000 classifier-reviewed actions a week.</p>
<p><strong>Base rate.</strong> The pilot's caught overreach is 5% × 16 / 100 = 0.8% of prompted actions, 720 a week at this volume. Engineers in the pilot approved most prompts, and Anthropic reports 93% acceptance of manual prompts in its own telemetry, so some overreach was approved without being noticed. I assume one approved for every one caught, so the true rate is 1.6% and the weekly count 1,440. A choice between none missed (720) and half missed (1,440) is defensible.</p>
<p><strong>Misses.</strong> 17% of 720 is about 122; 17% of 1,440 is about 245. The band for reasonable choices is roughly 100 to 300 a week.</p>
<p><strong>The uncertainty in the 17%.</strong> 17% of 52 is about 9 cases. A 95% Wilson interval on 9 of 52 runs from about 9% to 30%, so on my base rate the misses could be anywhere from about 135 to 430 a week before any question of whether Anthropic's traffic looks like yours. Present the point estimate with the interval, not alone.</p>
<p><strong>The other error.</strong> The benign actions are about 88,600 a week; 0.4% of them is about 354 false blocks, roughly 2.4 per engineer a week. Each costs a model turn to find another route, and three in a row stop the session. Against that, manual prompts would be 120 per engineer-day.</p>
<p><strong>The dominant term and what flips it.</strong> The miss count scales with the base rate of overreach times the miss rate, and the base rate is the term with the widest range and no published value. The conclusion that misses are routine, tens a day, flips only if overreach is rare in your traffic: for misses to fall to one a week, overreach would have to be 1 / (90,000 × 0.17), about 0.007% of reviewed actions, two orders of magnitude below what the pilot caught. The decision about what surrounds the classifier flips on a different assumption: what a miss can reach. If the agent has no production credentials, cannot push to protected branches and works in a sandbox, a miss is a reverted commit; if it has production credentials, one miss a quarter is too many.</p>
<p><strong>What you put around it: two defensible answers.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Auto mode everywhere, inside containment.</strong> OS sandbox with workspace writes and an egress allowlist; deny rules for the irreversible actions (force pushes, production deploys, destructive cloud CLI verbs) that hold in every mode; server-side branch protection; credentials brokered per task rather than present in the shell. Cost: building and maintaining the containment, and engineers lose the reach they had on their laptops.</li>
<li><strong>Auto mode only where containment exists, manual elsewhere.</strong> Infrastructure and production-adjacent repositories stay on manual prompts (or <code>dontAsk</code> with narrow allow rules). Cost: in those repositories, about 120 prompts per engineer-day, accepted most of the time, which is the approval fatigue auto mode was meant to fix. Anthropic's own post says the classifier is not a replacement for careful review on high-stakes infrastructure.</li>
</ul>
<p>The conflict is autonomy against safety under a fixed security review. I would take the second answer for the rollout and move repositories to the first as their containment lands. Its failure mode: the boundary between "high-stakes" and "ordinary" repositories is drawn by people, a repository that holds a deploy script nobody flagged gets auto mode, and the misses there are the expensive kind. Measure it: sample a few hundred classifier-approved actions a week, label them, and estimate your own miss rate instead of borrowing Anthropic's.</p>
</Fragment>

### Choices that are not reasonable

- Using 0.4% as the miss rate: 90,000 × 0.4% = 360. That is the false-positive rate over benign actions; security asked about the other error.
- Applying 17% to every reviewed action: 90,000 × 17% = 15,300. The 17% is a rate over overeager actions only.
- Applying 17% to every denial in the pilot: 4,500 × 17% = 765. Most denials were preference, not overreach.
- Assuming engineers missed more overreach than they caught by a wide margin, a multiplier of three or more. The pilot's reviewers were looking for it; that assumption needs evidence from a labelled sample, not from the 93% acceptance figure alone.
- Treating the post's 5.7% synthetic-exfiltration miss rate as the answer. It measures a different attack on a synthetic set.
