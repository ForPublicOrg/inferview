## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"A customer says your support bot feels dumb. What do you change first?"
</Callout>

The trap is in "change first". Answer the question you would actually work on: what do you find out before changing anything, how do you turn "dumb" into something you can measure, and what is the first change you would test?

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask for examples before you form a theory. "Dumb" covers at least four different failures.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Each hypothesis should name a metric that would move if it were true.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Nothing yet. I would ask for ten or twenty conversations they consider dumb, read them with the customer, and sort the failures: wrong facts, right facts but missed intent, correct but unhelpful (vague, long, hedging), or unable to act (no tool, no access). The biggest bucket becomes a hypothesis with a metric, a small labelled set built from those conversations, and one change tested against it. "Dumb" turns into, for example, "resolves 6 of 20 refund questions; target 15".</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Clarify. Who said it, about which conversations, compared with what? A frontline agent comparing the bot with themselves and an executive who tried it once mean different things. I ask for concrete transcripts and what a good answer would have been for each.</p>
<p>Diagnose by reading. I label each transcript with one primary failure. Retrieval failures: the right document existed but was not found, or was not in the index. Instruction failures: the bot answered a different question, ignored context the user gave, or lost a detail from earlier in the conversation. Style failures: correct but long, generic or over-cautious, which users experience as dumb even when the facts are right. Capability failures: the user needed an action (refund, change an address) the bot cannot take.</p>
<p>Turn the largest bucket into a hypothesis and a number. If it is retrieval, the metric is whether the needed document is in the retrieved context for those questions. If it is style, the metric is a rubric score from a judge I have checked against my own labels on the same set. If it is capability, the answer is product scope, not prompting, and the customer needs to decide whether to grant the tool.</p>
<p>Experiment. One change at a time, run on the labelled set before and after, and shown to the customer with the transcripts, not only the score. Then add the set to a regression suite so the next prompt change cannot quietly undo it.</p>
<p>What I would not do first: switch models, or rewrite the system prompt from scratch. Either might help, and neither tells you why.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>The customer will not share transcripts for privacy reasons.</em> Ask them to label failures on their side with a short form, or work from redacted samples, and agree what data the eval set may hold.</li>
<li><em>Your fix improves the set but the customer still says it feels dumb.</em> The set does not represent what they see. Collect a fresh sample from recent traffic and compare the failure mix.</li>
<li><em>How do you decide whether this bot should exist at all?</em> If the high-volume questions need actions the bot cannot be trusted with, a better search page or a form might serve users more cheaply.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

The numbers in the 30-second answer are an illustration of the shape of a good target, not a benchmark: a count of successes out of a named set, with a goal agreed with the customer. What an interviewer listens for is the order of operations. Examples, then categories, then a hypothesis with a metric, then one change measured against it.

This matches the forward-deployed job descriptions, which put evaluation frameworks next to prompt engineering and agent development. In a customer-facing round, the eval is how you make a vague complaint into a commitment both sides can check.

## Follow-ups

- *Write the rubric for "helpful".* Three to five observable criteria (answers the question asked, uses the customer's details, gives a next step, under a length limit), each scored yes or no, so two people label the same transcript the same way.
- *What if the complaint is latency, not quality?* Measure time to first token and total time on the same transcripts. A complaint about quality can be a complaint about waiting, so check both; streaming the answer changes what the user waits for without changing the model.
- *Who owns the fix if the root cause is stale documentation?* The customer does. Say so, offer a report of the questions the documents cannot answer, and keep that out of the bot's metrics.

## Common mistakes

- Proposing a model upgrade or a prompt rewrite before seeing a single transcript.
- Accepting "dumb" as one problem rather than a label for several.
- Measuring with an LLM judge you have not checked against your own reading.
- Fixing style problems by adding more instructions to the system prompt until it contradicts itself.
- Reporting only a score to the customer, without the transcripts that make it believable.
