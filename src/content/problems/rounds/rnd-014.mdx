## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Customer round">
"Our head of support says your bot feels dumb. Our quarterly review is in three weeks and she will present on it. What do you do?"
</Callout>

What the interviewer tells you when asked (scenario):

- The customer sells payroll software. The bot answers their end users' support questions from the help centre and can look up an account's pay schedule; it cannot change anything.
- It handles about 2,000 conversations a week. Transcripts contain employee pay data, so they may be read only inside the customer's environment or after redaction.
- The head of support has forwarded four transcripts. Two are long answers to short questions; one confuses two products; one says it cannot help with a pay date the bot could have looked up.
- You have one engineer for three weeks. Nobody has labelled a transcript yet.

Turn the complaint into hypotheses, choose the sample you read and its size, design one experiment, and say what result would make you ship the change, what result would make you stop, and what you take to the review.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether the four forwarded transcripts were picked because they were typical or because they were the worst.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the share of conversations that end in a human handoff or a repeat contact within a day, split by the bot's top intents.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what result the head of support would accept as "fixed" before you run anything.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Read before changing anything. I take the forwarded transcripts plus a random sample of about 100 from the last month, read them inside the customer's environment, and give each one a primary failure label written in my own words. Grouping the labels gives the hypotheses: retrieval missed the right article, the bot answered a different question, the tool was not called when it should have been, or the answer was correct but long. The largest group becomes a binary pass or fail test on a set built from real conversations, one change is run against it and against a guard set of conversations that pass today, and the decision rule is written before the run. At the review she gets the failure mix, the before and after counts on the same items, and the transcripts behind them.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The sample, and its size.</strong> The four forwarded transcripts are where the complaint comes from, not what users see on a normal day, so I read them and add a random sample drawn from the whole month. A sample of two weeks is cheaper but can miss month-end, when pay-run questions arrive; for a payroll product that is the traffic most likely to differ. On size: a group that holds 20 percent of conversations, measured on 100, has a 95 percent interval of about 1.96 × √(0.2 × 0.8 / 100) ≈ ±8 points. That is enough to rank groups that differ by 15 points or more, and 100 transcripts take one engineer about a day. Anything from 60 to 150 is defensible; 20 is not, since the same group measured on 20 transcripts carries about ±18 points and the ranking is noise.</p>
<p><strong>Labels before categories.</strong> For each transcript I write one sentence on the first thing that went wrong, then group the sentences. Starting from a fixed taxonomy hides failures that do not fit it. Husain's field guide makes the same argument and shows the payoff: in his NurtureBoss example the reading found date handling failing on 66 percent of such requests, and targeted fixes took that from 33 to 95 percent success.</p>
<p><strong>Hypotheses, each with what would move.</strong> Suppose the month sample groups as follows (scenario counts): the right help article existed but was not retrieved (31 of 100), the answer was correct but long or hedged (22), the account lookup was not called when the question needed it (14), two products confused (6), and 27 acceptable. Each group predicts a different measurement. Retrieval: the needed article is absent from the retrieved context. Length: the answer is right and a handoff or repeat contact follows anyway. Lookup: the question names a pay date and no tool call appears in the trace.</p>
<p><strong>The decision that conflicts with the deadline.</strong> The head of support noticed the long answers; the sample says retrieval is the largest group. Fixing length first is cheap, visible at the review and likely to change how she feels about the bot. Fixing retrieval first moves more conversations but needs re-chunking and an index rebuild that may not land in three weeks. I would do both in order of cost, and say so: the length change first, measured, then retrieval, reported as in progress if it is not finished. If only one fits, retrieval, because a shorter wrong answer is still wrong.</p>
<p><strong>The experiment.</strong> For the retrieval group I build a set of 60 real questions with the article that answers each, labelled pass or fail by me and checked by one of her senior agents on 20 of them. Pass means the answer is correct and uses the right article. Baseline, say, 24 of 60. The decision rule, written down first: ship if the net gain is at least 12 items (items that flip from fail to pass, minus items that flip from pass to fail) and no more than 2 of a 40-item guard set of currently passing conversations fail. Stop and rethink if the net gain is under 6, a size I would first check against what re-running the unchanged bot on the same 60 items produces. Comparing the same items before and after, item by item, is what makes 60 enough.</p>
<p><strong>What I take to the review.</strong> The failure mix with its counts, the items that flipped, and three transcripts before and after. If the lookup group is real, that is also a scope conversation: the tool exists and the bot is not using it, which is fixable, while a request to change a pay date is a capability she would have to grant.</p>
<p><strong>Where this plan fails.</strong> The labels are mine. If I misread what her users count as a good answer, I optimise the wrong thing and the bot still feels dumb. Having her senior agent label 20 of the same items and reading our disagreements catches this early; more than a handful of disagreements means the definition of pass needs rewriting before any experiment. The set is also built from today's failures, so it rewards changes that fix them and says nothing about what those changes break elsewhere; the guard set covers part of that and the next random sample covers the rest.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>She will not let anyone outside her team read transcripts, even redacted.</em> Her agents label a sample on their side with a one-line form per transcript and the failure groups you give them; you work from counts and the few transcripts they choose to redact.</li>
<li><em>The fix passes your set but she still says it feels dumb.</em> Compare the failure mix of a fresh random sample with the first one; if the mix moved, her impression is anchored on old transcripts, and if it did not, your set does not represent her traffic.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *The review is in three weeks and the retrieval fix needs an index rebuild that takes four. What do you put in front of her, and in what words?* The length change with its measured result, the retrieval failure count with the date the fix lands, and the guard-set result showing nothing broke.
- *You want an LLM judge so the 60-item test can run on every prompt change. What do you require before it replaces your labels?* Its verdicts compared with yours on the same items, with every disagreement read; agreement on items you already understand is cheap to check and expensive to skip.
- *The lookup group turns out to be 30 percent of conversations at month-end and 5 percent otherwise. Does that change your order of work?* Yes if the review falls near month-end or the customer's support load peaks then; the month-long sample that found it is the argument.

## Common mistakes

- Changing the model or rewriting the system prompt before reading a transcript.
- Reading only the transcripts the customer forwarded and treating them as the traffic.
- Starting from a fixed taxonomy of failures instead of writing what went wrong in each transcript.
- Scoring with a 1 to 5 scale, which makes a half-point change impossible to interpret.
- Running the experiment without a written decision rule, then reading any improvement as success.
- Taking a score to the customer without the transcripts behind it.
