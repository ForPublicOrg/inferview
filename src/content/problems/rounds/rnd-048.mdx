## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported format">
A candidate report from a staff machine learning engineer loop at Cohere lists a take-home task: present a paper chosen from a list the company provided. The report gives no length and no questions. The length, the list and the probes below are constructed for this problem.
</Callout>

The team you would join works on post-training. You get this list a week before the round:

1. Hoffmann et al., *Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models* (2022).
2. Shao et al., *DeepSeekMath* (2024), the paper that introduced GRPO.
3. Biderman et al., *LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less* (2024).
4. Gekhman et al., *Does Fine-Tuning LLMs on New Knowledge Encourage Hallucinations?* (2024).

You have twenty minutes to present, then the panel probes. Choose a paper, choose how to split the twenty minutes, and write down the three probes you expect to be hardest. Then prepare the talk and run it once against a clock.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Pick the paper whose result changes a decision the team makes, present it as question, design, one number with its conditions, limits and practice, and keep a third of the time for evidence. For this list and a post-training team, <em>LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less</em> works: it answers "when does a low-rank adapter cost us quality, and what does it buy back", with a design the panel can audit. The risk is that the panel knows the paper better than you, so the limits section has to be your own reading, not the abstract's.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The choice.</b> Two defensible picks. The paper closest to the team's work (LoRA, or GRPO) shows you can talk about their problems; its cost is an audience that may include people who have run the same experiments, so a thin spot shows in the first probe. A paper adjacent to their work that you know deeply (the new-knowledge hallucination paper, which is about fine-tuning data) gives you a deeper defence; its cost is that it can read as avoiding the obvious pick, and you have to make the bridge to their work yourself in the talk.</p>
<p><b>The split.</b> One reasonable allocation of 20 minutes: 2 on the question, 5 on the design, 7 on the evidence, 4 on limits, 2 on practice (2 + 5 + 7 + 4 + 2 = 20). Spending fewer than 5 minutes on evidence turns the talk into a summary of the abstract; spending more than 10 leaves no time for limits, which is where a staff panel spends its probes. Any split that keeps limits at 3 minutes or more is defensible.</p>
<p><b>The constraint conflict.</b> Twenty minutes is not enough for both breadth (all four regimes, the spectral analysis, the hyperparameter sweeps) and depth (one result defended to its conditions). Depth wins, and the cost is stated below.</p>
</Fragment>

<ul>

### A worked version on the LoRA paper

**Question (2 minutes).** Does LoRA match full finetuning when the target domain is far from pretraining, and if not, what does it give back? Every post-training team chooses between adapters and full updates for each run, so the question is theirs.

**Design (5 minutes).** One base model, Llama-2-7B. Two domains, code and maths. Two regimes: instruction finetuning on about 100K prompt-response pairs, and continued pretraining on up to 20B tokens. LoRA at ranks 16, 64 and 256 on all modules, against full finetuning. Learning is measured on HumanEval and GSM8K; forgetting on HellaSwag, WinoGrande and ARC-Challenge. The point to land is what is held fixed: the base model, the data and the evaluations, so differences come from the update method and its hyperparameters.

**Evidence (7 minutes).** Two numbers, each with its conditions. In code instruction finetuning, LoRA at rank 256 reached 0.498 on HumanEval at epoch 4 against 0.497 for full finetuning at epoch 8, so a high-rank adapter can match full finetuning in that regime. In code continued pretraining at 20B tokens, the source-domain average was 0.545 for full finetuning and 0.617 for LoRA at rank 256: full finetuning learned more and forgot more. Then the spectral result: the paper reports that the weight change from full finetuning needs a rank 10 to 100 times larger than typical LoRA ranks to explain 90 percent of its variance, which is the authors' explanation for the continued-pretraining gap.

**Limits (4 minutes).** The authors state that the high-rank finding does not rule out low-rank solutions, that the spectral analysis covers continued pretraining only, and that scaling with model size is future work. Add your own: one base model, so "LoRA forgets less" is a 7B Llama-2 result; the forgetting benchmarks are multiple-choice scores, which measure a narrow slice of what a product loses; and the throughput comparison is for standard implementations at a fixed batch size.

**Practice (2 minutes).** The paper's recommendations are adapters for instruction finetuning rather than continued pretraining, all modules rather than attention only, rank 256 where memory allows, alpha at twice the rank, and a learning-rate sweep. For this team, the decision it informs is which runs get full updates.

### What depth costs, stated

Going deep on two numbers and the spectral result means the hyperparameter sections, the diversity analysis and the Tülu experiments get one sentence each. A probe on any of them has to be answered from your reading notes, not from the talk. Prepare one sentence for each skipped section with what it claims and how much it changes the conclusion. That is also the failure mode of this answer: the panel picks the skipped section precisely because you skipped it, and a sentence prepared the night before is thinner than a slide.

### The hardest probes to rehearse against

- Why should a 7B result tell us anything about the models we train?
- Your two numbers come from different epochs. Is that a fair comparison?
- What would you run first to check whether "forgets less" holds on our evaluation suite rather than HellaSwag?
- Which claim in the paper do you trust least, and why?

## Follow-ups

- *The panel says your limits section reads like the authors' own. Name one limit they did not state, and the experiment that would expose it.*
- *We have budget for one replication on our stack before we change our default. Which result do you replicate, at what scale, and what outcome reverses your recommendation?*
- *Present the same paper again in five minutes for the product team. What survives the cut, and what do you drop that a researcher would object to?*
- *Two senior researchers disagree about whether the spectral analysis supports the authors' explanation. How do you decide without deciding by seniority?*

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the paper with the most famous result instead of the one whose result changes a decision for the team in the room.
- Spending half the time on background the panel already knows and running out of time before limits.
- Quoting a headline number without the model, data, metric and checkpoint it came from.
- Presenting the authors' conjecture as the finding, or the authors' limitations as your own critique.
- Bluffing on a skipped section instead of saying what it claims and how much weight it carries.
