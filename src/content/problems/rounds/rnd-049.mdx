## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported format">
Aggregated candidate reports for OpenAI's L5 loop describe a presentation of past work of about thirty minutes, then detailed questions; one account had fifteen minutes of questions inside a one-hour round. The probes reported are the architecture, your decisions, the outcome, a rejected approach, what went wrong and what you would do differently.
</Callout>

Prepare a thirty-minute presentation of one project you led, using your own work. Before you build slides, decide three things and write them down: which project, how the thirty minutes are split, and which number you will put on screen as the result. Then list the five probes you least want to be asked and prepare an answer for each.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask the recruiter whether slides are expected, how long the whole round is, and how much of it is questions.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask your former team which of your numbers you can show and which you must turn into relative changes.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Time a run-through with someone who interrupts twice, and record how far over you run.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Pick the project where you made decisions you can defend against an alternative, not the one with the biggest headline. Spend most of the thirty minutes on two decisions and the measured result, name your part in pieces of work rather than "we", and put what you would undo on a slide of its own. The cost of choosing a smaller project you owned is that the panel may probe scale you never had; prepare the honest answer to that.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The choice of project.</b> Two defensible picks. A large team project shows scope, and its cost is that your part is a slice: the probe "which of these decisions was yours?" can shrink a thirty-minute story to five minutes of your own work. A smaller project you owned end to end lets you defend every decision; its cost is the probe "what would change at ten times the traffic or ten times the team?", which you answer from reasoning, not experience. If your part of the large project contains at least two decisions you made and can defend, pick it. Otherwise pick the one you owned.</p>
<p><b>The split.</b> If the round is an hour with fifteen minutes of questions, 60 minus 30 minus 15 leaves 15 minutes for introductions and interruptions, and interruptions come out of your thirty. One allocation: 3 minutes on the problem and the constraint, 5 on the architecture, 10 on two decisions, 5 on the result and how it was measured, 4 on what went wrong and what you would undo, 3 held back for interruptions (3 + 5 + 10 + 5 + 4 + 3 = 30). Any split that gives the decisions and the result together at least half the time is defensible.</p>
<p><b>The constraint conflict.</b> Confidentiality against specificity. You often cannot show your former employer's absolute numbers, and a result without a number does not survive the first probe. Show relative changes against a stated baseline, with the window and the sample, and say that the absolutes are confidential. The cost is that some panels discount relative numbers; the alternative, inventing round numbers, fails the first follow-up.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li>Your result slide shows a relative gain. What else shipped in that window, and how do you know the gain was yours?</li>
<li>What would you need to see from the system today to conclude your main decision was wrong?</li>
<li>Your team and a partner team disagreed about the design. How was it decided, and would you decide it the same way now?</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

### The decisions section carries the talk

For each of the two decisions, one slide with four lines: the options considered, the one chosen, the cost paid for it (latency, a migration, a team that had to change its workflow), and how you would know now if it was wrong. A decision with no rejected alternative is a description, and the probe "why not X?" turns it into one live.

### Your part, written down

Before the talk, write an attribution list: each major piece of the project, and for each, whether you designed it, built it, decided it, reviewed it or had no part. Use the same verbs on the slides. The probe on ownership is easy to answer from that list and hard to answer from memory under questioning.

### The result

One number on screen, against a baseline, with the window it was measured over and what else changed in that window. If the result was measured with an A/B test, the comparison and the interval; if it was a before-and-after, say so and name the confounders you could not rule out. If adoption by other teams is part of the claim, state how you counted it.

### Where this answer fails

Spending ten minutes on two decisions means the architecture gets five, and a panel that wants to probe a component you summarised gets a thinner answer than they expected. The other failure is the "what would you undo" slide: if the undo is cosmetic ("I would have written more docs"), it reads as having nothing to undo. Pick a decision that cost something real and say what evidence changed your mind.

## Follow-ups

- *Your thirty minutes ran to thirty-eight with our interruptions. Which section do you drop on the spot, and what do you say about it in one sentence?*
- *What would you need to see in production a year later to reverse the decision you defended hardest?*
- *Which part of this project would you not let a coding agent write today, and which part would you hand it first?*
- *You owned the design but another engineer owned the rollout. What went wrong in the rollout, and what was your part in it?*

## Common mistakes

- Presenting the project in the order it happened instead of the order of the decisions that matter.
- "We" throughout, so the ownership probe has to reconstruct your part live.
- A result with no baseline, no window and no sample, or a number that cannot be traced to a measurement.
- A "what I would undo" slide with nothing on it that cost anything.
- Filling the thirty minutes so tightly that one interruption pushes the result off the end of the talk.
