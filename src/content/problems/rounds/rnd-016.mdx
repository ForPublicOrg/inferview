## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Tell me about an LLM feature you shipped. What did you measure, and what went wrong?"
</Callout>

Prepare this story before every loop; it is the most reusable answer you own. Write it in STAR form (situation, task, action, result), in two lengths: a 90-second version and the version you give when the interviewer starts digging. Use your own project. The worked answer below is a scenario, not a real company's result, and exists only to show the shape.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>The question has two parts. Most candidates answer the first well and the second vaguely.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Say "I" for what you did. Amazon's own guide asks for it, and every interviewer is trying to separate your work from the team's.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>A result without a number and a baseline is an opinion.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Shape, with scenario numbers: "Support agents spent most of each ticket searching internal docs (situation). I owned building a drafting assistant that suggested replies with citations (task). I built a 150-ticket eval set from past tickets with the agents' own answers, chose retrieval plus a cited draft over fine-tuning, and shipped to one team behind a flag (action). Median handling time on that team dropped from 9 to 6 minutes over a month, and agents accepted about half the drafts with edits (result). What went wrong: in week two a pricing change made the docs stale and the drafts confidently quoted old prices. I added a freshness check on retrieved documents and an eval case for it."</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Situation, two sentences. Who the users were and what hurt, with one number that shows scale. Long context eats the time you need for the action; give enough for the decision to make sense.</p>
<p>Task, one sentence about your ownership. "I owned" or "I led" if true; "I built the retrieval and eval pieces in a team of four" if that is the truth. Inflating ownership fails at the first follow-up.</p>
<p>Action, most of the time. The two or three decisions that mattered, the options you rejected and why, and how you knew it worked before launch. For LLM work, the eval is usually the most convincing part of the story: how you built it, what it caught, and how you kept it honest.</p>
<p>Result, with a baseline, a number, the measurement window and how it was measured. Then the failure, told plainly: what broke, how you found out, what you changed, and what you would do differently. A story with no failure sounds rehearsed; the failure and what you changed afterwards show how you work when things go wrong.</p>
<p>Prepare for the dig: why that model, what it cost per request, how you handled bad outputs, what the users said, and what you would build next.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>How did you know the improvement came from your feature?</em> Name the comparison: a control team, a before and after window with the same ticket mix, or a holdout.</li>
<li><em>What would you do differently?</em> One concrete change, ideally earlier evaluation or an earlier conversation with users.</li>
<li><em>What did it cost to run?</em> Per request and per month, and whether that was acceptable against the time saved.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

STAR comes from Amazon's interview guidance, and the guide's two practical instructions carry over to every company: speak about your own actions as "I", and give precise numbers. OpenAI's guide names collaboration, effective communication and openness to feedback among what it looks for; the failure half of this story is where you show the last one.

Use the STAR builder in the behavioural lesson to check your draft for the usual gaps: no number in the result, "we" everywhere in the action, a situation longer than the action.

## Follow-ups

- *Tell me about a time you were confident and wrong.* Reuse the failure half of this story or prepare a second one; the structure is the same.
- *Give me the 30-second version.* Situation and task in one sentence, the key decision in one, the result with its number in one.
- *What would a peer on that project say you could improve?* Have a real answer; deflecting this one costs more than any weakness you could name.

## Common mistakes

- "We" throughout, so the interviewer cannot tell what you did.
- A result with no baseline ("users loved it") or no measurement method.
- Spending most of the time on the situation.
- A failure that is really a boast ("I cared too much about quality").
- One version of the story at one length, so you run out of time or out of detail.
