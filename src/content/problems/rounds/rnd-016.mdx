## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Behavioural">
"Tell me about an LLM feature you shipped. What did you measure, what failed, and what did you change?"
</Callout>

Use your own feature. If you need a case to practise the structure on, use this one (scenario): you led a reply-drafting assistant for a support team. It retrieves from the help centre and internal runbooks and drafts a reply with citations, which the agent edits and sends. The pilot ran on one team of 35 agents; a comparable team of 35 on the same queue was the control. Two weeks in, a pricing change went live and the drafts kept quoting the old prices.

Prepare three versions: two minutes, five minutes, and the deep dive when the interviewer pulls on one decision. In each, say how you defined success before launch, the number you got and how you know the feature caused it, the failure, and the change you made because of it.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what counted as an accepted draft: sent unchanged, sent with edits, or sent at all.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the control team's handling time over the same weeks, not only the pilot team's before and after.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask where the drafts got prices from, and who owns that source.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Two-minute version, on the scenario: "Agents spent most of a ticket searching runbooks. I led a drafting assistant that cites its sources, and before launch I fixed two measures: handling time against a control team, and drafts sent with less than half their text changed. Over four weeks the pilot team's median handling time fell from 9.1 to 6.4 minutes while the control team's fell from 9.0 to 8.7, so about 2.4 minutes per ticket came from the feature. 52 percent of drafts were sent, but only 36 percent with light edits. In week two a pricing change made the drafts quote old prices, and 23 went out before an agent flagged it. I moved prices out of retrieval and into a call to the billing service at draft time."</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Defining success before launch.</strong> Two measures, written down before the pilot. Handling time per ticket, compared with a control team on the same queue, because ticket mix changes week to week and a before and after on one team measures the season as much as the feature. And drafts sent with light edits, which needed a threshold I chose: a draft counts if the sent reply keeps more than half of its text. A threshold of a quarter would have counted rewrites as help; requiring the draft to be sent unchanged would have undercounted drafts that saved the search but needed a sentence fixed. Anything between about a third and two thirds is defensible if it is stated.</p>
<p><strong>The number and why it is the feature's.</strong> Pilot median handling time went from 9.1 to 6.4 minutes, a change of 2.7. The control team went from 9.0 to 8.7, a change of 0.3 with no feature. The difference, 2.7 minus 0.3, is 2.4 minutes per ticket attributable to the drafts. At about 30 tickets per agent per day, 35 agents saved roughly 35 × 30 × 2.4 = 2,520 minutes, or 42 agent-hours, a day. Acceptance: 52 percent of drafts were sent, and 31 percent of those had more than half their text changed, so 0.52 × 0.69 ≈ 36 percent were sent with light edits. Reporting 52 would have been the flattering number.</p>
<p><strong>What failed.</strong> Prices lived in help-centre articles, the index was rebuilt weekly, and a pricing change went live midweek. For three days drafts quoted the old prices with a citation, which made them look more trustworthy, not less. 23 such replies were sent before an agent flagged one. The offline eval had no case where two documents disagreed or a document was stale, so nothing caught it.</p>
<p><strong>The decision.</strong> Two options were defensible. Rebuild the index on every content change and rank by effective date: cheap and general, but it still trusts that the help centre is updated before the price goes live, and it was not. Or take prices out of retrieval and call the billing service at draft time: correct by construction for list prices, at the cost of a new dependency, a timeout path and an owner on the billing team. I chose the second for prices and the first for everything else, and added stale-document and conflicting-document cases to the eval.</p>
<p><strong>Where the fix failed.</strong> The billing service returned list prices. Customers on negotiated contracts got list prices in drafts until we passed the account id and used the contract price endpoint. That one came from a reviewer reading a sample of drafts for enterprise accounts, which became a standing weekly check.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *Your control team had a different team lead and different tenure. How much of the 2.4 minutes do you trust?* Compare the two teams' handling times over the four weeks before the pilot; if the gap was stable, the difference-in-differences holds, and if it was moving, say the estimate has that bias and give its direction.
- *The drafts cost money per call. At what handling-time saving would you have switched the feature off?* Compute cost per ticket from tokens per draft and the price per token against agent cost per minute; the switch-off point is where the saving per ticket no longer covers the model cost plus the review time on bad drafts.
- *What would you change if the support director told you agents were sending drafts they had not read?* Measure time between draft shown and reply sent; a cluster of very short times is the signal, and the response is a product change on high-risk topics (prices, refunds), not a reminder email.

## Common mistakes

- A before and after on one team presented as the feature's effect.
- An acceptance rate with no definition of accepted.
- A failure with no number of affected customers and no account of how it was found.
- A fix described without the failure it introduced.
- "We" for every decision, so it is unclear which were yours.
