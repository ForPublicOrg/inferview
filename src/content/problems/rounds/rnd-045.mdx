## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported by a prep vendor, not confirmed by the company">
A prep newsletter says OpenAI asks questions like "How would you double ChatGPT image creation WAU with 3 engineers?" The numbers below are a scenario, not OpenAI's.
</Callout>

What the interviewer tells you when asked (scenario):

- Image-creation WAU is weekly active users who create at least one image. Today it is 12 percent of the assistant's weekly active users.
- Of weekly active users, 30 percent have ever created an image; of those, 40 percent create one in any given week.
- Weekly creators make 6 images a week on average. The capacity team can give image generation 60 percent more GPU time over the next two quarters, and no more.
- Three engineers, no model or infrastructure team. The content policy is owned by another team.
- Target: double image-creation WAU in two quarters.

Say what you build, what you measure, and what you refuse to do, and write the one-page plan.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Request the share of first-time creators whose first attempt was refused or produced something they regenerated more than twice.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how many images a new creator makes per week compared with an established one.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask the policy team how they sample refusals and whether they label false refusals.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Image WAU is 30 percent tried times 40 percent weekly return, 12 percent of actives. Doubling to 24 percent can come from trial (60 percent tried at today's return) or from return (40 percent tried at 60 percent return) or a mix; neither alone is likely in two quarters. The binding constraint is GPU: twice the creators at 6 images each needs twice the GPU time and the allocation is 1.6 times, so new creators must average 4.8 images a week or fewer. That points at return led by first-session success, with a bounded trial bet: fix the first attempts that end in a refusal or a regenerate loop, make past images easy to find and edit, and test in-conversation suggestions on a capped slice. I refuse to loosen content policy as a growth lever, to promise latency work without an infrastructure team, and to count a suggested image nobody looked at as a creator.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Decompose before choosing.</strong> 0.30 × 0.40 = 0.12. To reach 0.24: 0.60 × 0.40 (double trial), 0.30 × 0.80 (double return), or 0.40 × 0.60 (a third more trial, half again as much return). The mix is the plausible target, and it says each bet only has to move its factor by a third to a half.</p>
<p><strong>The capacity arithmetic, which constrains everything.</strong> Today's weekly images per active user are 0.12 × 6 = 0.72. The allocation allows 0.72 × 1.6 = 1.15. At 0.24 creators per active user, that is 1.15 / 0.24 ≈ 4.8 images per creator per week. New creators therefore have to be lighter users than today's, or images per creator have to fall. A plan that doubles WAU by making each creator generate more is infeasible on this allocation, and the launch history shows what happens when image demand outruns capacity: OpenAI's leadership cited delays and temporarily degraded service after the 2025 launch.</p>
<p><strong>The decision: trial-led or return-led.</strong> Trial-led (entry points in conversation where an image would answer the request, templates on the home screen) moves the bigger factor fast and is cheap to build. Its cost: suggested images inflate WAU with low-intent users who never return, and every one of them costs GPU time. Return-led (first-session success, history and editing of past images) moves a smaller factor more slowly and may not reach the target alone, but each added creator is a user who wanted images. I lead with return, because the GPU constraint makes low-intent trial the most expensive WAU there is, and keep one engineer on a capped trial experiment.</p>
<p><strong>The three bets, one engineer each.</strong></p>
<p>First-session success. Measure the share of first-time creators whose first attempt was refused or regenerated more than twice, and their return rate against those whose first attempt worked. If refusals dominate, sample them and label false refusals with the policy team; the fix is theirs, and the evidence is ours. If regenerate loops dominate, the fixes in product scope are prompt rewriting with the user's confirmation and editing the image instead of regenerating it, which also saves GPU time.</p>
<p>Return. Images are buried in old conversations; a findable history with one-step edit and variation gives a reason to come back that costs less GPU than starting over.</p>
<p>Capped trial. In-conversation suggestions on 5 percent of traffic, counted as a creation only when the user accepts the suggestion and then views, downloads or edits the image. The cap protects capacity while the experiment measures whether suggested creators return.</p>
<p><strong>A quantity I choose: the trial cap and its kill rule.</strong> 5 percent of traffic for four weeks; kill the bet if suggested creators' four-week return is below half of organic first-time creators'. Anything from 2 to 10 percent is defensible; the lower end keeps GPU risk small and takes longer to read.</p>
<p><strong>Metric and guardrails.</strong> The goal metric is image-creation WAU, counted only for created images that were requested or accepted and then viewed. Guardrails: GPU-seconds per image WAU against the 4.8-image budget, refusal rate with its labelled false-refusal share, policy-violation reports per 1,000 images, p95 time to image, and the assistant's overall retention, so image growth does not come from cannibalising other use.</p>
<p><strong>What I refuse.</strong> Loosening content policy to grow: policy for image generation was set as a deliberate trade-off by the team that owns it, as Jang's post on the launch describes, and a growth team changing it would trade a metric for real-world harm. Latency or model work without the teams that own them. Counting unviewed suggested images. Expanding to under-18 users as a growth source, since the policy explicitly applies tighter guardrails there.</p>
<p><strong>The constraint conflict.</strong> Growth against capacity and against safety: the cheapest WAU is the most expensive in GPU time, and the largest leak in the funnel may be refusals that only another team can change.</p>
<p><strong>Failure mode of this plan.</strong> Return-led growth may land at 1.5 times, not 2, in two quarters; the plan has to say so at the start rather than at the end. And first-session success measured through regenerations can mislead: a user who regenerates five times may be exploring, not failing, so the regeneration signal is checked against return before it drives a fix.</p>
<p><strong>What you give up.</strong> The fastest route to the headline number, model quality and latency improvements, and any growth from policy changes.</p>
<p><strong>The one-page plan.</strong> Goal: image WAU from 12 to 24 percent of actives, expected landing 18 to 24 percent. Bets: first-session success, return, capped trial, each with an owner, a primary metric, a kill rule and a guardrail. Budget: GPU-seconds per image WAU at or below the 4.8-image equivalent. Refusals: labelled sample shared weekly with the policy team. Rollback triggers: policy-violation reports per 1,000 images above the current rate for two weeks, or p95 time to image above an agreed limit, pauses the bet that moved them.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why not just raise the free-tier image limit?</em> It raises images per creator, which is the one direction the GPU budget forbids, and it moves WAU only for users who already hit the limit.</li>
<li><em>How do you attribute WAU to three bets running at once?</em> Each bet runs as an experiment against a holdout; the sum of the three lifts will not equal the total, and the plan reports both.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the capacity team revises the allocation to 30 percent more GPU, not 60. Which bet do you cut, and what do you accept in writing?* Images per creator would have to average 0.72 × 1.3 / 0.24 ≈ 3.9; the trial bet goes first, and the plan states a revised landing below doubling, signed by the product owner.
- *Reversal: what result after four weeks would make you switch to trial-led?* Suggested creators returning at a rate close to organic first-time creators', at an images-per-week rate inside the budget; then the cheapest factor to move is also a good one.
- *The policy team and the growth lead disagree about whether a class of refusals is false. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* A labelled sample of those refusals under the written policy, scored by both sides blind, with disagreements read together; the policy owner decides, on evidence.
- *Halfway through, image WAU is up 40 percent and assistant retention is down. What do you do?* Check whether the gain came from users who previously used the assistant for something else; if it did, pause the bet that drove it, because the goal was more value, not moved activity.

## Common mistakes

- Starting from features before decomposing the metric into trial and return.
- Ignoring GPU capacity, which turns most growth plans into outages.
- Counting suggested images as creators whether or not anyone looked at them.
- Treating content policy as a growth lever.
- Promising the full doubling without saying what the plan most likely delivers.
