## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported format">
A candidate report from Sierra's agent engineering loop describes a five to ten minute explanation of a technical topic of the candidate's choice, given to two agent engineers; the candidate explained how RAG works. The report lists no questions on the talk. The probes below are constructed.
</Callout>

Choose a topic and a length between five and ten minutes. Write the talk, then list every simplification you made, one line each. The panel will spend the questions on that list: for each simplification, what decision did it hide, what does each option cost, and when does your simplified version stop being true?

If you choose retrieval-augmented generation, as the reported candidate did, the listeners build customer-facing agents and run retrieval in production every day.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask who is in the room and what they build, so you know which simplifications they will notice.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask whether the talk is timed strictly and whether slides or a whiteboard are expected.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Run the talk once for an engineer who works on the topic and write down every question they ask.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Pick a mechanism, not a field: "why hybrid retrieval and a reranker fail less than dense retrieval alone" survives a staff panel better than "how RAG works", because the questions land inside what you explained rather than around it. Explain it with one sourced number, then say out loud the three or four things you simplified and what each hides. The price is less breadth, and a panel that wanted the overview may ask for it in the questions.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The choice.</b> Two defensible picks. A broad topic (how RAG works) is easy to teach and fits a teaching exercise; its cost is that every sentence is a simplification, and the panel can pick any of them. A narrow mechanism (contextual chunking, hybrid search, reranking) lets you go to the conditions under which it holds; its cost is that the talk can read as a niche interest, and you have to spend the first minute on why it matters to the listeners' systems.</p>
<p><b>The length.</b> The reader chooses between five and ten minutes. Seven is a reasonable choice for a narrow mechanism: 1 minute on the problem, 3 on the mechanism, 2 on the evidence, 1 on the simplifications (1 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 7). Using all ten is defensible only if the extra minutes go to evidence or limits, not to background.</p>
<p><b>The constraint conflict.</b> Simplicity against correctness. A teaching talk has to simplify, and every simplification is a trade-off the panel can open. Naming them is how a short talk stays correct: "I treated chunks as fixed-size; that hides the chunking decision, which I can open in the questions."</p>
</Fragment>

<ul>
<li>You said the reranker cut retrieval failures. Our latency budget for the whole turn is fixed. Where does the reranker's time come from, and what do you cut?</li>
<li>What would you need to see in our own traces to drop the reranker?</li>
<li>Your number is a vendor's measurement on its own datasets. How would you check it holds on our customers' documents before changing anything?</li>

### A worked version on hybrid retrieval

**The evidence, with who measured it.** Anthropic's contextual retrieval post (September 2024, its own measurements across codebases, fiction and papers) reports the share of queries where the relevant chunk is missing from the top 20: 5.7 percent with plain embeddings, 3.7 with contextualised embeddings, 2.9 adding BM25 over the contextualised chunks, 1.9 adding a reranker. The relative reductions, computed: (5.7 - 3.7) / 5.7 is 35 percent, (5.7 - 2.9) / 5.7 is 49 percent, (5.7 - 1.9) / 5.7 is 67 percent. The post's one-time cost for contextualising chunks is $1.02 per million document tokens, under its stated chunk and document sizes with prompt caching.

**The simplifications, said out loud.**

- Recall at 20 is a retrieval metric, not answer quality; a talk that stops there hides whether the model used the chunk.
- The numbers are averaged across four kinds of corpus, so a corpus like the listeners' support documents can sit far from the average.
- The reranker adds a model call per query; the talk did not price its latency.
- The contextualisation cost is paid again whenever a document changes; for a support knowledge base that is edited daily, that cost is recurring, not one-time.
- Access control on retrieved chunks was left out entirely.

### Where the recommended approach fails

A narrow talk with a named list of simplifications hands the panel a map of where to probe. If the list includes something you cannot defend for three minutes (the reranker's latency, say), leave it off the slide and keep a one-sentence answer ready instead; a listed simplification you cannot discuss is worse than one you did not list. The other failure is the vendor number: a panel that runs retrieval in production may know its corpus behaves differently, and "the post reports" has to be followed by how you would measure it on theirs.

## Follow-ups

- *Your talk claims a 67 percent reduction. On our customers' documents we measured nothing like it. Which of your simplifications explains the gap first, and how do you check?*
- *Contextualising every chunk costs a model call per chunk at ingestion. Our largest customer re-uploads their whole knowledge base weekly. Do you still recommend it, and what do you accept in writing?*
- *Two engineers on the panel disagree: one says long context makes retrieval unnecessary for customers under a certain size, the other says it does not. What measurement decides it, and what would reverse your answer?*
- *Give the same talk in three minutes. Which simplification do you stop naming, and what does that cost the listener?*

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a whole field and spending the time on definitions the panel uses every day.
- Quoting a vendor's number without saying it is the vendor's, on which data, by which metric.
- Hiding simplifications and waiting for the panel to find them.
- Listing a simplification you cannot discuss when the panel opens it.
- Running over the chosen length, so the evidence section is the one that gets cut.
