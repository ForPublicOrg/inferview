## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Behavioural">
"Tell me about a time you had to choose between accuracy and latency. How did you decide?"
</Callout>

Use your own decision; cost against quality or scope against a date has the same structure if latency was never the constraint. To practise, use this case (scenario). Your product answers questions about a customer's own analytics data inside a dashboard. On a 400-question eval, a reasoning model answers 88 percent correctly with a p95 latency of 14 seconds; a fast model answers 74 percent with a p95 of 2.2 seconds. Session logs show that 6 percent of users abandon a question that takes 2 seconds and 19 percent one that takes 6 seconds. A colleague proposes a router that sends the hardest 25 percent of questions to the reasoning model and the rest to the fast one. The product lead wants the feature in the next release, five weeks away.

Say what latency budget you set and why, what the router would do to it, the options you weighed, what you chose, and what it cost.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask for the reasoning model's latency at the percentiles below p95, not only p95.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the eval broken down by the questions the router would call hard and the ones it would call easy.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask the product lead whether every question has to be answered in the same interaction.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>On the scenario: "I set a 3-second p95 budget from the abandonment data and showed that the router breaks it: with a quarter of traffic on the slow model, the product's p95 becomes the slow model's 80th percentile, about 11 seconds. So I kept the fast model in the synchronous path, improved it with the customer's schema in the prompt (74 to 81 percent on the eval), and moved the reasoning model behind an explicit 'analyse further' action that streams its answer. Abandonment stayed near the 2-second level, and about one in ten questions used the slow path. What I gave up: users who never click it get the 81 percent answer."</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The budget, which I chose.</strong> Abandonment rose from 6 percent at 2 seconds to 19 percent at 6, so I set 3 seconds at p95: the most the curve suggested before abandonment rose sharply, with headroom for model latency drift. Anything from 2 to 4 seconds is defensible from that curve. A budget set from what the reasoning model can do, or from a competitor's demo, is not.</p>
<p><strong>What the router does to p95.</strong> The router's latency is a mixture, and a p95 of a mixture is not a weighted average. With 75 percent of traffic on the fast model, nearly all of it under 2.5 seconds, the overall p95 is the latency x where 0.75 × 1 + 0.25 × F_slow(x) = 0.95, so F_slow(x) = 0.8: the product's p95 is the reasoning model's p80. On our traces that was about 11 seconds. Any routed share above 5 percent puts the slow model's tail into the product's p95, which the proposal's averaged latency of 0.75 × 2.2 + 0.25 × 14 ≈ 5.2 seconds hid.</p>
<p><strong>The options and their costs.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><em>Router as proposed.</em> Best accuracy on average. Cost: the budget, and with it the abandonment curve.</li>
<li><em>Fast model only, improved.</em> Put the customer's table schema and metric definitions in the prompt and retry once on a SQL error. It took the fast model from 74 to 81 percent on the eval within budget. Cost: 7 points below the reasoning model on the questions that matter most to analysts.</li>
<li><em>Fast synchronous answer, slow answer on request.</em> The fast model answers in the budget; an "analyse further" action runs the reasoning model and streams. Cost: a second UI state, two models to evaluate per release, and a user who now sees two answers that can disagree.</li>
</ul>
<p>The colleague who proposed the router had a fair point: users asking hard questions may accept a wait. I agreed that was testable and proposed testing it through the explicit action rather than an invisible router, so the user chooses the wait. That is the third option, built on the second.</p>
<p><strong>Where this answer fails.</strong> Users rarely click a button that implies the first answer was wrong, so the 81 percent answer is what most of them get on hard questions too. Two weeks after launch I checked the eval by question difficulty: on the hardest quarter the fast path was at 63 percent. I added a confidence signal, a failed or empty query on the first try, that offers the deeper analysis instead of waiting for a click. Its own failure is the confident wrong answer, which that signal cannot see.</p>
<p><strong>Written down.</strong> The budget and the data behind it, the mixture calculation, the decision and the date to revisit it: when a model in the fast tier reaches the reasoning model's accuracy on the hardest quarter within the budget.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *What if the answers were feeding a financial report, not a dashboard?* Latency loses: the slow path or a human check becomes the default for anything exported, and the budget applies only to exploration.
- *Your improvement to the fast model was prompt work. How do you know it still holds after the next model upgrade?* The 400-question eval, broken down by difficulty, runs on every model or prompt change, and the hardest quarter has its own threshold.
- *Six months later the vendor ships a reasoning model with a 3-second p95. What do you remove?* The explicit action, if the new model's accuracy on the hardest quarter matches; keep the per-difficulty eval, since it is the measurement that made the original call.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the trade as binary when the synchronous path and the slow path can be split.
- Averaging latencies across a mixture and reporting the result as the user's experience.
- A latency budget with no data from users behind it.
- No number on either side of the trade.
- Leaving out who disagreed and how it ended.
