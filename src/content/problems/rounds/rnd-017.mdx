## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Describe a time you had to choose between model accuracy and latency. How did you decide?"
</Callout>

The question is about how you decide, not which side you picked. Prepare a story from your own work. If you have not faced this exact trade-off, a cost-versus-quality or a scope-versus-deadline decision with the same structure works, and saying so up front is better than stretching a story to fit.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Name who was affected by each option and how you found out what they needed.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>The strongest answers find a third option rather than accepting the trade as stated.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Shape, as a scenario: "Our autocomplete used a large model that was more accurate on our eval but too slow for typing. I measured where users abandoned suggestions, set a latency budget from that, and ran the eval for three options inside it. A smaller model with retrieval of the user's recent context scored close to the large one on the cases that mattered and met the budget. We shipped that, and kept the large model for an explicit 'rewrite' action where users would wait."</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Set up the tension in one or two sentences: what accuracy meant, what latency meant, and who felt each.</p>
<p>Show how you turned it into numbers. A latency budget from user behaviour or a product requirement, and an accuracy measure on an eval set you trusted. Without both, the decision is a matter of taste.</p>
<p>List the options you actually considered. Common ones in LLM products: a smaller or distilled model, fewer output tokens, streaming so the first token arrives sooner, caching a shared prompt prefix, a fast path for common cases with a slow path for hard ones, or moving the slow model to an asynchronous step.</p>
<p>Say how you decided and who you brought in. A product manager who owned the user experience, an engineer who owned the cost, the users themselves through a small test.</p>
<p>Close with the result and what you watched afterwards, including anything you would undo.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>What if the stakeholder disagreed with you?</em> Describe how you used the measurements to settle it, or how you agreed to run a test instead of arguing.</li>
<li><em>What did you give up?</em> Name the cases the faster option handles worse and how you protect users from them.</li>
<li><em>How did you measure latency?</em> Time to first token and total time, at the median and at a high percentile, from the user's side rather than the server's.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

The scenario in the 30-second answer is illustrative; use your own story with your own numbers. Interviewers at companies that state values will listen for them. Anthropic, for instance, lists doing the simple thing that works among its values, and a story where you found a simpler option that met the need maps onto that directly.

## Follow-ups

- *Tell me about a time a simpler approach beat a sophisticated one.* The same structure; lead with the measurement that showed it.
- *What would you do if accuracy were a safety issue, for example medical or financial advice?* Latency loses; the design changes to slower paths, human review or refusing out-of-scope requests.

## Common mistakes

- Describing a choice someone else made.
- No numbers for either side of the trade-off.
- Treating it as binary when caching, streaming or routing could change both sides.
- Leaving out who disagreed and how the disagreement ended.
