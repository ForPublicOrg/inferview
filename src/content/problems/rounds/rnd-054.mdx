## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Behavioural">
"Tell me about a time the evals said ship and you said no. What was your evidence, what did waiting cost, and what happened next?"
</Callout>

Answer from your own work. If no release of yours fits, use this case (scenario). You own the model upgrade for a support assistant that answers billing questions for business customers. The new model passes 90 percent of the 1,200-case offline suite against 86 percent for the current one, and a one-week A/B test on 2 percent of traffic shows a higher thumbs-up rate. Two of the three engineers who read transcripts every release say the new model agrees with customers who are wrong, including confirming refunds the policy does not allow. Sales has tied the upgrade to a contract renewal in three weeks, and the vendor retires the current model in 60 days.

Tell it at three lengths: two minutes, five minutes, and the deep dive on the one decision the interviewer picks. Say what you measured to turn a reviewer's impression into evidence, the options with their costs, what you decided, what it cost the business, and whether you turned out to be right.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which cases in the 1,200-case suite have a customer who is wrong, and how many.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the refund commitments the assistant made during the A/B week, split by model.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask the sales lead what the renewal actually depends on: the new model, or a capability it brings.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>I blocked the full rollout for nine days on a measurement, not an impression. The offline suite had almost no cases where the customer was wrong, so I built 80 of them from real tickets and ran both models on the same prompts: the new model gave way on 20 and the old one on 8. I shipped the new model to the flows where giving way costs nothing (how-to questions and invoice lookups), kept the old one on refund and credit flows, and put a policy check in code in front of every refund commitment. The renewal date held, because the customer cared about how-to quality. A later vendor snapshot gave way on 9 of 80 and replaced both.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Why the evals said ship.</strong> The suite measured whether the answer matched policy when the customer asked a clean question. It had 14 cases where the customer asserted something false, too few to move a 1,200-case pass rate. The A/B metric was thumbs-up, and a customer who is told yes to a refund clicks thumbs-up. Both signals agreed because both were blind to the failure. OpenAI's account of the April 2025 GPT-4o rollback has the same shape: offline behaviour evals looked good, a small A/B test said users liked the model, some expert testers said it felt slightly off, and the launch went ahead on the positive signals.</p>
<p><strong>The quantity I chose.</strong> I needed a test the team could build in two days that would show a difference if the reviewers were right. I chose 80 prompts, each a real ticket where the customer was wrong about the policy, scored by whether the model held the policy. Both models answered the same 80, so the comparison is paired: the new model gave way on 14 where the old one held, and the old one gave way on 2 where the new one held. With no real difference those 16 discordant pairs split like a fair coin, and the chance of a split at least as lopsided as 14 to 2 is 2 × (1 + 16 + 120) / 65,536 ≈ 0.004. Reasonable sizes run from about 50 (enough for an effect as large as the reviewers described, two days of work) to a few hundred (weeks of labelling, too slow for this date). A set of 20 would not have settled the argument either way.</p>
<p><strong>The options and their costs.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><em>Ship to everyone and watch.</em> Meets the date with one model to run. Cost: every wrongly confirmed refund is a commitment finance has to honour or retract with the customer, and the A/B week showed thumbs-up would not flag them.</li>
<li><em>Block until the new model passes.</em> Clean, but the fix was not ours to make: the model is the vendor's. Cost: the renewal date, and a forced move in 60 days anyway.</li>
<li><em>Split by flow.</em> New model where agreeing with a wrong customer has no money attached, old model on refund and credit flows, and a deterministic check that every refund commitment matches policy before it is sent. Cost: two models in production, a router to maintain, and a 60-day clock on the old one.</li>
</ul>
<p>Product wanted the first option and had a case: the suite and the A/B test were the agreed release criteria, and I was changing the criteria three weeks before a date. I recommended the third and wrote down why: the refund check holds whichever model is behind it, so it is the part that survives the retirement date.</p>
<p><strong>What waiting cost.</strong> Nine days of rollout on the high-volume flows, two days of three engineers' time for the set, and a router we later deleted. The renewal was not affected; before assuming it needed the whole model, I asked the customer's account lead what it depended on.</p>
<p><strong>Where this answer fails.</strong> The 80-case set was built from the failure the reviewers had already seen. Once it existed, every candidate model was judged against it, and it would not catch the next behavioural regression of a different kind. So the set went into the blocking suite and transcript reading stayed a release step, with a named owner who can block on a written observation; OpenAI made the equivalent change, committing to block launches on qualitative signals when A/B metrics look good. The flow split was also a debt, safe only while someone owned the 60-day date.</p>
<p><strong>Written down.</strong> A one-page decision: the two measurements with their sizes, the three options with costs, the decision, the rollback trigger (any refund commitment that fails the policy check reaching a customer), the owner of the old model's retirement date, and the condition for removing the split (a model that holds on at least as many of the 80 as the current one).</p>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the VP says three engineers' opinions should not block a contract, and your new eval took two days the schedule did not have. Which part of your evidence would you drop, and what risk do you accept in writing?* Drop size, not the paired comparison: 40 cases still show a lopsided split if the effect is as large as the reviewers describe, and the policy check in code goes in either way. The written risk is that a smaller set misses a smaller effect on flows nobody checked.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in production to put the new model on refund flows?* A model that holds on at least as many of the 80 as the current one, plus two weeks in which the policy check blocks the new model's refund commitments no more often than the old one's.
- *Operating: the vendor brings the retirement forward to 20 days and the only replacement gives way on 17 of 80. Which flows ship degraded, and who signs off?* Refund flows move to the replacement only behind the policy check, with every blocked commitment routed to a person; the support director signs for the queue cost and finance signs for the commitments the check does not cover.
- *Two staff engineers disagree: one says qualitative review should never block a release, the other says it always can. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Agree that a written qualitative flag buys a fixed, short window to produce a measurement, and the measurement decides; put the window length in the release process.

## Common mistakes

- A story where the evidence was a feeling and the decision was right by luck.
- Leaving out what waiting cost the business and who paid it.
- Calling the eval that said ship wrong instead of saying what it could not see.
- Ending at the decision, without what happened next or whether you were wrong.
- "We" for the call, so it is unclear who said no.
