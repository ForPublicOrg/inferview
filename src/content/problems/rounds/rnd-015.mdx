## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported by a prep vendor, not confirmed by the company">
A prep vendor describes an applied-AI round at Anthropic built around a 45-minute discovery call in which the interviewer plays an enterprise buyer, and lists asking about past failed AI deployments among the habits of candidates who advance. The case below is original material in that format.
</Callout>

The interviewer plays the CTO of a 900-person insurer (scenario). What the CTO reveals, and only when asked:

- Two AI pilots ran in the last 18 months. A contract-review assistant was dropped by the legal team after six weeks; an internal policy chatbot was launched and barely used.
- Legal reviews about 400 broker contracts a month, most of them one of three templates with negotiated changes.
- Any vendor that touches customer data needs the security team's approval, which took eleven weeks last time.
- The CTO's board wants "an AI result" by the end of the quarter, nine weeks away.

Thirty minutes in, the CTO says: "You're asking a lot of questions. If you're so sure, run the contract pilot again. Two weeks. If it fails, we're done with this."

Part 1: plan the discovery and your response to that ultimatum. Part 2: the interviewer steps out of role and asks for a time you worked with a stakeholder who had been burned by a previous project. Tell it.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask who stopped using the contract assistant and what they did instead the week after.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request a measurement of how often two of their own reviewers agree on the same contract.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask whether the security approval is per vendor or per data class, and which data the pilot needs.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Discovery aims at one thing first: why each pilot failed, in the words of the people who stopped using it, because the CTO's scepticism is evidence about this company and not a debating position. Then the workflow, the volume, the cost of an error, how success would be judged and the constraints (security approval, residency, audit). Faced with the ultimatum I accept a narrowed version of the same workflow rather than steering to an easier one: one contract template, extraction and flagging of changed clauses rather than full review, success judged against how often their own reviewers agree with each other, and data that fits inside an approval path the security team can finish in time. The cost of that choice is that the pilot is smaller than the problem, and the CTO has to agree to the narrowing in the room.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>What discovery has to establish, in order of what it unlocks.</strong> Why the contract assistant was dropped: wrong answers, answers that took longer to check than to produce, a tool outside the reviewers' editor, or no one asked them. Why the chatbot went unused: no need, no trust or no discovery. The workflow being replaced: 400 contracts a month, three templates, who reviews, how long one takes, what an error costs and who catches it today. How success would be judged, and by whom. What the data may touch: customer data, residency, retention, the approval path. Who else has to say yes, and who said no last time. I reflect each answer back in the CTO's words before moving on, because a correction from the buyer is the cheapest information in the call.</p>
<p><strong>The ultimatum is the decision.</strong> Two answers are defensible.</p>
<p>Run the contract pilot again, narrowed. The CTO has named the workflow that matters to them and staked credibility on it; agreeing keeps the buyer's frame and the board's attention. The cost: the pilot inherits whatever killed the first one, and two weeks may not be enough to separate a fixed cause from a lucky run.</p>
<p>Propose a different first workflow with clearer success criteria, for example triage of incoming broker emails. The cost: it reads as avoidance to a buyer who already doubts the product, and a success there does not answer the question the CTO actually asked.</p>
<p>I take the first, conditional on what discovery found. If the legal team dropped the assistant because checking its output took longer than doing the work, the narrowing is extraction: list the clauses that differ from the template, with the text and location, and leave judgement to the reviewer. That changes the unit of checking from "is this review right" to "is this clause flagged", which a reviewer can verify in seconds. If they dropped it because it lived in a separate tool, the narrowing is delivery inside their document system. If the cause was wrong answers on one template, the pilot runs on the other two.</p>
<p><strong>The quantity I choose: the pass bar.</strong> Before any model runs, two of their reviewers mark changed clauses on the same 30 contracts. If they agree on, say, 88 percent of clauses (scenario), a pilot target above 88 percent cannot be measured with their own labels, and a target of 80 percent with no missed clause from a short list of high-risk clause types is a claim both sides can check. The pilot runs on about 100 contracts of one template, which is one template's share of a month. Any bar between the reviewers' own agreement minus ten points and that agreement is defensible; a bar above their agreement is not.</p>
<p><strong>The constraint conflict.</strong> Two weeks against an eleven-week security approval. Two ways through: run the pilot on contracts with customer data redacted, which may fit an existing approval for de-identified documents, at the cost of testing on documents that differ from production; or start the full approval now and run the two weeks on redacted data while it proceeds. I propose both together, and say the two-week result is evidence about extraction quality, not about production.</p>
<p><strong>Where this fails.</strong> The diagnosis of the first failure comes from the CTO, secondhand. If the real cause was that legal never wanted the tool, a better pilot changes nothing, and the CTO's "we're done" arrives anyway. The guard is to ask for twenty minutes with one reviewer who used the first assistant before the pilot starts, and to make the reviewer's yes a condition. The second failure mode is the call itself: a CTO who has said "you're asking a lot of questions" may end the meeting before the narrowing is agreed, so the proposal has to arrive within a few minutes of the ultimatum, not after more discovery.</p>
<p><strong>Part 2, the story.</strong> A story that answers this question carries four things that can be checked: what the stakeholder had lost before and how you learned it, an alternative you considered and why you did not take it, the measured result with its unit, and what the stakeholder did differently afterwards that shows the trust changed. A story where the stakeholder was won over by a demo carries none of them.</p>
</Fragment>

<li><em>The reviewer who used the first assistant refuses to meet.</em> That is the strongest signal in the call; say the pilot depends on it and offer to observe a review instead of interviewing.</li>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: your pass bar depends on 30 contracts double-reviewed, and legal says that costs them a week they do not have. What do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?* Cut to 15 contracts and accept a wider band on the agreement figure, written as a range in the pilot memo, rather than drop the measurement and set the bar by guess.
- *Reversal: what would you need to hear in the first ten minutes to recommend that the company not run an AI pilot this quarter at all?* That the contracts arrive as scans no one has extracted, or that legal's backlog is not the bottleneck the board cares about; then the useful result for the quarter is a process fix, and saying so is the pitch.
- *The board wants a result in nine weeks and the pilot shows 74 percent agreement against a bar of 80. Who decides whether to continue, and what do you bring?* The CTO decides; you bring the clause types where it failed, the reviewer time saved on the rest, and the change you would test next with its cost.
- *In your Part 2 story, what would the stakeholder say you got wrong?* Name the mistake, when you learned of it and what you changed; a story with no mistake in it cannot answer this question.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the failed pilots as objections to overcome rather than as the requirements.
- Answering the ultimatum with a feature list or a model comparison.
- Steering to an easier workflow without saying why, which reads as avoidance.
- Setting a success bar without measuring how often the customer's own reviewers agree.
- Promising a two-week pilot without asking how long the security approval takes.
- Telling a Part 2 story with no measured result and no alternative considered.
