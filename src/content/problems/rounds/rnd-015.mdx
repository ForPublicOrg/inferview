## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported by a prep guide, not confirmed by the company">
A prep vendor describes an applied-AI role-play at Anthropic where the interviewer plays an enterprise buyer, for example a VP of Engineering at a large fintech evaluating Claude for an internal copilot. In a second example from the same guide, the interviewer plays a CTO who opens by doubting that Claude does anything ChatGPT cannot and asks the candidate to convince them. The candidate is expected to run discovery rather than pitch. Practise the format; do not rely on the details.
</Callout>

The interviewer, playing the CTO of a fintech, opens: "I don't see why we'd pay for this. My team already uses ChatGPT. Convince me."

Plan your first ten minutes. What do you say first, what do you ask, and how do you end the conversation?

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>"Convince me" is an invitation to pitch. A question back is usually stronger than a claim.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>A fintech buyer has constraints you should surface before proposing anything: compliance, data residency, audit.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>End with a next step the buyer can say yes to, sized small.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>I would not argue. I would acknowledge the point, then ask what they use ChatGPT for today, what has worked, and what has not. Then I would dig into one workflow that matters to them: volume, who does it now, what an error costs, and what their compliance and data constraints are. I reflect back what I heard in their words, propose one small, measurable pilot on that workflow with a success criterion they set, and ask for the data and the person I need to start.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Open by agreeing with what is true. "That's fair. If your team is getting value from ChatGPT, the question is where it stops being enough. Can I ask how they use it today?" This moves the conversation from my product to their work, which is where any decision will be made.</p>
<p>Discovery questions I would want answered, roughly in this order. What is the team using it for, and where did it fall short? Have they tried to put an AI tool into production before, and what happened? Which workflow, if it improved, would someone above them notice: what volume, who does it, how long it takes, what a mistake costs? How would they judge success: accuracy on a set of real cases, time saved, fewer escalations? What can the data touch: regulated customer data, residency requirements, audit trails, model-provider approvals? Who else has to say yes?</p>
<p>Reflect back. "So the expensive part is analysts reconciling disputed transactions, a mistake there is a regulatory problem, and anything that touches customer data needs to stay in-region and be logged. Is that right?" If they correct me, that is good; the correction is information I would not have got from pitching.</p>
<p>Propose small. A two-week pilot on a sample of their real cases, scored against their own reviewers' answers, inside their data constraints. Name what I would build (for example a retrieval tool over their policy documents and an evaluation harness) and what I would not. Close with a concrete ask: a sample of cases, a reviewer for an hour, and a date to look at results together.</p>
<p>What I avoid: benchmark scores they did not ask about, claims I cannot back on the spot, and criticising the tool their team already likes.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>They say "just tell me why you're better".</em> Give one concrete, checkable difference relevant to what they told you, then offer to prove it on their cases rather than argue it.</li>
<li><em>They raise a security objection you cannot answer.</em> Say you do not know, write it down, and commit to a date for an answer from the right person.</li>
<li><em>The workflow they care about is a bad fit for an LLM.</em> Say so and suggest where it does fit. Credibility with a sceptic is worth more than one pilot.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

Why discovery wins here. The forward-deployed job posts describe the job as building inside customer systems and scoping work from prototype to production, so the round is checking whether you would gather requirements before building. A pitch shows product knowledge; a good discovery conversation shows you would scope the right thing. The prep guide that describes this round presents pitching as the common failure; treat that as practice advice, since the company has not published a rubric.

## Follow-ups

- *Write the one-paragraph summary you would send after the call.* Their problem in their words, the constraints, the pilot, the success criterion, the next date. If you cannot write it, the discovery was not finished.
- *How do you handle a buyer who will not give you time with the people doing the work?* Ask for recordings, tickets or a shadowing session; a pilot designed without them will measure the wrong thing.

## Common mistakes

- Answering "convince me" with a feature list.
- Asking a long list of generic questions without following up on the answers.
- Proposing an agent before learning what the workflow is.
- Ending without a next step, or with one so large the buyer cannot agree to it in the room.
- Disparaging the tool the buyer's team already uses.
