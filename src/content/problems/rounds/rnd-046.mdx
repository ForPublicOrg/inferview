## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Decomposition round">
"A customer says their dashboard is wrong. They can't tell you which number. What do you do?"
</Callout>

What the interviewer tells you when asked (scenario):

- Your team built the dashboard for the customer's support organisation. An LLM classifies each ticket into one of 14 contact reasons and marks whether the AI assistant resolved it; the dashboard shows weekly volume by reason, the AI resolution rate and the top five reasons.
- The VP of support says it is wrong. Tomorrow morning she presents it to her executive team as the case for expanding the assistant.
- In the last month your team changed the classification prompt once and the customer renamed two contact reasons.
- It is 3 p.m. You have an hour before she leaves for the day.

Say what you do in the first hour, in order, what you decide about tomorrow's presentation, and what you tell her before she leaves.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask her what she believes the true numbers are, even roughly, and where that belief comes from.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the ticket count for last week from the help desk itself, with the time zone it uses.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask how her team decides a ticket was resolved, and compare it with the dashboard's definition.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Turn "wrong" into a testable claim, then split the numbers by where they come from. Ten minutes with her: what decision the dashboard is for, what she expected to see and why, and since when it has looked wrong. Then the numbers that come straight from the help desk (ticket counts) are reconciled against the source for the same window and time zone, and the numbers that come from the model (reasons, resolution) are checked against the change log and a hand-labelled sample of 40 tickets. Before she leaves she gets the source-verified figures, a clear mark on any model-derived figure I could not verify, and a time tomorrow morning for the rest. Pulling the whole dashboard and keeping it untouched are both defensible; I split it, and say which numbers I stand behind.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Minutes 0 to 10: make "wrong" testable.</strong> She cannot name the number, but she can name a belief: "refund requests are our top reason", "the assistant does not resolve half of anything". Each belief points at a figure. I also ask what she compared the dashboard with (another report, her team leads, her own reading of tickets) and when it started looking wrong, because a date lines up with the change log.</p>
<p><strong>Minutes 10 to 25: reconcile what does not depend on the model.</strong> Weekly ticket count on the dashboard against the help desk's own count for the same week and the same time zone. If totals differ, the problem is the pipeline: lag, deduplication, a filter, or a week boundary in UTC against her local week. If totals match, the problem is in the distribution or the definitions, which narrows the next half hour.</p>
<p><strong>Minutes 25 to 45: check what does.</strong> Two places, in parallel. The change log: the prompt change and the renamed reasons both landed in the last month; a renamed reason the prompt does not know about sends its tickets to the nearest old label or to "other", and a prompt change can move tickets between reasons with no change in the underlying mix. And a sample: 40 tickets from the reason she doubts, labelled by me pass or fail against the reason the model gave. 40 is my choice for the hour. At 80 percent observed agreement the 95 percent interval is 1.96 × √(0.8 × 0.2 / 40) ≈ ±12 points, enough to catch a gross failure (half the labels wrong) and not a 5-point drift. 30 to 60 is defensible in an hour; 10 is not, since the interval widens to about ±25 points.</p>
<p><strong>The definition check.</strong> The AI resolution rate counts a ticket as resolved if the customer did not reply within 24 hours of the assistant's answer. If her team counts a ticket reopened within a week as unresolved, the dashboard is right by its definition and wrong by hers, and her executive team will use hers.</p>
<p><strong>Minutes 45 to 60: the decision about tomorrow.</strong> Two defensible answers.</p>
<p>Withdraw the dashboard from tomorrow's presentation. Nobody makes an expansion decision on a number that may be wrong, and the relationship survives an honest "not yet". The cost: she presents without data the day she asked for it, and if the numbers were right, that cost was for nothing.</p>
<p>Keep it, split. Ticket volumes reconciled against the help desk are presented as verified; the resolution rate and the reason breakdown are presented with a mark that they are under review, with what I found so far. The cost: the figures she most wanted are the ones marked, and a split dashboard invites the executive team to ask why.</p>
<p>I split it, because the volumes are verifiable in the hour and the expansion case does not rest on them alone; she decides whether to show the marked figures. If the reconciliation itself failed, I would recommend withdrawing all of it.</p>
<p><strong>What I tell her.</strong> What I verified and how, what I found in the change log, what I have not verified, and when tomorrow I will have it, in writing, before she leaves.</p>
<p><strong>The constraint conflict.</strong> A deadline tomorrow against correctness, with a relationship cost on both sides: admitting a fault before I know it is one, or defending a number I have not checked.</p>
<p><strong>Where this fails.</strong> The first real discrepancy found tends to end the search. A week boundary in UTC that moves a few percent of tickets between weeks is real, easy to fix and satisfying, and may have nothing to do with what she saw. The guard is to take each finding back to her belief from the first ten minutes and ask whether it explains it; a finding that does not explain the belief is a second bug, not the answer.</p>
</Fragment>

<ul>
<li><em>She says "it just feels off" and will not name a belief.</em> Show her last week's top five reasons and ask her to rank them herself before she sees the dashboard's order; the disagreement is the belief.</li>
<li><em>The hand-labelled sample agrees with the model 95 percent of the time.</em> The classifier is not the problem on that reason; the definition check and the reconciliation are where to look next.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: she says a split dashboard will look worse to her executives than no dashboard. Do you change your recommendation, and what do you write down?* She owns the presentation; the recommendation stays, and the note records which figures were verified and which she chose to show or hold.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see to move the classification from the LLM to the help desk's own reason field that agents fill in?* A sample showing agents' labels agree with careful labels at least as often as the model's, at a coverage the dashboard needs; agent fields are often blank or defaulted, which is why the model was there.
- *Your team's prompt change caused it. Who tells the customer, how, and what changes in your release process?* You tell her, with the before and after on the same tickets; prompt changes to the classifier run against a frozen labelled set and the dashboard shows the prompt version in effect for each week.
- *Two weeks later the executive team asks for the resolution rate back. What has to be true before you publish it again?* The definition agreed with her in writing, the classifier checked against a labelled set after the rename, and history either recomputed or marked at the change.

## Common mistakes

- Opening the classifier prompt before learning what she believes is wrong.
- Debugging every number at once instead of separating source counts from model-derived figures.
- Stopping at the first discrepancy without checking whether it explains her complaint.
- Missing a definition mismatch and fixing code that was correct by its own definition.
- Letting her leave with no written statement of what was checked and what was not.
