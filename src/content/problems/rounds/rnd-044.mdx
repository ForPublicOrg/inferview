## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported by a question-bank vendor, not confirmed by the company">
A vendor's question bank lists "You have text-to-music capability. How would you productise it?" as a product-sense prompt, attributed to OpenAI from candidate reports it says are not verified. The case below is original material.
</Callout>

What the interviewer tells you when asked (scenario):

- Your company has a model that turns a text prompt into a 30-second instrumental clip in about 20 seconds on one GPU. It can also sing, but vocals are weaker and legal has not cleared them.
- Licensing talks with the major labels are open and will not close this quarter. Legal's position: no generation in the style of a named artist, and no product that hands out files until there is a way to catch outputs that resemble existing recordings.
- Six engineers, a launch review in one quarter, and a fixed GPU allocation.

Productise it. Pick the user, the job and the surface; define the success metric with its guardrails; work out the cost per outcome; say what the user cannot detect going wrong and how you would catch it; and write the launch plan with what you give up.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which users today pay for music they do not care about owning, and what they do with the file afterwards.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the number of clips a test user generates before keeping one.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask legal what evidence would let a file leave the product: a screen with a measured miss rate, a licence, or both.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Two products are defensible. A creator tool for short-video makers who need background beds and export them: clear willingness to pay and a measurable outcome, but every exported file carries rights exposure, so it cannot ship until a similarity screen exists. An in-app creation and sharing experience where clips play only inside the product, the shape Udio moved to after its UMG settlement: legal can approve it this quarter, at the cost of the users who need the file and leave. I would launch the in-app product this quarter as the place to measure prompt quality and the screen's miss rate, and open export to creators once the screen's measured misses satisfy legal. The metric is clips kept and played back by someone other than the creator, with guardrails on screen blocks, takedown reports and cost per kept clip.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The user and the job.</strong> The text-to-music capability serves at least three users: people making a song for fun, creators who need a 30-second bed under a video, and developers who want music in their own product. A 30-second instrumental is close to what the second group buys today, but their job ends with a file on another platform, which is exactly what legal has not cleared. The first group's job can end inside the product.</p>
<p><strong>The decision.</strong> Export-first creator tool or in-app-first. Export-first wins if legal clears it and if the outcome is "a clip in a published video", which is easy to price. Its cost is exposure: a file that resembles a recording can draw a claim on the creator's video, and the creator could not have known. In-app-first wins on time to launch and on control of distribution; the press record shows the cost of that control when it arrives late, since Udio's users reacted badly when downloads were disabled after the UMG deal. Suno's WMG terms sit between the two: free-tier songs play and share but do not download, paid tiers get a monthly download cap. I choose in-app-first now, with export designed as a capped, paid feature that switches on per account once the screen meets the bar legal sets.</p>
<p><strong>The failure the user cannot detect.</strong> A clip that closely resembles an existing recording sounds like a good result to the person who made it. Prompting for a named artist is refused by policy, but resemblance can arise without a named artist. The control is a similarity screen against a reference catalogue at generation time, with its recall measured before launch on a seeded set: prompts written to elicit known recordings, and clips with known recordings mixed in. The screen's miss rate is the number legal needs; its false-block rate is the number users feel.</p>
<p><strong>A quantity I choose: cost per kept clip.</strong> Scenario inputs: 20 GPU-seconds per clip and a GPU-hour at $2 give 20 / 3600 × $2 ≈ $0.011 per clip. If a test user generates 8 clips before keeping one, a kept clip costs about $0.089 in generation alone, before the screen's compute. The ratio of generated to kept is the term that dominates and the one the product can move: better prompt controls (tempo, mood, length) and variations of a kept clip instead of fresh generations both lower it. Measure it in a closed beta of a few hundred users before fixing prices; a free tier sized on a guess of 2 clips per kept clip is the unreasonable choice, since a fourfold miss on that ratio quadruples the cost of every free user.</p>
<p><strong>Metric and guardrails.</strong> The success metric: clips kept and played at least once by someone other than the creator within seven days, which counts clips that did a job rather than generations. Guardrails: the screen's block rate and false-block reports, takedown or rights reports per 1,000 shared clips, cost per kept clip, and p95 generation latency against the 20-second baseline. Generations per user is not the metric; it rises when the model gets worse.</p>
<p><strong>The eval plan before launch.</strong> Prompt adherence on attributes that can be checked automatically (tempo with a beat tracker, length, presence of vocals when none were asked for). Audio quality by pairwise listening against the current release on the same prompts. Screen recall and false-block rate on the seeded set. Refusal of named-artist prompts, including misspellings and descriptions that point at one artist.</p>
<p><strong>The constraint conflict.</strong> Speed to market against rights exposure, with a fixed GPU budget: export is where the value and the revenue are, and it is the one thing that cannot ship this quarter without a screen whose miss rate legal accepts.</p>
<p><strong>Failure mode of the recommendation.</strong> In-app-first can teach the wrong lesson. The users who stay in a no-export product are the ones having fun, and their behaviour says little about creators who need files; tuning the model and prompts for them can produce a product creators do not want when export opens. Tracking a creator cohort separately in the beta, with export offered under a test agreement, guards against it. A second failure: the screen's recall is measured on recordings in the reference catalogue, so resemblance to recordings outside it goes uncaught, and the miss rate reported to legal is a lower bound on the real one.</p>
<p><strong>What you give up.</strong> Vocals, named-artist style, exports in v1, and the creator revenue that exports would bring this quarter.</p>
<p><strong>The launch plan.</strong> Closed beta: in-app creation, sharing by link inside the product, instrumental only, screen on every clip. Gate to open beta: screen recall on the seeded set above legal's bar, kept-clip cost measured, false-block rate below an agreed level. Export: paid tier, monthly cap, per-account switch. Rollback triggers: a rights report confirmed against a shared clip disables sharing for that clip's prompt pattern and sends the case to the screen's regression set; screen false blocks above the agreed rate for a week pause the open-beta expansion.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why instrumental only, when vocals are what people share?</em> Vocals add voice likeness to the rights question and the model is weaker there; both are reasons to ship them second, with their own screen.</li>
<li><em>Who owns the reference catalogue for the screen?</em> It has to come from licensors or a fingerprinting vendor; without one, the screen's recall cannot be measured and legal's condition cannot be met.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the screen adds 6 seconds to every 20-second generation and costs a third as much as the generation itself. Which half do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?* Run the screen only on clips that are kept or shared, not on every generation; the accepted risk is that unscreened clips exist in the user's history, which matters only once they can leave the product.
- *Reversal: what would make you switch to export-first?* A licence that covers the outputs, or a screen whose measured miss rate legal signs off, plus beta evidence that creators keep clips at a ratio that prices out.
- *The label deal closes with a term that free-tier clips may never leave the product. Your creator cohort is all free-tier. What changes?* The creator product becomes paid from day one, and the beta's free-tier creator data stops predicting conversion; rerun the cohort with a paid trial.
- *A competitor ships export with no screen and grows faster. The CEO asks why you are slower. What do you put in writing?* The exposure per exported file, the measured miss rate you would ship with, and the date the screen meets legal's bar.

## Common mistakes

- Starting from features (genres, stems, editing) before picking a user and the job that ends with a kept clip.
- Measuring generations per user, which rises when the model gets worse.
- Ignoring the failure the user cannot hear: an output that resembles a recording.
- Pricing a free tier without measuring how many generations a kept clip costs.
- Treating the licensing question as legal's problem rather than a product constraint that decides the surface.
