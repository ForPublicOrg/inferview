## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported in a hiring-manager round">
A candidate report from Anthropic's machine learning engineer loop lists these questions about one past project: why use AI or machine learning on that problem, how you knew it was the right use case, whether a business user was in the loop, whether people adopted it, and how long it took to reach production.
</Callout>

Pick one project of your own where a model made or shaped a decision for users. Answer the five questions in about five minutes. Then write down the number you would use to claim adoption, the baseline you compared the model against, and the date by which you would have killed the project if the number had not moved.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Find out what the process did before your model existed, and what it cost per decision.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Pull the rate at which users overrode, edited or ignored the model's output in the first month.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Check whether using the feature was optional for the people you count as adopters.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A complete answer names the decision the model made, what made it before (a person, a rule, nothing), and why a model beat the simplest rule you could have shipped, with the measurement that showed it. Adoption is a number you chose before launch, on users who could opt out, with an override rate next to it. Then one thing that argued against machine learning, and how you handled it.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Why a model.</b> The question has two defensible answers and the story has to say which one it was. Ship a heuristic first and collect data: cheap, debuggable, and Zinkevich's first rule says to launch without machine learning when it is not required. Cost: a heuristic that grows special cases becomes unmaintainable, which is his third rule's reason to switch. Go straight to a model: right when the input is unstructured text or images, when the rules would be a list nobody can maintain, or when a model already exists that does the task with a prompt. Cost: you need labelled examples to know it works, and you own its failure modes from day one. The story is credible when it names the heuristic you rejected and the evidence (an error analysis, a pilot, an offline comparison on labelled cases) that made the model worth its cost.</p>
<p><b>How you knew it was the right use case.</b> Three facts, each from your project: the cost of a wrong output and who caught it; the volume, which decides whether automating saves anything; and the quality bar the business user agreed to before launch, on a labelled set you can describe. If the business user was in the loop, say what they changed: a label definition, a threshold, a review step.</p>
<p><b>Adoption.</b> The reader chooses the metric; defensible ones are the share of eligible decisions where users accepted the model's output unedited, the share of eligible users who used it weekly when it was optional, or the drop in time per case. Usage counts where the feature was mandatory are not adoption. State the number with its window, and put the override rate next to it, because high usage with high overrides means people are working around the model.</p>
<p><b>The conflict.</b> Deadline against a measured baseline. A team under a launch date often skips building the heuristic baseline, and then cannot say whether the model beat anything. If that happened in your project, say so and say what you measured instead.</p>
<p><b>Time to production.</b> The date of the first user-facing release, what was in it, and what blocked it longest. If the answer is "evaluation", say what the evaluation was.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li>Your heuristic baseline got most of the way there. Was the model worth its maintenance cost, and who decided?</li>
<li>Your adoption number counts accepted outputs. How do you know users accepted them because they were right and not because checking took too long?</li>
<li>What would you have needed to see in the first month to switch the model off and go back to the rule?</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

### Where this answer goes wrong

The structure above invites a story where machine learning was the right call. The question can also be answered with the opposite story: a project where you argued against a model, shipped a rule, and measured that it was enough. If you have that story, it answers "how did you know it was the right use case" more directly than a success does. The failure mode of telling only the success is that the follow-up "did you consider not using ML at all?" finds no prepared answer, or finds a baseline that was never built.

### What to write down before the round

- The decision the model made, in one sentence, and what made it before.
- The baseline you compared against, with its measured quality on the same set.
- The adoption metric, its value, its window, and whether use was optional.
- The override or edit rate in the first month.
- The date the model reached users and the longest blocker before it.
- One thing you would do differently, with what it cost.

## Follow-ups

- *Your model costs more per decision than the rule it replaced, and quality went up by a margin the business user cannot see. Do you keep it, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree about whether to replace your model with a prompt to a hosted model. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *The business user who championed the project left. Adoption falls by half in a quarter. What do you look at first?*
- *If you built the same feature today, which part would a hosted model replace, and which part of your system would still be needed?*

## Common mistakes

- Answering "why ML" with the technique ("we fine-tuned a classifier") instead of the decision and the rejected alternative.
- No baseline, so "it worked" has nothing to be compared with.
- Counting usage of a mandatory feature as adoption.
- An adoption number with no override or edit rate next to it.
- Treating the business user as an audience rather than someone who changed the system.
