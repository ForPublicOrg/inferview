## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported format">
A candidate report from Cognition's forward deployed engineer loop describes a take-home assignment submitted with a five-minute Loom video explaining it. The report does not say who watches the video or what happens next.
</Callout>

You have finished a take-home: a small service that takes a customer's support tickets, groups them by root cause with a model call, and writes a summary per group. The brief was two paragraphs long and left the grouping rule to you.

Plan the five-minute recording. Write down the order of the sections, how many seconds each gets, what is on screen during each, and which input you will run live. Assume the recording is the first thing a reviewer sees of your work and they cannot ask you anything while watching.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask the recruiter whether the video is watched before a follow-up call and whether that call starts from it.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Record sixty seconds of yourself explaining one function, count the words, and use that rate to size the script.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Watch your first take at the size a reviewer would, and note every moment you could not read the code.</p></details>

## Answer

The recording is a presentation with no interruptions, so every probe a live reviewer would ask has to be answered before they think of it. Five minutes is 300 seconds; the decision is where they go.

### The decision: demo first or decisions first

- **Demo first.** Run the service on a hard input in the opening thirty seconds, then explain. The reviewer knows it works before hearing why. Cost: if the run is slow or the output is long, the demo eats the time the decisions needed.
- **Decisions first.** Open on the ambiguity in the brief and the choice you made, then show the run as evidence. Cost: a reviewer who wants to see it working waits two minutes and may stop watching closely.

Demo first wins when the run is short and the output fits on one screen, which is true of a grouping service on a dozen tickets. Pick the input deliberately: tickets where two groups share vocabulary, so the grouping decision is visible in the output.

### One allocation, computed

| Section | Seconds | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| The run, on the hard input | 30 | terminal and output |
| The brief as you read it, and the assumption you made | 30 | the brief, highlighted |
| Two decisions and what you rejected | 90 | the two relevant functions |
| One failure handled, one not handled | 60 | the error path, then the gap |
| What is missing before a customer could use it | 60 | a short list in the README |
| How you used AI tools | 30 | nothing new; say it |

30 + 30 + 90 + 60 + 60 + 30 = 300 seconds. Any allocation that gives the decisions and the failure handling together at least half the time is defensible. One that spends more than a minute on setup, folder structure or reading the brief aloud is not, because the reviewer has the brief and the repository.

### Size the script from your own rate

Measure your speaking rate from the sixty-second recording in hint 2. Then time the silent parts (the run, switching files, scrolling) and subtract them from 300 seconds. The remainder, in minutes, times your rate is the word budget for the script. A script written to five times your per-minute rate overruns, because the silent parts have no words in them.

### The conflict: polish against the take-home itself

Every re-take costs time that could have gone into the code, and a heavily edited video can look rehearsed in a way that a live call will not match. One or two takes, with a written outline rather than a word-for-word script, is the balance I would choose. The failure mode of that choice is a stumble at the one point that matters; if it lands on a decision or the failure path, re-record that take.

### What reviewers can check that you cannot hide

The reviewer can pause, rewind and read your code at their own speed, which a live audience cannot. A claim in the video that the code contradicts ("it retries on timeout") is found. Say only what the code does, and name the gaps yourself.

## Follow-ups

- *On the call, the reviewer says your grouping rule merges two root causes a customer would want separated. Do you defend the rule or change it, and what would you need to see in their tickets to change your mind?*
- *You said retries were missing. The customer goes live next week. Which of your missing items do you build first, and which risk do you accept in writing?*
- *Your video ran 5:40. Which section did you cut to get it under five minutes, and what did the reviewer lose?*

## Common mistakes

- Spending the first minute on the folder structure or reading the brief aloud.
- Demonstrating only the happy path, so the reviewer has to guess how the code fails.
- Claiming behaviour the code does not have; the reviewer has the repository open.
- Code on screen at a size that cannot be read in a video player.
- Leaving out how AI tools were used, then being asked about it with no prepared answer.
