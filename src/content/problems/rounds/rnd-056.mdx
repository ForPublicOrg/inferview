## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported by a candidate, not confirmed by the company">
"Tell me about an ethical or policy concern you raised under pressure."
</Callout>

Use a real one; a story invented for the round does not survive the second follow-up. To work the structure on a case, use this one (scenario). You lead the engineering side of a contract-review assistant that flags risky clauses for in-house legal teams. Launch is in six days. The VP of Marketing has approved a press release saying the assistant is "98% accurate at finding risky clauses". The number comes from a set of 200 contracts the team curated during development: 196 flagged correctly. On 300 contracts sampled from the pilot customers' real uploads, it flagged 261 correctly. The VP says the curated set is "the benchmark", the release is already with the agency, and the CEO is quoted in it.

Say what you did, in what order, whom you told before you went over anyone's head, what the release said in the end, and what it cost you.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask the VP what claim the release needs to make for the launch to work, separately from the number.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the error breakdown on the 300 real contracts: which clause types it misses.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask who in legal reviews public product claims, and by when they need the text.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>I went to the VP first, the same day, with one page: the 98 percent comes from contracts we picked while building, the pilot customers' own contracts give 87 percent with a range of about 83 to 91, and a customer's legal team will measure it on their contracts. I proposed a claim we could defend and told her that if the number stayed, I would ask counsel to review it before release, and that I was telling her first. She disagreed and we went to counsel together. The release went out on time with the pilot result and its conditions. It cost me a week of a strained working relationship and a slower claim than marketing wanted.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The evidence, computed so anyone could check it.</strong> 196 of 200 is 98 percent on contracts selected by the people who built the system, which is a development set, not an estimate of what customers will see. On the 300 real uploads, 261 of 300 is 87 percent, and the 95 percent interval is about 0.87 ± 1.96 × √(0.87 × 0.13 / 300) ≈ 0.87 ± 0.04, so 83 to 91 percent. The misses were concentrated in indemnity and limitation-of-liability clauses, the two types customers care about most, which made the gap a product risk and not only a wording problem.</p>
<p><strong>The quantity I chose to defend.</strong> What the release could say: I proposed "87 percent of risky clauses found on contracts from pilot customers, with a lawyer reviewing every flag". A lower bound (83 percent) is also defensible and safer; the point estimate with its conditions is defensible if the conditions are printed. The 98 percent without conditions is not, and neither is any number without saying whose contracts it was measured on.</p>
<p><strong>The order, which was the decision.</strong> Two defensible paths. Go straight to counsel: fastest way to stop a claim that may not be lawful, at the cost of the VP learning about it from legal, which ends the working relationship and teaches her to route around engineering next time. Or go to the VP first with the evidence and an alternative claim, and tell her what I would do if we disagreed: slower by a day and gives her the chance to fix it herself, at the cost of time the launch did not have. I chose the second because there were six days and counsel needed two, so a day of conversation fit. With one day left I would have gone to counsel and the VP at the same time.</p>
<p><strong>Why it was more than taste.</strong> The FTC's Operation AI Comply in September 2024 included an action against DoNotPay, which the FTC alleged had marketed an AI legal service without testing whether its output matched a human lawyer's. A public accuracy claim is a claim about evidence. I put that in one sentence in the note to counsel and did not lead with it to the VP; leading with a regulator turns a disagreement into a threat.</p>
<p><strong>Where this answer fails.</strong> The 87 percent has its own scope: three pilot customers' contracts over two months. Once printed, it gets quoted to customers with different contracts, and the next team will be asked to defend it. I added the measurement conditions to the release and to the sales deck, and an owner to re-measure it each quarter on new customers' uploads. The other failure is being the engineer who escalates every disagreement; I kept the escalation to the one claim and agreed with the rest of the release as written.</p>
<p><strong>If counsel had approved the 98 percent.</strong> Then the call was theirs, not mine. I would have written my disagreement and the evidence into the launch record and stopped there; escalating past counsel on a judgement call they own is not the same as raising a concern.</p>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the VP says every company in the market quotes its best benchmark, and your caution will cost the launch its headline. What do you accept in writing, and what do you refuse?* Accept a benchmark number if the release names it as a benchmark on curated contracts next to the pilot number; refuse an unqualified accuracy claim, because the customer's legal team is the audience and will measure it.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see to agree to the 98 percent?* A held-out set of real customer contracts, not chosen by the team, on which it scores at that level, with the interval reported.
- *Two directors disagree on whether this is a marketing decision or a legal one. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* The content of a public claim about product performance is marketing's; whether it can be substantiated is legal's. Both sign, on the same page, and engineering supplies the evidence for both.
- *Six months later sales is quoting 87 percent to a customer in a different industry. What do you do?* Tell the account owner the conditions under which the number holds, offer a measurement on the customer's own sample during the trial, and update the sales deck so the conditions travel with the number.

## Common mistakes

- A story where the concern was raised and nothing it cost is mentioned.
- Going over someone's head without telling them first, and presenting it as courage.
- A concern that is really a preference, with no evidence and no proposed alternative.
- No account of what you would have done if you had lost.
- A story about someone else's concern, told in the first person.
