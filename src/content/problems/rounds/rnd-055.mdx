## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported by a prep vendor, not confirmed by the company">
"If you were deciding whether to release a new AI model, what criteria would you use?"
</Callout>

Answer as the person who owns the go or no-go, and tie it to a release you have been part of if you have one. For the deep dive, use this case (scenario). Your team post-trained a new version of the model behind an API with 900 business customers and a consumer app. It is better on your capability evals. One cyber eval sits just under the threshold your company set for extra safeguards. On a 300-prompt behaviour eval, the rate of responses that agree with a false premise went from 2 percent to 3 percent. Twelve of forty customers who shared their production prompts see a regression on at least one. A competitor shipped a comparable model last week, and leadership wants a date.

Name your criteria, say which ones block the release and which ones are tracked after it, what you would decide here, and what is written down and signed.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what form the release takes: API only, the consumer app, or weights.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the eval's size and how many prompts it would take to tell 2 percent from 3 percent.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask the twelve customers' account owners what each regression breaks.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Four groups of criteria, each tied to a decision. Severe-harm capability: if the model crosses a threshold, it does not ship until safeguards are shown to reduce the risk enough, and the residual risk is written down; that is how OpenAI's Preparedness Framework and Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy are built. Behaviour: regressions on known failure modes block, including qualitative ones, which is the change OpenAI made after the April 2025 GPT-4o rollback. Customer regressions: the model ships if every regression has an owner and a fallback, such as pinning the old version. Reversibility: an API rollout can be rolled back, weights cannot, so the bar rises with the form of release. Here I would ship to the API with the old version pinnable, hold the consumer app until the behaviour eval is large enough to read, and not release weights.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Severe-harm thresholds block, with a person deciding.</strong> The cyber eval just under the threshold is the first item, because "just under" depends on how hard the evaluators tried to elicit the capability. I would ask the evaluators for their elicitation effort and the run-to-run spread before accepting "under". OpenAI's framework puts this in a Capabilities Report and a Safeguards Report, with residual risk and notable limitations, to a Safety Advisory Group that recommends and leadership that decides. A smaller company needs the same three parts: the evidence, the safeguards with their measured efficacy, and a named decider.</p>
<p><strong>Behaviour evals: block or track, and the number that decides it.</strong> A 300-prompt eval cannot tell 2 percent from 3 percent: 6 against 9 events. To detect a doubling from 2 to 4 percent at 95 percent confidence and 80 percent power with two independent samples takes about (1.96 + 0.84)² × (0.02 × 0.98 + 0.04 × 0.96) / 0.02² ≈ 1,137 prompts per model; running both models on the same prompts and comparing pairs needs fewer. I would set the blocking eval at roughly 1,000 to 1,500 prompts for the failure modes that have hurt users before, and keep smaller sets as tracking signals. Two defensible positions: make behaviour evals blocking, which costs delayed launches and false blocks from noisy measurements; or track them and rely on a fast rollback, which costs users exposure until the rollback. OpenAI ran the second for hallucination and deception before April 2025 and moved behaviour issues to blocking after it.</p>
<p><strong>Customer regressions: ship with owners, not with zero.</strong> Twelve of forty regressing is normal for a model change; the criterion is that each regression is triaged (wrong, or different and fine), the customers whose regressions break them can pin the old version with a stated end date, and the release notes say what changed. Zero regressions as a criterion would block every release.</p>
<p><strong>Reversibility sets the bar.</strong> The same model can be fine for the API and not for weights. OpenAI's framework treats releasing weights or enabling fine-tuning as a change in deployment conditions that can need new reports. A staged rollout (internal, opt-in alpha, a percentage of API traffic, then the app) buys detection time only if someone watches a named metric at each stage with a rollback trigger.</p>
<p><strong>The competitor.</strong> A competitor's release changes the market, not the measurements. OpenAI's framework allows adjusting safeguards when another developer ships without comparable ones, but only if overall risk does not meaningfully rise, the change is announced, and its safeguards stay more protective than the other developer's. That is a narrow exception, not a deadline.</p>
<p><strong>Decision on the case.</strong> API release in stages with the old version pinnable for 90 days; the consumer app held for the time it takes to run a 1,200-prompt behaviour eval, about a week; no weights. Signed: the capability determination by the safety lead, the residual risk by the product owner, and the customer regression list by the head of the API business.</p>
<p><strong>Where this answer fails.</strong> Criteria fixed before a launch become targets: teams tune to the blocking evals, and a failure mode nobody wrote down ships green. The defence is a qualitative review that can block in writing, which brings back the judgement the criteria were meant to replace. A staged rollout has its own gap: a harm that occurs once in 10,000 conversations is expected about five times in an alpha of 50,000 conversations, and is seen only if a classifier or a person is looking for it.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: running a 1,200-prompt behaviour eval on every candidate costs more than the team that trains the model. Which half do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?* Keep the large set only for failure modes with a history of user harm, run on the final candidate; use small sets during training. The written risk: a new failure mode with no history ships unmeasured, caught only by qualitative review and post-launch monitoring.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in production to move a behaviour criterion from tracked to blocking?* An incident attributable to it, or a monitored rate after launch above the rate the team agreed was acceptable, for two consecutive releases.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one says the cyber eval result is noise, the other says it is a crossing. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Rerun with more elicitation effort and report the spread; if the result is still ambiguous, treat it as crossed for the safeguards decision, because the cost of the safeguard is known and the cost of being wrong is not.
- *Operating: leadership wants the current model retired in sixty days, not ninety, to free serving capacity, and four of the twelve regressing customers still regress on the final candidate. Which of them ship degraded, and who signs off?* The ones whose regression is a different but acceptable output ship after their account owner confirms with the customer; the ones with a broken workflow get a migration plan with a named engineer, and the head of the API business signs the list, because shortening the pin window is a business decision with these customers named on it.

## Common mistakes

- A list of evals with no statement of which ones block.
- "Zero regressions" as a criterion.
- Treating API release and weight release as the same decision.
- Letting a competitor's launch date stand in for a criterion.
- No named person who decides and no written residual risk.
