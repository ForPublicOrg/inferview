## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Llama 3 uses a RoPE base of 500,000 where the original paper used 10,000. Why not just raise the base of a trained model to extend its context?"
</Callout>

Answer for a trained model with head dimension 128, base 10,000 and a trained window of 4,096 positions that you want to run at 16,384. Say what changes for the fastest pair and the slowest pair, and when raising the base is the right answer.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Compute the wavelength of the slowest pair at the old base and compare it with 4,096.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask whether the base would change before pretraining or after it.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Raising the base of a trained model is NTK-aware interpolation: it slows the slow pairs a lot and the fast pairs barely, which is the right shape, but the stretch it gives is not the stretch you asked for, so some slow pairs still reach angles they never saw in training. The YaRN paper reports exactly that, plus inferior fine-tuning results to Position Interpolation, which is why YaRN replaced the base change with a per-frequency ramp. Raising the base before pretraining is different: the model learns with it, which is what Llama 3's 500,000 is.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The arithmetic.</b> Pair <i>i</i> rotates at θ<sub>i</sub> = base<sup>−2i/128</sup>, with wavelength 2π / θ<sub>i</sub>. At base 10,000 the fastest pair has a wavelength of 2π ≈ 6.3 tokens and the slowest 2π · 10,000<sup>126/128</sup> ≈ 54,410 tokens. In a 4,096-token window the slowest pair never completes a turn: the largest angle it ever saw is θ<sub>63</sub> × 4,096, about 0.47 radians. Running to 16,384 without any change takes it to about 1.9 radians, four times anything it was trained on.</p>
<p><b>What a larger base does.</b> The YaRN paper writes the NTK-aware change as b′ = b · s<sup>128/126</sup>, which for s = 4 is about 40,890. Pair 0 is unchanged (its exponent is zero), so local resolution is untouched. The slowest pair slows by s<sup>(128/126)(126/128)</sup> = 4 exactly, so it stays in range. The pairs in between slow by less than 4. In the module's widget, at this shape and factor, 17 of the 18 pairs that never complete a turn in 4,096 positions still exceed their trained range at 16,384, the worst by about 1.45 times. That is the paper's "out of bound" observation, and the reason it says the factor has to be set higher than the extension you want.</p>
<p><b>When raising the base is the answer.</b> Before or during long-context pretraining. A model trained with base 500,000 learns attention patterns on those frequencies from the start, and its slowest pair, with a wavelength of 2π · 500,000<sup>126/128</sup> ≈ 2.56 million tokens, turns slowly enough that long training sequences stay inside a fraction of a turn. Raising the base after training asks every pair except the first to work at an angle schedule the weights never saw.</p>
<p><b>The failure mode of this answer.</b> "Use YaRN instead" is not free either. YaRN keeps the fast pairs and interpolates the slow ones, but it is applied statically in most engines, so short prompts run on the stretched schedule too. The base change, YaRN and PI all trade something; only measurement on your task at your lengths says which trade is acceptable.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- A colleague raised the base on a trained model and reports perplexity on long documents went down. What do you ask for before believing the model got better at long context?
- Why does a frequency-dependent method need the trained window length as an input, while Position Interpolation does not?

## Common mistakes

- Treating Llama 3's base of 500,000 as evidence that a base change works on a model trained at 10,000.
- Assuming the base change slows every pair by the factor; only the slowest pair slows by exactly s.
- Forgetting that the fastest pairs carry local order, and that any method that slows them pays for it on short-range tasks.
