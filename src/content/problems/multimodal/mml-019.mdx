## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 45 minutes">
"Design the real-time voice system behind our customer-support phone line: audio in, recognition, orchestration with tools, speech out, session state, interruptions, safety, latency and cost. Tell me what you would build first and what you would measure."
</Callout>

The situation, as a scenario:

- A subscription retailer's support line. Callers check orders, change delivery addresses and request refunds. Most turns need one backend call (order lookup takes 200 to 900 ms; the refund API is slower and its owners will not change it this year).
- Calls arrive over the phone network through an existing SIP provider; a web widget follows next year.
- Refunds above a threshold must be confirmed by the caller and logged with the exact words the agent said. Legal requires that the agent never promises a refund amount the policy engine has not approved.
- The business wants "no dead air" and has a cost ceiling per resolved call it will not share until you propose a number.
- Three engineers, a launch in one region in four months.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A cascaded pipeline: SIP media into a session service, streaming recognition with a semantic turn detector, a text agent with tools whose replies stream clause by clause through a policy check into streaming TTS. Each session is one stateful worker that owns cancellation, with conversation state and the audit log written per turn. A watchdog filler covers slow tools and the refund flow speaks only amounts returned by the policy engine. Target P95 first audio at 1,500 ms, measured from the media edge, and report answer latency separately.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Architecture.</strong> The SIP provider forks media to a session service; one worker per call holds the media stream, the recognizer connection, the turn detector and the outbound audio queue, so cancellation on barge-in is a local operation. Recognition streams interim and final transcripts. Turn detection is a VAD with a short trigger followed by a semantic or audio-native turn model, because callers read order numbers with pauses. The orchestrator is a text agent with tools (order lookup, address change, refund request) and a short system prompt; it streams tokens. A policy filter sits between the agent and TTS and sees the reply so far plus each new clause; it blocks unapproved amounts by checking every currency figure against the policy engine's response for this turn. TTS streams from the first approved clause.</p>
<p><strong>State.</strong> Session state (caller identity after verification, the order in focus, pending confirmations) lives in the worker and is checkpointed to a store at each turn end, keyed by call id, so a worker crash resumes the call with a "sorry, I lost you for a moment" rather than starting over. Conversation history holds what the caller heard: on barge-in the assistant message is truncated using TTS word timestamps.</p>
<p><strong>Latency.</strong> The primer's typical cascaded stack takes 1,293 ms microphone to speaker against a 1,500 ms target; the phone path adds its own transport, so the first deliverable is a measured round trip on the SIP path. Choose the LLM on P95 time to first token, since measured hosted P95s range from 712 to 1,771 ms. For tool turns, prefetch the caller's recent orders when the call connects, and run a watchdog: if a tool has not returned in 400 ms, play a cached phrase. The refund API gets a spoken acknowledgement before the call, because it is always slow.</p>
<p><strong>Safety and audit.</strong> The audit log records, per turn, the final transcript of the caller, the exact text sent to TTS, the TTS audio id and the playout cut point if interrupted. Refund confirmation is a structured step: the agent reads back the approved amount and the caller's "yes" is captured by the recognizer and stored with its audio segment.</p>
<p><strong>Cost.</strong> Price it per resolved call: recognition per audio minute, LLM tokens per turn with the prompt cached, TTS per character, plus telephony. Propose a ceiling from a pilot's measured median, not from list prices.</p>

### Two defensible designs

**Cascaded pipeline (recommended).** Text between every stage gives the policy filter a place to block an amount before it is spoken, gives Legal a text record of what was said, and lets the team swap the recognizer or TTS independently. Cost: turn-taking is a decision made by the endpointer rather than heard by the model, more components to operate, and a per-clause policy check on the critical path.

**Full-duplex speech front end with the same text backend.** A full-duplex voice layer (GPT-Live in OpenAI's lineup, at $0.05 per minute plus the backend) handles turn-taking and filler while the existing text agent does tools and policy. Cost: whatever the voice layer says on its own while the backend works is outside the policy filter, two vendors' latencies in series on tool turns, and an audit record assembled from two systems. This wins if pilot recordings show cut-offs and awkward overlap are what callers complain about, and Legal accepts a check on the backend's text only.

A pure speech-to-speech session is the third option, and the refund constraint rules it out for this line: the amount would be generated as audio with no text stage to block it first.

The conflict is "no dead air" against "never promise an unapproved amount": the cheapest latency fix (start speaking as soon as the model does) is the one that removes the policy check's chance to act.

### Choose the numbers

- **Watchdog threshold.** Something between 300 and 600 ms is defensible given a 200 to 900 ms lookup; under 200 ms puts filler on lookups that would have returned in time, and over a second leaves dead air on every refund.
- **Latency target.** P95 first audio of 1,500 ms from the media edge, with answer latency for tool turns reported but not targeted at launch. A target of 800 ms copied from a vendor post measures a different interval and is not reachable on a P95 with tools.
- **Policy-filter budget.** Under 50 ms per clause is a reasonable design target for a rule-based amount check; a model-based filter on every clause blows the budget.

### Written down: the launch plan

1. Month 1: measured round trip on the SIP path; session worker with cancellation; recognizer and turn detector evaluated on 200 recorded calls for cut-offs.
2. Month 2: agent with order lookup and address change; policy filter; audit log with playout cut points.
3. Month 3: refund flow with read-back confirmation; watchdog filler; shadow calls with staff.
4. Month 4: 5% of live calls, then 25%. Rollback triggers: any refund amount spoken that differs from the policy engine's approval; cut-off rate above the human-labelled baseline; P95 first audio above 2,000 ms for a day.

**What you give up:** the conversational overlap of a full-duplex model, some latency on every clause for the policy check, and cost savings from a cheaper, slower LLM.

### Where this fails

The clause-level filter sees the reply in pieces. A reply that says "we can refund" in one clause and "the full amount" in the next contains no currency figure for the amount check to catch, and it is still a promise. The filter has to evaluate the reply so far, and the refund flow has to keep amounts out of free text by having the agent read them from a template filled by the policy engine. Without that, the design passes its tests and fails Legal's requirement on the first creative phrasing.

## Follow-ups

- *Your per-clause policy check adds 120 ms and pushes P95 over target. Do you drop it for low-value turns or accept the miss, and who signs the risk note?*
- *What would you need to see in the first month of live calls to switch to the full-duplex front end?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants recordings as the audit record, the other wants the TTS text log. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *The web widget arrives next year over WebRTC. Which parts of the design change, and which numbers do you re-measure?*

## Common mistakes

- Quoting 800 ms as the industry standard, rather than as one vendor's target for one interval.
- Adding stage medians and calling the sum the P95.
- Treating the transcript of the LLM's full reply as what the caller heard after an interruption.
- Putting a filler phrase into the latency target without reporting answer latency.
- Running the safety check only on the complete reply, which forces a wait for the full reply before speech.
- Choosing the LLM on benchmark quality and median latency, then finding its P95 time to first token exceeds the whole budget.
