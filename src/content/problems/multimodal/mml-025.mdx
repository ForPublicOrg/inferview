## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, as a candidate reported it">
"Design an image generation system end to end: data, model, safety, evaluation and inference. Then extend it so it takes and produces both text and images. Then pick a recent paper and walk me through it."
</Callout>

The situation, as a scenario:

- A consumer product inside a larger app. Launch in two quarters; a team of eight (four research, three infrastructure, one safety).
- Legal has approved two image sources: a licensed stock library with captions, and user-uploaded images where users opted in. Nothing scraped. Every training image must be traceable to its licence.
- The company already runs a large language model on its own serving stack, owned by another team.
- Product wants images in a few seconds and wants text in images (posters, cards) to be legible.
- The trust and safety lead wants no generation of real, identifiable people at launch, and will not sign off on output filtering alone.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Part 1: a latent rectified-flow transformer with joint text-image attention, trained on the licensed data after deduplication, safety filtering and recaptioning, with a large text encoder kept because the product needs legible text. Safety in three places: training data, prompt classification before generation, image classification after. Evaluation on a fixed prompt set with paired human preference plus automated adherence scores. Serving at 20 to 28 steps with guidance, bucketed by resolution, distillation as the second-quarter project. Part 2: compose, do not merge. The existing language model plans and writes prompts; the image model renders; an image encoder lets the language model read images. A single model that emits both modalities is the later bet. What I give up: the coherence of one model for edit-in-conversation, and two quarters of speed for the provenance requirement.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Data.</strong> Licence metadata travels with every image through every filtering stage, so any training example can be traced and removed; this is a table with an image id, licence id and stage outcomes, and removal triggers a retrain policy written down now (next scheduled run, not immediate). Pipeline: exact and near-duplicate removal, safety classification (sexual content, violence, minors, identifiable people per the launch policy), aesthetic and resolution filters, then recaptioning with the company's vision-language model, because stock captions are short and product prompts are long. Keep the original caption alongside; mixed caption sources at training make the model robust to short prompts. Oversample the images with rendered text, since legibility is a stated requirement.</p>
<p><strong>Model.</strong> A frozen autoencoder at downsampling 8 with a wide latent, then a transformer denoiser trained with rectified flow, text and image tokens in one attention, conditioning dropped for a fraction of examples (0.1 to 0.2, the range Ho and Salimans found equivalent) so guidance works. Train at 256 px, then fine-tune at 1024 px. Size the model before sizing steps: the DiT paper's comparison shows a larger model at 128 steps beating a smaller one at 1,000.</p>
<p><strong>Safety.</strong> The identifiable-people rule is enforced three times because each layer misses different cases: removed from training data (the model cannot draw what it never saw well), blocked at the prompt by a classifier and a name list, and checked on outputs by a face-matching classifier against public figures. The safety lead's refusal to rely on output filtering is right: output classifiers trained on public data miss the product's own failure cases, and every miss is a shipped image.</p>
<p><strong>Evaluation.</strong> A fixed prompt set, versioned, with slices for text rendering, counting, spatial relations, people (which should be refused), and the product's top use cases from the existing app's search logs. Automated adherence scores for every checkpoint; paired blind human preference for release decisions, with the acceptance rule decided before the run.</p>
<p><strong>Serving.</strong> Steps and guidance from the paired evaluation, not from the paper. Requests batched only within a resolution bucket. A distilled few-step student is the cost project for quarter three, with its own evaluation.</p>
<p><strong>Part 2.</strong> The language model already runs at scale and owns conversation; let it own the conversation here too. It rewrites the user's request into a prompt for the image model, calls it as a tool, and reads images through a vision encoder for "change the background of this". The image model gains an image-conditioning input for editing. This ships on existing stacks with existing owners.</p>

### Two defensible designs for part 2

**Compose (recommended).** The language model writes prompts and calls the image model; a vision encoder lets it read images. Cost: edits are lossy, because each turn regenerates from a text prompt and the image model never sees the conversation, so "keep everything, make the cup red" can change the rest of the image; two models with two owners and two release cadences; latency of an LLM turn plus a generation.

**One model that reads and writes both.** A single transformer over text tokens and image tokens (autoregressive over discrete image tokens, or a hybrid with a diffusion head) that sees the whole conversation and the images in it. Cost: a new pretraining run larger than anything in part 1, a serving path unlike both existing ones (image tokens decoded one at a time, or a diffusion loop inside a decode loop), and the eight-person team cannot build it alongside part 1 in two quarters. This wins if conversational editing is the product, not a feature, and the language-model team is already building the multimodal base.

The conflict is the launch date and team size against the product's edit experience; composition ships on time and gives up edit fidelity.

### Choose the numbers

- **Steps.** 20 to 28 with guidance on a rectified-flow model is a defensible launch range; the Stable Diffusion 3 paper's depth-38 model loses 0.08% CLIP score at 20 against 50 steps. Under 10 without distillation is not defensible for a product that needs legible text.
- **Evaluation set size.** For a paired preference test the 95% interval on a 50% rate is ±1.96 × √(0.25 / n). At n = 400 that is about ±4.9 points, enough to see a 55/45 preference; at n = 100 it is ±9.8 points, too wide for a release gate.
- **Drop rate for unconditional training.** 0.1 to 0.2. Much higher costs conditional quality, as the 0.5 setting did in the guidance paper.
- **Keep the 4.7B text encoder.** Dropping T5 in the Stable Diffusion 3 study won only 38% of typography comparisons against the full model; with legible text as a requirement, its memory is the price.

### Written down: the launch plan

1. Data pipeline with licence lineage and a removal procedure, signed by legal before training starts.
2. Base model trained and fine-tuned; release gate on the versioned prompt set, paired preference at n ≥ 400, and the people-refusal slice at the safety lead's agreed rate.
3. Beta at 5% of eligible users. Rollback triggers: any confirmed generation of an identifiable real person; report rate per thousand images above the beta's first-week baseline by an agreed margin; p95 latency above the product target for a day.
4. Part 2 composition behind a flag after part 1 has been stable for four weeks.

**What you give up:** training on scraped data and the quality it would buy; edit fidelity in part 2; speed, since three safety layers add latency to every request.

### Where this fails

The prompt classifier and the output face-matcher both look for named, famous people. A user who uploads a photo of a private person and asks to "put her in this scene" passes both: no public name, no match against public figures. The editing input added in part 2 creates exactly this path. It needs its own control (refuse edits of photos with faces unless the face belongs to the user, which needs a verification flow the product may not have) and its own evaluation slice before part 2 ships.

### The paper

A natural choice is DiT, walked through in this module's third lesson: a transformer over latent patches; quality tracking forward-pass Gflops; a larger model at fewer steps beating a smaller one at many; and the move onto language-model serving infrastructure. Tie it to this design: it is the reason the denoiser above is a transformer and the reason part 2 can share kernels with the language model.

## Follow-ups

- *Safety review triples your evaluation cost and pushes launch by six weeks. Which slice of the evaluation do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *Two senior researchers disagree on whether to train at 1024 px from the start or fine-tune from 256 px. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *What would you need to see in the first month of production to replace composition with a unified model?*
- *The language-model team will not take image generation as a tool call on their roadmap this half. What do you build instead, and who owns it?*

## Common mistakes

- Designing the model before the data's licence lineage, then discovering removal is impossible.
- One safety filter on outputs only, which the safety lead has already said will not pass.
- Quoting step counts from a paper as the product setting instead of measuring on the product's prompts.
- Proposing a single unified model for part 2 without pricing the pretraining run against the team and the date.
- Evaluating with FID on a public benchmark and calling it a release gate.
