## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Why does flow matching need fewer sampling steps than DDPM-style diffusion, and what does that change for serving? Our model was trained DDPM-style and research wants to retrain it with rectified flow. Would you?"
</Callout>

The situation, as a scenario: the model samples in 50 guided steps. The product lets enterprise customers fine-tune their own adapters on the base model; there are about 200 adapters in production, and customers retrain them on their own schedule. The latency target needs about half the current denoiser time within two quarters.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A sampler integrates a vector field from noise to data; fewer steps work when the paths are straighter. Flow matching trains the network to regress the velocity of a chosen path, and with the optimal-transport or rectified-flow path each noise-data pair is joined by a straight line at constant speed. DDPM-style paths curve, overshoot and remove most noise late, so a fixed-step solver needs more steps for the same error: the flow matching paper measured roughly 60% of the evaluations for equal error. For serving, step count multiplies cost directly, doubled by guidance. Would I retrain? Not as the first move here: the 200 adapters were trained against the current base and would all need retraining. I would first measure a better solver on the current model, then decide between a distilled student and the retrain on that data.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Why the step count drops.</strong> Sampling solves an ODE. Each solver step assumes the velocity is roughly constant over the step; the error per step grows with how much the path bends. Flow matching's optimal-transport conditional path is the straight interpolation between a noise sample and a data sample, so the conditional velocity is constant. Diffusion paths are not: the flow matching paper shows them overshooting and backtracking. In its Table 1, with an adaptive solver and the same U-Net, ImageNet 64 × 64 took 264 evaluations for DDPM against 138 for flow matching with OT, at a better FID (14.45 against 17.36).</p>
<p><strong>Why it does not drop to one.</strong> The network learns the average velocity over all the noise-data pairs passing through a point, and the average of straight lines is not straight. The marginal paths still curve. The Stable Diffusion 3 paper's depth-38 rectified-flow model loses 0.08% of its CLIP score at 20 steps and 0.14% at 10, but 2.71% at 5. Below about 10 steps, you need distillation.</p>
<p><strong>What it changes for serving.</strong> Denoiser cost is steps × guidance passes × cost per pass. A model that holds quality at 20 steps instead of 50 is 2.5 times cheaper per request with no change to the network's per-pass cost, batching or kernels. Rectified-flow models at high resolution also need the timestep shift they were trained with (the Stable Diffusion 3 paper uses 3.0 at 1024 px); a sampler config that omits it degrades quality in a way that looks like the step cut failing.</p>
<p><strong>The decision.</strong> Three routes, measured in this order. A better solver on the current model costs a config change and an evaluation, and may give part of the halving. A distilled few-step student of the current base (progressive distillation halves steps per round; adversarial distillation reports 1 to 4 steps) costs a training run and a new quality sign-off, and the adapters do not transfer cleanly to a student. A rectified-flow retrain costs a full base training run, a new sign-off, and every customer adapter. With 200 adapters on customers' schedules, the retrain is the route with the largest blast radius, and distillation has the same adapter problem at smaller scale.</p>

### Two defensible answers

**Retrain with rectified flow.** Fewer steps on every future model, no student to maintain, and research's likely quality gain. Cost: a base training run, two quarters of sign-off, and 200 customer adapters to retrain or migrate, with customers who chose their own schedules now forced onto yours. Defensible if the company plans a new base model within the year anyway: fold the objective change into that run, and the adapter migration happens once.

**Keep the base, distil for latency.** Leave the base and the adapters alone; serve a distilled student for traffic without adapters, and the full model for adapter traffic at a solver-tuned step count. Cost: two serving paths, a student to rebuild whenever the base changes, and adapter customers who get the slower path, which may be the customers paying most.

The conflict is the latency target against the customers' adapters: the cheapest path to half the denoiser time breaks something customers own, and the path that leaves them alone gives them the slower service.

### Choose the numbers

- **Step target for the retrained model.** 20 to 28 guided steps is a defensible launch setting for a rectified-flow model of this kind; 10 is defensible only with a paired evaluation on the product's prompts; under 10 needs distillation.
- **Adapter migration window.** Long enough for customers on quarterly retraining cycles to retrain once, so at least a quarter with both bases served in parallel.

### Where the recommended answer fails

The measure-first route assumes the better solver and the student together reach the target. If they do not, two quarters have gone to measurement and a student, and the retrain starts late with the same adapter problem. Put a date on the decision: if the solver measurement in the first month does not show at least a third of the halving, start the retrain plan immediately.

## Follow-ups

- *Your adapter migration plan needs a migration tool, docs and support for 200 customers, costing more engineering than the retrain. Which half do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one says flow matching's step savings come from the straight path, the other says they come from the solver. How do you settle it with an experiment, not rank?*
- *Guidance doubles every step. If research distils guidance into the model as well, how does that change the comparison between the three routes?*
- *What would you need to see after a retrain to roll back to the DDPM base?*

## Common mistakes

- Saying flow matching makes sampling one-step because the paths are straight; the conditional paths are, the learned marginal ones are not.
- Pricing the retrain as one training run and forgetting the customer adapters.
- Comparing step counts across models without matching the solver.
- Cutting steps on a rectified-flow model without the timestep shift it was trained with, then blaming the step cut.
