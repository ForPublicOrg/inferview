## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design review, 25 minutes">
"A team wants sign-off on this design for a voice agent that answers questions about energy bills. Review it. List every problem you see, in order of what you would fix first."
</Callout>

> **Billing voice agent: design v3**
>
> **Goal.** Answer billing questions by phone with industry-standard latency (800 ms) and full compliance with our content policy.
>
> **Flow per turn.**
> 1. Telephony streams caller audio to our server. The recognizer streams transcripts.
> 2. End of turn: server VAD with `silence_duration_ms: 1000` to avoid cutting off customers who pause.
> 3. The final transcript and the last 20 messages go to the LLM with tool definitions (account lookup, payment plan, tariff info).
> 4. If the model calls a tool, the orchestrator calls it and sends the result back to the model. Tools are synchronous; account lookup is about 600 ms at P50.
> 5. We wait for the complete reply, run it through the moderation model (about 150 ms), then send it to TTS. This guarantees nothing non-compliant is ever spoken.
> 6. TTS audio streams to the caller.
>
> **Interruptions.** If the caller speaks during playback, we stop playback and treat their speech as a new turn. The assistant's reply is kept in history so the model has full context.
>
> **Reliability.** If the LLM has not responded in 2 seconds, we retry once with the same request.
>
> **SLO.** P50 latency under 800 ms, measured from final transcript received to first TTS byte sent. Dashboards show P50 per day.
>
> **Out of scope for v3.** Per-stage tracing (planned for v4).

Write your list before opening the checklist.

## Answer

The design is reasonable on first read: it streams recognition, it has a compliance step, it handles interruptions and it retries. Most of its problems are latency terms placed in series and measurements that hide them.

**Waiting for the complete reply.** Step 5 puts the whole generation on the critical path. A 60-token reply at 10 ms per token (scenario values) is 600 ms of waiting before TTS starts; the primer's stack replaces that with 20 ms of sentence aggregation. Add moderation's 150 ms and the turn spends 750 ms on a guarantee that can be had more cheaply. The fix is to stream clauses into TTS and run the policy check on the reply so far each time a clause completes. Whether the whole-reply wait is acceptable is a judgement call: it is acceptable if replies are capped to one short sentence, or if it applies only to a narrow path (payment plan terms) while ordinary answers stream.

**A one-second silence window.** ElevenLabs puts it directly: waiting 700 ms of silence adds 700 ms to every turn. A 1,000 ms window adds a second, and it still cuts off customers who pause longer. A semantic or audio-native turn detector holds the turn on incomplete phrases and closes it quickly on complete ones.

**The target and its measurement.** 800 ms is ElevenLabs' P50 target from end of user speech, one vendor's figure. The doc measures from final transcript to first TTS byte sent, which excludes endpointing, both network legs, jitter buffers and playback; a turn can meet this SLO and take three seconds for the caller. The Pipecat primer's 1,500 ms target is measured voice to voice.

**P50 only.** The primer says P95 times matter. A daily P50 hides tool turns entirely if fewer than half the turns call a tool.

**Barge-in history.** Keeping the full reply in history tells the model the caller heard sentences that were cut off. The primer rebuilds the assistant message from word timestamps so it matches what the caller heard.

**The retry.** A 2-second timeout with one retry turns a slow response into a 2-second wait plus a new time to first token. If the first request was slow rather than dead, both responses can arrive; without cancelling the first, the caller hears two answers.

**Synchronous tools without filler.** A 600 ms median lookup plus a second inference is over a second of silence on every account question.

**No per-stage tracing.** Every item above is a guess until each stage is timestamped and joined by turn id. Deferring tracing to v4 means v3's dashboards cannot show which fix worked.

### Two defensible fixes for the compliance wait

**Clause streaming with a cumulative check (recommended).** Cost: a check per clause, on the critical path of every clause, and the risk that a clause already spoken turns out non-compliant once the next clause arrives. The cumulative check catches it one clause late, not before it is spoken.

**Keep the whole-reply check, cap reply length.** One short sentence per turn makes the full-reply wait small. Cost: answers to multi-part billing questions take several turns, and callers hear an agent that asks "anything else?" more often.

The conflict is the compliance guarantee against latency: only the second option keeps the doc's literal "nothing non-compliant is ever spoken", and it pays for it in conversation length.

## Follow-ups

- *Compliance refuses clause streaming because a spoken clause cannot be unsaid. What do you offer instead, and what do you accept in writing?*
- *What would you need to see in v3's traces to reverse the decision to replace the silence window?*
- *The team has two weeks before launch. Of your list, which two fixes ship, and which go into the risk note?*

## Common mistakes

- Calling the whole-reply wait wrong without asking what compliance requires.
- Accepting the 800 ms number as a standard.
- Missing that the SLO's start and end points exclude most of the turn.
- Fixing the retry by lengthening the timeout instead of cancelling the first request.
