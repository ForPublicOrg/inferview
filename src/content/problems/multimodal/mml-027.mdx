## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round">
"Design a text-to-video generation service. Users type a prompt and get a short clip. Cover queueing, how you allocate GPUs, safety filtering, and what one second of video costs us."
</Callout>

The situation, as a scenario:

- The research team hands you a model with Movie Gen's published shape: a 30B transformer, 48 layers, width 6,144, generating 16-second clips at 768 px and 16 fps, 50 sampling steps with guidance, then a 7B upsampler to 1080p.
- Infrastructure: a fixed reservation of 32 nodes of 8 H100s for the next year. No more capacity until the next budget cycle.
- Finance needs a cost per second of generated video for pricing, this month.
- Legal requires that clips depicting real, identifiable people are never delivered, and that every delivered clip carries provenance metadata.
- Product wants a paid tier and a free tier.

## Answer

<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Price one clip first: about 1.1 EFLOP of transformer compute at the published settings, which at a scenario 400 TFLOP/s per GPU is about 2,700 GPU-seconds, roughly 5.6 minutes on one 8-GPU node. The reservation then makes about 340 clips an hour at full use, so this is an asynchronous job service: submit, queue, notify. One clip per node with sequence parallelism inside the node; a priority queue with the paid tier first and the free tier capped; prompt safety before queueing because GPU time is the scarce thing, frame safety before delivery because the prompt check misses things. Previews at lower resolution and fewer steps cut wasted full renders. Cost per second of video at a scenario $2.50 per GPU-hour is about $0.12 before overheads.</p>

<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Price the clip.</strong> Tokens: 32 latent frames × 48 × 48 patches = 73,728. One forward pass: weights 2 × 30 × 10⁹ × 73,728 ≈ 4.4 PFLOP, attention 4 × 48 × 73,728² × 6,144 ≈ 6.4 PFLOP, total about 10.8 PFLOP. 50 steps × 2 guidance passes = 100 passes ≈ 1.08 EFLOP. At 400 TFLOP/s sustained per GPU (a scenario figure, about 40% of the H100's dense BF16; replace it with research's measurement), that is 1.08 × 10¹⁸ / 4 × 10¹⁴ ≈ 2,700 GPU-seconds, 0.75 GPU-hours. The upsampler and decoder are extra; measure them.</p>
<p><strong>Cost per second of video.</strong> At a scenario $2.50 per GPU-hour, 0.75 × $2.50 ≈ $1.88 per clip, $1.88 / 16 ≈ $0.12 per second, for the transformer only. Add the upsampler, failed and rejected clips (every clip safety blocks after rendering was paid for), and idle reservation. Finance gets a formula with these terms and the measured values, not a single number.</p>
<p><strong>GPU allocation.</strong> One clip needs about 2,700 GPU-seconds and its activations at 73,728 tokens are large; run each clip on one node with sequence or context parallelism over its 8 GPUs, keeping the heavy all-to-all traffic on the node's fast interconnect. That is about 2,700 / 8 ≈ 340 s per clip at perfect scaling, worse in practice. 32 nodes × 3,600 / 340 ≈ 340 clips per hour at full use.</p>
<p><strong>Queueing.</strong> A job API: submit returns a job id; status by poll or webhook; clips land in object storage with provenance metadata. Priority queue by tier; the free tier has a per-user daily quota and a share cap (for example, at most a third of nodes) so it cannot starve paid users. Admission control: when the paid queue's estimated wait passes the tier's promise, stop accepting free jobs rather than letting them wait hours. Cancellation is checked at step boundaries so an abandoned job frees its node within one step.</p>
<p><strong>Previews.</strong> Offer a cheap preview first: fewer frames or lower resolution, and fewer steps. A 4-second preview at the same resolution is 8 latent frames, 18,432 tokens; attention cost per pass falls 16 times and the weight term 4 times. If users discard many clips after seeing them, previews move the throw-away to the cheap render. Whether they do is hint 2's measurement.</p>
<p><strong>Safety.</strong> Prompt classification before the queue (seconds of CPU or a small model, against minutes of a node). Frame sampling and classification after the render, before delivery, including face matching against public figures for the real-people rule. Provenance metadata written at delivery. Blocked renders are logged with their cost; the block rate times clip cost is a line in finance's formula.</p>

### Two defensible allocation designs

**One clip per node, whole node for the whole job (recommended).** Simple scheduling, all parallel traffic inside the node, predictable per-clip time. Cost: a paid user waits the full five-plus minutes plus queueing; a node is held through the upsampler and decoder phases, which may not need 8 GPUs; head-of-line waits behind long jobs in each node's slot.

**Shared pool with step-level scheduling.** Split the job into phases (transformer steps, decode, upsample) and schedule each phase on whatever GPUs are free; the transformer steps can span two nodes for a paid user to halve latency, and the cheaper phases run on fewer GPUs. Cost: a scheduler that understands phases and checkpoints latents between them, cross-node communication for the two-node case, and a far harder system for a team to operate. This wins if latency for the paid tier is the product's selling point and the measured phase split shows a large share of time outside the transformer.

The conflict is paid-tier latency against total throughput on a reservation that cannot grow: spreading a clip over more GPUs cuts its wait and adds communication, which lowers clips per hour.

### Choose the numbers

- **Sustained throughput per GPU.** 300 to 500 TFLOP/s is a reasonable planning range against about 990 dense; above 700 assumes near-perfect kernels at this sequence length and should be distrusted until measured.
- **Free-tier share cap.** A quarter to a third of nodes. Zero kills the funnel; no cap means a viral day starves paying users.
- **Preview size.** Large enough to judge the content (a few seconds at the final resolution, or the full length at half resolution); a preview users cannot judge just adds a render.

### Written down: the rollout and the cost formula

Cost per delivered second = (transformer GPU-hours + upsampler and decode GPU-hours) × price per GPU-hour × (1 + safety-block rate + failure rate) / 16 / utilisation. Every term is a measured value with a date.

Rollout: paid tier invite-only for two weeks at a cap of 100 clips per hour; then free tier at a quarter-node share. Rollback triggers: any delivered clip with an identifiable real person; paid p95 wait above the promise for a day; measured cost per delivered second above finance's price floor for a week.

**What you give up:** interactive latency (this is a job service, not a request-response one), and some throughput to keep paid waits bounded.

### Where this fails

The recommended design runs the decisive safety check after the render. A prompt engineered to pass the text classifier but produce a blocked clip costs a full node for five minutes and delivers nothing; a free-tier user who learns that the block costs them nothing can burn capacity on purpose. Count blocked renders against the user's quota, and alert on users with a high block rate.

## Follow-ups

- *Your safety pipeline adds a second render pass for borderline clips and costs a fifth of capacity. Which half of it do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants previews at full length and half resolution, the other short clips at full resolution. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *What would you need to see in production to drop the free tier's share cap?*
- *Research ships a distilled model that needs 8 steps without guidance. Recompute the clips per hour, and say what in your design you would change first.*

## Common mistakes

- Designing a synchronous request-response API for a job that takes minutes per clip.
- Pricing from the weight term alone (2 × params × tokens), which misses the larger attention term at 73,728 tokens and understates cost by more than half.
- Splitting one clip across nodes by default, paying cross-node communication for a latency nobody priced.
- Running safety only after the render, so every blocked prompt costs a full render.
- Giving finance one number instead of a formula with measured terms.
