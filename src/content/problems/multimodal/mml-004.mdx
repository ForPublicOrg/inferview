## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Sales wants to say we support 1M-token contexts because the model's needle heatmap is all green. Build the eval that tells us what our product actually supports."
</Callout>

Scenario: the product answers questions over a customer's uploaded document set (contracts, amendments, correspondence) of 20K to 400K tokens. Typical questions: "Which amendments change the termination clause, and what does the latest one say?", "List every party that signed after 2024", "Is the indemnity cap in the master agreement still in force?". Two engineers, three weeks, and an inference budget for the eval that finance will set once you give them a figure. The sales lead disagrees with any number below 1M and will read your report.

Design the eval: task types, lengths, how items are built and scored, how many items, how results are reported and compared, and what you write down. Then check yourself against the list.

## Answer

**Task types, from the questions.** Each real question type maps to a probe family. "Which amendments change the termination clause" is aggregation over the set plus a hop to the latest one. "Every party that signed after 2024" is aggregation with a filter. "Is the indemnity cap still in force" is a multi-hop chain: the cap, then any amendment that changes it, then its effective date. Add a no-overlap retrieval family, where the question and the evidence share no key words ("Who pays if the warehouse floods?" against a clause about force majeure and property damage), because NoLiMa found that removing literal overlap is what breaks models that pass needles: at 32K, 11 of 13 models it tested fell below half their short-length baseline. Keep a single-needle family as a smoke test for broken configurations only.

**Items from real documents.** Build each item from a real document set (with permission) or a realistic synthetic set with the same structure: a question, the passages that answer it, the expected answer in a checkable form (a list of party names, a clause number, yes or no with the governing clause). Pad to each target length with other documents from the same customer type, not with essays, so the distractors look like the evidence.

**Lengths and depth.** Sweep the lengths production sends: say 32K, 64K, 128K, 256K and 400K from the upload distribution, plus 1M only if a customer has asked for it. Vary where the evidence sits (start, middle, end, and spread) and record it, since Lost in the Middle showed position alone moves accuracy.

**How many items.** Choose the cell size from the decision. If a 10-point drop between two lengths would change what sales may claim, the standard error of the difference between two independent cells at about 80% accuracy is √(2 × 0.8 × 0.2 / n): about 8 points at n = 50, 5.7 at n = 100 and 4 at n = 200. Only the last puts a 10-point drop at more than two standard errors. Building each length's items from the same questions and documents, padded to different lengths, makes the comparison paired and tightens it further. Then give finance the token cost: items × lengths × tokens per item. At 200 items per cell and the five lengths above, the prompt tokens alone are 200 × (32K + 64K + 128K + 256K + 400K) = 176 million per configuration.

**Scoring.** Deterministic checks wherever the answer has a form: set overlap for lists, exact match for clause numbers and dates. Where a model judge is needed, label 100 or more outputs by hand first and report the judge's agreement by class. Compare configurations paired on the same items.

**The report.** Score by length per task family, with an effective length per family: the longest tested length at which that family, and every shorter length, meets a threshold the product owner sets in advance. The supported length is the minimum over the families that match real questions. Needle results go in a separate table labelled as a configuration check.

**What you give up, in writing.** Three weeks and two people do not cover every document type. The note says which types are covered, that 1M is untested unless it was in the sweep, that the result holds for this model version and prompt, and that the eval re-runs on any model, prompt or retrieval change. To the sales lead it says what can be claimed today and what evidence would extend it.

**How this fails.** Items built by the team reflect the team's idea of hard questions. If production questions are mostly short lookups, the eval understates what the product supports; if they are mostly cross-document reasoning, the sampled families may still miss the hardest one. Refresh a share of items from production questions each quarter.

## Follow-ups

- Finance gives you a third of the 176 million tokens you asked for. Which lengths or families do you cut, and what claim do you drop because of it?
- The sales lead points to a competitor's 1M needle heatmap. What do you put in front of them that is not an argument about benchmarks?
- What result at 256K would make you recommend retrieval over whole-set prompting for that size, and who signs off on the change?

## Common mistakes

- Reporting the needle heatmap as a capability and the realistic probes as caveats.
- Padding with unrelated text, which makes distractors easy to ignore.
- One accuracy number averaged over lengths, which hides where the product stops working.
- Picking 20 items per cell because it is cheap, then reading noise as a trend.
- Running the eval once at launch with no trigger for re-running it.
