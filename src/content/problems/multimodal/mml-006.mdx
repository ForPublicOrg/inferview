## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Our vendor shipped this with their new model. Product wants to rewrite our pipeline around it and drop retrieval. Review it."
</Callout>

The launch note, as received (the vendor and model are invented; the method is assembled from public needle tests):

> **Perfect recall across 1M tokens.** Model X achieves 100% retrieval accuracy at every context length from 1K to 1M tokens and every document depth, as shown in the heatmap below. This means you can put entire codebases, contract libraries or a year of support tickets in one prompt and the model will use all of it.
>
> **Method.** We inserted the needle *"The magic number for the blue warehouse is 7041."* into a haystack of public essays, concatenated and repeated to reach each target length. We tested 10 context lengths (1K to 1M, log-spaced) and 10 depths (0% to 100%), one trial per cell. The prompt ended: *"Find the magic number for the blue warehouse in the text above."* A grader model scored each answer from 1 to 10 against the needle; 7 or above counts as a pass. Cells where the model refused or returned an empty answer were re-run, up to three times. All 100 cells passed.
>
> *[A 10 by 10 heatmap, every cell green.]*

List the flaws you would raise before anyone changes the pipeline, rank the two that matter most for the decision in front of you, and say what evidence would support dropping retrieval. Then open the checklist.

## Answer

**The two that matter most for dropping retrieval.**

1. *The test is literal matching.* The question and the needle share "magic number", "blue" and "warehouse". NoLiMa built the variant where they share nothing meaningful and found that, at 32K, 11 of the 13 models it tested fell below half their short-length score; GPT-4o went from 99.3% to 69.7%. A product question ("who pays if the warehouse floods?") rarely repeats the words of the clause that answers it.
2. *Retrieval of one sentence is not use of the context.* The note's second sentence claims the model will use all of a codebase or contract library. RULER found near-perfect needle scores alongside large drops on multi-hop tracing and aggregation, and HELMET states that synthetic tasks like the needle test do not reliably predict downstream performance. Dropping retrieval is a bet on those harder tasks, and the note has no evidence about them.

**The flaws a first read misses.**

- *The re-runs.* Re-running refused or empty cells up to three times turns a failure rate into a pass. The note does not say how many cells were re-run; ask.
- *The sample.* One trial per cell gives 10 trials in the 1M row. Zero failures in 10 bounds the true failure rate only below 1 − 0.05<sup>1/10</sup> ≈ 26% at 95% confidence; "100%" is a statement about ten prompts.
- *The haystack.* Repeated unrelated essays make the needle the only sentence about warehouses or numbers, so the distractors do no work. Real contract libraries are full of near-matches.
- *The grader.* A 1-to-10 grader with a pass at 7 is a lenient threshold with no reported agreement with human labels, for an answer that could be checked by exact match.

**Also worth raising.** No comparison with short-context accuracy or with the previous model, nothing about latency or cost for a 1M-token prompt, and a prompt that tells the model it is looking for a number.

**What is acceptable.** The sweep is a good configuration check. A model or deployment with broken position handling (a missing or wrong `rope_scaling` block, a truncating server) fails it immediately, and it is cheap. It is acceptable in the launch note if it is labelled as that, next to task results.

**What would support dropping retrieval.** Your questions, on your documents, at the sizes your customers upload, scored by length for each question type, compared paired against the current retrieval pipeline on the same items, with latency and cost per request. If whole-context prompting matches retrieval on the hardest question type at the sizes that matter, and the cost is acceptable, drop it for those sizes. If it matches only on lookups, keep retrieval.

**How this review fails.** It can read as "the vendor is lying", which loses the room. The vendor's test is honest about its method; the defect is the inference drawn from it. Real launches make the same pairing: Meta's Llama 4 post supports a 10M-token context for Scout with a retrieval needle-in-haystack result and a code likelihood curve. The review asks for the missing evidence, not for a retraction.

## Follow-ups

- The vendor sends a NoLiMa-style result at 128K showing 80% against 95% at 4K. Product says 80% is fine. What do you ask, and what do you sign?
- Your own probes cost 40 engineer-hours and the decision is due Friday. Which single probe do you run?
- What would you need to see in production after dropping retrieval for small document sets to reverse that decision?

## Common mistakes

- Rejecting the needle test outright instead of naming what it can and cannot show.
- Missing the re-runs, which inflate the result more than any other single choice in the method.
- Reading 100 green cells as 100 trials at every length.
- Arguing about the vendor's numbers instead of running your own questions at your lengths.
