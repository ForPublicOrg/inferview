## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The round">
"Our RL run's reward climbs the whole way, but the people rating it say the late checkpoints are worse. What is happening, and when should we have stopped?"
</Callout>

As a scenario: PPO on a 13B assistant from its SFT checkpoint against a pairwise reward model with 71% accuracy on held-out comparisons. Every 500 steps, a vendor's raters compare 200 responses from the checkpoint against the SFT model on fixed prompts. The KL coefficient is 0.01.

| step | proxy reward | KL from SFT (nats) | mean length (tokens) | human win rate vs SFT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 500 | 0.82 | 2.1 | 345 | 58% |
| 1,000 | 1.45 | 4.3 | 402 | 63% |
| 1,500 | 1.93 | 6.8 | 470 | 61% |
| 2,000 | 2.31 | 9.5 | 552 | 54% |
| 2,500 | 2.62 | 12.4 | 640 | 47% |

The SFT model's mean length is 310 tokens. The rating vendor changed at step 2,000; when it started, the new vendor re-rated the step-1,000 comparisons and gave 61%. In the reward model's training data, the chosen response is the longer one in 64% of pairs. Nobody has looked at transcripts.

Pick the explanation for the fall in human preference. Then rank the rest, say what you would collect, and propose a stopping rule for the next run, including how many comparisons per checkpoint you would buy.

## Answer

**The cause is over-optimisation of the proxy.** The proxy rises monotonically; the human win rate rises to 63% around KL 4.3 and then falls to 47% at KL 12.4. That is the shape Gao et al. measured with a gold reward model standing in for people: with d = √KL, the gold reward under RL follows d(α − β log d), rising and then falling, while the proxy keeps rising. Here d is 2.07 at step 1,000 and 3.52 at step 2,500. The run had no stopping rule tied to people, so it ran past the peak.

The length data suggests what the policy found. Mean length doubles from 310 to 640 tokens, and the reward model's data has the longer response chosen in 64% of pairs, so length is a feature the proxy rewards. That is a hypothesis until the length-controlled win rate and transcripts are in, and length need not be the only exploited feature.

**Why the others are tempting, and how they rank.**

1. *The rater switch* is a real defect in the measurement and ranks second. Changing vendors mid-series confounds the last two points. The calibration re-rating of step 1,000 at 61% against 63% is inside the noise (below) and says the new pool is not much harsher, so the switch cannot turn a peak in the low 60s into 47%. Re-rate step 2,500 with the old vendor's guidelines if a contract allows it.
2. *The KL coefficient.* A larger penalty would have made the run converge earlier, at lower KL, which looks like a fix. Gao et al. found that varying it did not move the gold-versus-KL frontier in their setup, so the penalty works as early stopping you do not control precisely. They flag the result as possibly sensitive to hyperparameters.
3. *Reward model accuracy.* 71% held-out accuracy leaves room for error, but the early gain from 50% to 63% shows the signal was real; a noise-only reward would not have moved people at all.

**The noise in the human signal, computed.** At 200 comparisons and a win rate near 60%, the standard error is √(0.6 × 0.4 / 200) = 3.5 points. The difference between two checkpoints rated on independent samples has a standard error of √2 × 3.5 = 4.9 points. So 63% against 61% is no evidence of a fall, 63% against 54% is suggestive, and 63% against 47% is clear. The peak lies somewhere between steps 1,000 and 1,500 and this data cannot place it more precisely.

**What to collect.** Length-controlled win rates per checkpoint; transcripts of the highest-reward step-2,500 responses; the reward model's score against length on the held-out comparisons; and, if possible, one checkpoint between 1,000 and 1,500 rated on a larger sample.

**The stopping rule: two defensible answers.**

- *A KL budget.* Stop the next run at the KL where this run's human score peaked, around 4 to 7 nats, and rate only the final checkpoint. Cost: almost no labelling. Risk: the peak moves when the reward model or data changes (Gao et al. found larger reward models peak higher and further out), so the budget is stale the moment the reward model is retrained.
- *Human-gated checkpoint selection.* Rate every checkpoint after the first and stop when the win rate has fallen by a set margin from its best. Cost: labelling at every checkpoint, and selecting the best of several noisy measurements overstates the winner. Risk: slower runs, and a decision that still depends on the sample size.

The quantity you choose is the sample size. To detect a 5-point fall between two checkpoints at about two standard errors, you need √(2 × 0.24 / n) ≤ 0.025, so n ≥ 768 comparisons per checkpoint. Reasonable choices are 500 to 1,000 per rated checkpoint with fewer rated checkpoints, or 200 per checkpoint with a fall threshold of about 10 points. Rating 200 and acting on a 2-point move is unreasonable.

The constraint conflict is labelling budget against how precisely you can find the peak. A combined rule fits the budget: a KL budget from this run as the hard stop, plus a 768-comparison rating of two candidate checkpoints near it, choosing between them.

**Failure mode of the recommendation.** The KL budget is calibrated on a run whose reward model has a length bias. If the next reward model is trained on length-balanced pairs, the policy can go further before the human score turns, and the old budget stops the run early and leaves quality on the table. Re-derive the budget after any reward model change.

## Follow-ups

- Rating 768 comparisons at two checkpoints costs as much as the RL compute for the run. Which half do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?
- What would you need to see on the next run to drop human gating and trust the KL budget alone?
- The research lead wants to fix the reward model by adding a length penalty to the reward; the data lead wants to retrain it on length-balanced pairs. How do you decide without deciding by rank?
- Transcripts show the step-2,500 responses add a closing paragraph that restates the answer. Is that reward hacking or a preference people hold that the raters' guidelines suppress? What would settle it?

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the checkpoint with the highest proxy reward.
- Reading 63% against 61% as the start of a decline, at 200 comparisons per checkpoint.
- Treating the KL penalty as the cure, when in Gao et al.'s setup it set the stopping point and did not move the frontier.
- Blaming the rater switch without checking the calibration re-rating.
