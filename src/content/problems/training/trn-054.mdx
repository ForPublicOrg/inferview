## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The round">
"In GRPO, what does a group contribute when every sample in it is correct? Our run is at 80% training accuracy and steps are getting less useful. What would you change?"
</Callout>

As a scenario: GRPO with a binary verifier, G = 8, 64 prompts per step. At the current checkpoint the prompts fall roughly into three bands by pass rate: 40% of prompts near 0.9, 40% near 0.5 and 20% near 0.1. The loss is a token mean over the batch, and the KL coefficient is 0. Say what the all-correct group contributes, how much of each step that is, and what you would do, including any number you choose.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what the loss divides by, and whether tokens with zero advantage are counted in it.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask whether the KL coefficient is zero in this run.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask how much a step's generation costs compared with its gradient update.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Nothing useful. Every reward equals the group mean, so every advantage is zero and the policy-gradient term is zero; with a KL term the group would pull the policy toward the reference and nothing else. It still costs eight generations, and under a token-mean loss its tokens still sit in the denominator, so it shrinks the gradient from the groups that do carry signal. With the pass rates given, about a quarter of each step's groups are like that, and the share grows as the policy improves. DAPO's answer is dynamic sampling: keep drawing groups and keep only those whose accuracy is strictly between 0 and 1 until the batch is full. That costs about 1.35 times the generation here, and the cost rises steeply as training accuracy approaches 1.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Why it is zero.</strong> The GRPO advantage is the reward minus the group mean, divided by the group standard deviation. Eight correct samples have reward 1, mean 1 and a std of 0, so the numerator is 0 and an implementation that adds a small epsilon to the std returns exactly 0. An all-wrong group is the same. DAPO names both and notes that the count of such prompts grows during training.</p>
<p><strong>How much of the step it is, computed.</strong> With G = 8, a prompt with pass rate p gives an all-correct group with probability p<sup>8</sup> and an all-wrong group with probability (1 - p)<sup>8</sup>. For p = 0.9 that is 0.9<sup>8</sup> = 0.43; for p = 0.1 the all-wrong case is also 0.43; for p = 0.5 each is 0.0039. The expected informative share is 0.4 × 0.57 + 0.4 × 0.992 + 0.2 × 0.57 = 0.739. So 26% of the groups, and of the generation spent on them, carry no gradient. Under a token-mean loss their tokens also dilute the step: the effective learning rate on the informative groups falls by roughly the same share, and it falls further each time the policy improves.</p>
<p><strong>What to do.</strong> Three options, each with a cost.</p>
<ul>
<li><em>Dynamic sampling (DAPO).</em> Over-sample and keep only informative groups until the batch is full. Here that is 1 / 0.739 = 1.35 times the groups per step. Cost: more generation, which the OpenRLHF authors put at over 90% of RLHF runtime, and a variable step time. At a uniform pass rate of 0.95 the informative share is 1 - 0.95<sup>8</sup> = 0.34 (the all-wrong term is negligible), so the multiplier is 2.97.</li>
<li><em>Filter prompts by recent pass rate.</em> Track each prompt's pass rate across steps and stop drawing prompts that have been solved every time for several visits. Cost: estimates go stale, and a prompt that regresses is not seen until it is re-tested.</li>
<li><em>Raise G for the prompts near the edges.</em> With G = 16 a prompt at 0.9 is all-correct with probability 0.9<sup>16</sup> = 0.19 instead of 0.43. Cost: twice the generation on those prompts, and the extra samples mostly confirm what the group already shows.</li>
</ul>
<p>The number to choose is the generation budget per step, or equivalently the cap on how many groups dynamic sampling may draw before it trains on a partial batch. A cap of about 1.5 to 2 times the batch is reasonable at this accuracy; an unbounded loop is not, because on a nearly solved prompt set it never returns.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li>Your cap on resampling is hit on most steps late in training. Do you raise the cap, add harder prompts, or stop the run, and what tells you which?</li>
<li>What would you need to see to keep the all-correct groups in the batch on purpose?</li>
<li>How does the answer change if the KL coefficient is 0.04, as in DeepSeekMath?</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

My recommendation for this run is dynamic sampling with a cap of twice the batch, plus a refresh of the prompt pool with harder prompts once the cap is hit on most steps. Its failure mode is that it hides progress: the batch is always made of frontier prompts, so the training reward stays near 0.5 by construction and stops being a measure of anything. Track accuracy on a fixed probe set, and the share of drawn groups that were discarded, which is the real measure of how solved the prompt pool is.

## Follow-ups

- The generation fleet is already the bottleneck and dynamic sampling adds 35% to it. Do you cut G, cut prompts per step, or accept a smaller effective batch, and what does each cost in signal?
- A colleague argues the all-correct groups are useful because they stop the model forgetting what it can do. What does the gradient say, and what measurement would settle it?
- The prompt pool is fixed by a licence and cannot grow. What do you do when most prompts are solved?

## Common mistakes

- Saying the all-correct group reinforces the correct answers; its advantage is zero, so it reinforces nothing.
- Forgetting that a token-mean loss still counts zero-advantage tokens in the denominator.
- Resampling without a budget.
- Reading the training reward after dynamic sampling as a measure of progress.
