## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Walk me through how raw web text becomes the training set our next base model reads: sources, filtering, dedup, mixing and how you know each step helped. Then: after filtering there are too few high-quality tokens, and the first checkpoint regresses in a language our users write in while English benchmarks go up. What do you change?"
</Callout>

Scenario inputs, all labelled as scenario:

- The main run is a 70B dense model on 15T tokens. The data freeze is in eight weeks; after it the mix cannot change.
- The data team is four engineers. The crawl pipeline (extraction, URL dedup, language ID) exists and cannot be rewritten before the freeze; everything after language ID is open.
- Ablation compute is capped at 1% of the main run.
- The product ships in English, Hindi and Indonesian. Product leadership wants the general bucket as clean as possible; the regional teams want their languages to keep their share.
- After language ID and dedup, the English web pool is 40T tokens. The quality classifier you inherit keeps the top 10%.

Design the pipeline after language ID, the evaluation that decides each stage, and the first response to both follow-ups. Write the decision memo for the filter threshold.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>After language ID: heuristic filters, MinHash dedup within each language, line-level boilerplate removal, a model-based quality ranking applied per language, decontamination, then a domain mix chosen by small-model experiments. Each stage is decided by a pair of small ablation runs, not by reading samples. The 1% budget buys about a hundred two-seed ablations at FineWeb's scale. Keeping the top 10% of English gives 4T against the 7.5T a 50% general share needs, which is 1.9 epochs, inside the range Muennighoff et al. found harmless. For the language regression, the first thing to pull is token counts per language after each stage, because a ranking that ran across languages is the usual cause.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Stages, in order.</b> Heuristic filters first, because they are cheap and remove what a classifier would score badly anyway: Llama 3's duplicated n-gram coverage for logs and error pages, dirty-word counts for adult sites missing from block lists, and a token-distribution KL check for outlier tokens. Then MinHash near-dedup within each language, as Llama 3 does, and per snapshot, which FineWeb found better than global dedup across its 96 snapshots. Then line-level removal of boilerplate (Llama 3 drops lines seen more than 6 times in each 30M-document bucket). Then the quality classifier, then decontamination against every eval set the team reports, then the mix.</p>
<p><b>Ablation budget, computed.</b> FineWeb's ablation unit is a 1.71B model on 28B tokens: 6 × 1.71e9 × 28e9 = 2.9e20 FLOPs, 5.7e20 with two seeds. The main run is 6 × 70e9 × 15e12 = 6.3e24. One two-seed ablation is 9.1e-5 of the main run, so 1% buys about 110. That covers every stage and threshold in English and a handful per other language, not a grid over everything.</p>
<p><b>Mix.</b> Llama 3 reports roughly 50% general, 25% maths and reasoning, 17% code and 8% multilingual, chosen with small models trained on candidate mixes. At 15T, 50% general is 7.5T; the filtered English pool at top 10% is 4T, so the general bucket repeats 1.9 times. The multilingual bucket is 8% of 15T, 1.2T, shared by every language that is not English.</p>
<p><b>Too few tokens.</b> In order of cost: relax the threshold where ablations show the next decile is close to the current one; repeat, which is cheap up to about 4 epochs by Muennighoff et al.; rephrase the best sources with a model, which costs generation compute and fidelity checks; synthesise last. Problem trn-035 works the costs.</p>
<p><b>The language regression.</b> Pull token counts per language after each stage for the old and new pipeline. A drop concentrated at the quality stage points at an English-trained classifier or a global top-k; a drop at language ID points at a threshold that removes code-mixed or romanised text. The user-facing eval per language goes into the gate for every later change. Problem trn-037 works one such case.</p>
</Fragment>

<ul>
<li>Your plan spends most of the 110 ablations in English and leaves Indonesian with three. The Indonesian lead says that guarantees another regression. Which English ablations do you cut, and what do you accept in writing?</li>
<li>The first ablations show the model-based classifier beats heuristics alone by less than the seed-to-seed spread. Do you keep it for the main run?</li>
<li>What would you need to see after the freeze to reverse per-language ranking and go back to one global ranking?</li>
</ul>

</Depth>

**The decision with two defensible answers: where the quality ranking applies.**

*Per-language ranking with a multilingual classifier*, as Llama 3 does with its Llama 2-based multilingual ranker. Each language keeps its own top fraction, so Hindi and Indonesian keep tokens in proportion to their pools. It costs a classifier trained and calibrated per language, ablations in each language, and a judgement about what "top 10%" means in a language whose web is smaller and noisier.

*One English-trained classifier with a global ranking, plus a separate multilingual bucket filtered by heuristics only.* Cheaper to build inside eight weeks, and the English bucket gets the best classifier the team has. It costs unfiltered quality in the multilingual bucket, and any score computed across languages can rank non-English text low for being non-English rather than for being poor.

Under this scenario the first is the right call because two non-English languages are product requirements; the second is defensible for an English-first model where the multilingual bucket is a hedge.

**The quantity you choose and defend: the English keep fraction.** Top 10% gives 4T and 1.9 epochs of general data. Top 20% gives 8T and less than one epoch. Either is reasonable if an ablation pair shows the second decile within the seed spread of the first. Keeping the top 5% (2T, 3.75 epochs) is unreasonable without evidence: it sits at the edge of the range where repetition was found harmless and leaves no slack for the maths and code buckets, which will also want to repeat.

**The constraint conflict.** Quality against token count, and English benchmark gains against coverage of the languages the product sells. Product leadership's "as clean as possible" and the regional teams' "keep our share" cannot both be met with one threshold.

**What you give up.** Per-language ranking gives up some English cleanliness for the same eight weeks of engineering, and accepts a multilingual classifier that is less validated than the English one.

**How the recommended answer fails.** Per-language top fractions keep the same share of a small, noisy pool as of a large, clean one, so kept Hindi text can be worse in absolute terms than discarded English text. Per-language ablations at 1.71B may also sit below the scale where those languages register on any benchmark, so the gate passes on noise. Mitigate with per-language perplexity on a held-out set written by the product's users, and a floor on absolute classifier score as well as a top fraction.

**The memo.**

> Decision: rank quality within each language with the multilingual classifier; English keeps the top 10% (4T tokens, 1.9 epochs at a 50% general share) unless the second-decile ablation is within the seed spread, in which case the top 20%.
> Evidence: two-seed ablations at 1.71B on 28B tokens, about 110 within the 1% budget; per-language held-out perplexity on text written by Hindi and Indonesian users.
> We give up: the cleanest possible English bucket; a fully validated multilingual classifier.
> Gates and rollback: any stage change that worsens Hindi or Indonesian held-out perplexity beyond the seed spread is reverted before the freeze, whatever it does to English benchmarks.
> Owners: the data lead signs the threshold; the regional leads sign the per-language gate.

## Follow-ups

- *The freeze moves two weeks earlier and you can run 40 of your 110 ablations. Which stages ship on published evidence alone, and which risk do you write down?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree on per-snapshot versus global dedup, one citing FineWeb and one citing Llama 3. How do you decide without deciding by rank, inside the ablation budget?*
- *The first main-run checkpoint shows Hindi held-out perplexity worse than the previous model and English benchmarks better. What would you need to see to stop the run rather than correct it in mid-training?*

## Common mistakes

- Describing stages without the evaluation that decides each one.
- Picking a keep fraction without computing the epochs it implies at the target budget.
- Running one quality ranking across languages with a classifier trained on English references.
- Treating a 1.71B ablation result smaller than the seed spread as a signal.
- Leaving the user-facing languages out of the gate and finding the regression at the first main-run checkpoint.
