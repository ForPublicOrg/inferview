## Question

Your content-moderation service scores each message with an ensemble of three classifiers: three fine-tunes of the same 3B base, trained with different seeds and learning rates on the same labelled data. A message is escalated to human review when the ensemble's mean probability of "violating" is above 0.7. The ensemble costs three forward passes per message, and the infrastructure lead proposes replacing it with one model whose weights are the average of the three, cutting serving cost by two thirds.

Explain what the average requires to work, how it can fail here specifically, and what you would measure before agreeing. Choose the acceptance margin you would hold the averaged model to, and defend it.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask for the learning rate and base checkpoint of each fine-tune.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask what the 0.7 threshold was tuned on, and what the review queue's staffing assumes about the escalation rate.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A weight average only works when the three fine-tunes lie in one low-loss region, which needs a shared initialisation and moderate learning rates; here the base is shared, so it may work. The specific risk is the threshold: ensembles improve calibration and soups do not, so even with equal accuracy the averaged model's probabilities shift and the 0.7 cut sends a different volume to review. I would require accuracy within a margin of the best single model and recalibrate the threshold to hold the escalation rate.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>An ensemble and a soup look alike and are different objects. An ensemble runs every model and averages outputs, so it works for any three models. A soup runs one model at the average of their weights, and that point is only good if the loss stays low along the lines between them. Wortsman et al. found fine-tunes of one pretrained model often share such a basin, and found error barriers mainly when fine-tuning used high learning rates. So the first measurement is cheap: the learning rates, then the held-out accuracy of the uniform average against each ingredient. If one ingredient used a much higher learning rate, a greedy soup that adds models only when held-out accuracy does not drop is the better recipe; the paper's uniform soup on CLIP ViT-B/32 scored 79.97 against 80.38 for the best single model, and the greedy soup 81.03.</p>
<p>The failure specific to this service is calibration. The paper reports that ensembles improve calibration and soups do not, and this service consumes a probability, not a label. A soup with the same accuracy can push more messages above 0.7 or fewer, which changes the review queue's volume and the miss rate on violations, neither of which an accuracy number shows.</p>
<p>Before agreeing: accuracy, precision and recall at the operating point against the ensemble on a held-out set the soup was not selected on; the escalation rate at 0.7; and a re-tuned threshold (or temperature scaling on the soup's logits) that restores the escalation rate, with recall at that threshold reported.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li>The soup matches the ensemble on accuracy but needs a threshold of 0.62 to hold the escalation rate, and recall on the rarest violation class drops. Do you ship?</li>
<li>One of the three fine-tunes is retrained next month on new labels. What happens to the soup, and to the threshold?</li>
<li>Why would averaging a fine-tune of this base with a fine-tune of the base's next release fail, even with identical architecture?</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

**The margin, chosen.** I would accept the soup if recall on violations at the recalibrated threshold is no more than one point below the ensemble's on a held-out set of at least a few thousand labelled messages, and the escalation rate stays within the review team's staffed capacity. A tighter margin is reasonable for a service with legal exposure; a looser one is not, because the ensemble's cost is known and a missed violation is not. The margin should be agreed with the trust-and-safety owner before the numbers come in, not after.

**Where the recommended answer fails.** Recalibrating the threshold on today's held-out set fixes today's distribution, and moderation content shifts. The soups paper's shift evidence is about accuracy, where soups held up (on CLIP ViT-B/32 the uniform soup averaged 51.45 across its distribution-shift sets against 50.77 for the ensemble), not about calibration, so it does not tell you whether the recalibrated threshold survives the next shift. The soup also gives up a signal the ensemble had and this service never used: disagreement between members. The monitoring plan therefore needs the escalation rate and the reviewer overturn rate as alerts, with the ensemble kept deployable for rollback.

**The trade in one line.** Two thirds of the serving cost against calibration you must now maintain by hand.

## Follow-ups

- Could you keep the ensemble's calibration and cut cost by distilling the ensemble into one model instead? What does that cost, and what does it need from the ensemble?
- The infrastructure lead says the review team can absorb a ten percent increase in escalations. Does that change your margin?

## Common mistakes

- Treating weight averaging as a cheap ensemble that works for any set of models.
- Comparing accuracy only, when the service consumes a probability at a fixed threshold.
- Selecting the soup and reporting its score on the same held-out set.
