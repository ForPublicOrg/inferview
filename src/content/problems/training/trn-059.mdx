## Question

Published inputs (Qwen3 report, Table 21, May 2025): starting from the same off-policy-distilled Qwen3-8B checkpoint, RL reached 67.6 on AIME'24 for 17,920 GPU hours, and on-policy distillation from a Qwen3-32B or Qwen3-235B-A22B teacher reached 74.4 for 1,800 GPU hours. The report does not say whether the 1,800 include serving the teacher.

Scenario: your team wants to repeat the comparison for its own 8B student. You would host the teacher yourself, Qwen3-235B-A22B (22B active parameters), on the same GPU type as training. Your RL run would need about as many steps as Qwen3's.

Estimate the ratio of RL GPU hours to on-policy distillation GPU hours for your team, as a single number. Use the rule of thumb that a forward pass costs about 2 × (active parameters) FLOPs per token and a training step about 6 × (parameters) per token, and state every assumption you choose, in particular what the 1,800 hours contain and whether teacher work costs GPU time in proportion to its FLOPs.

Then say what about your own task would change the comparison more than this arithmetic does.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Between 5 and 10, and 6 to 7 as my estimate. If the 1,800 hours include teacher scoring, the ratio is 17,920 / 1,800 = 10. If they exclude it, the teacher adds roughly 44 FLOPs for every 64 the student spends per token, so distillation costs about 3,000 hours by FLOPs and the ratio is about 6; because teacher scoring is prefill, which uses the GPU better than the student's decode, the true figure sits between 6 and 10. The dominant term in both runs is student sampling, and the assumption that flips the conclusion is whether a teacher already exists.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Per sampled token in on-policy distillation, the student generates it (a forward pass, 2 × 8B = 16 GFLOPs) and trains on it (6 × 8B = 48 GFLOPs), 64 GFLOPs in all. The teacher scores it in one forward pass over the finished sequence: 2 × 22B = 44 GFLOPs using active parameters, since an MoE token only runs its routed experts. The teacher adds 44 / 64 = 0.69 of the student's work.</p>
<p>Case A, the 1,800 hours already include the teacher: ratio 17,920 / 1,800 = 10.0. Case B, they exclude it and GPU time follows FLOPs: 1,800 × 1.69 = 3,040 hours, ratio 17,920 / 3,040 = 5.9. Case C, they exclude it and teacher prefill runs at about twice the utilisation of the student's decode-heavy work, which is the direction Thinking Machines describes when its saving goes from 9x in FLOPs to about 18x in GPU hours: 1,800 × (1 + 0.69 / 2) = 2,420 hours, ratio 7.4. I would quote 6 to 7 and say that the report does not let anyone narrow it further.</p>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

**The dominant term.** In both RL and on-policy distillation the student samples its own trajectories, and long reasoning traces make sampling, which is decode, the largest cost per step. Distillation is cheaper because it needs far fewer steps for the same gain, not because each step is cheaper; each distillation step is more expensive than an RL step by the teacher's scoring.

**Reasonable and unreasonable choices.** Any answer from about 5 (treating teacher work as costly as its FLOPs and adding a margin for teacher memory) to 10 (the 1,800 include everything) is defensible if the assumption is stated. Using the teacher's total parameters, 2 × 235B per token, gives a teacher cost of 470 / 64 = 7.3 times the student's and a ratio of 17,920 / (1,800 × 8.3) = 1.2; that is wrong for FLOPs, because an MoE token does not touch inactive experts. It is not wrong for memory: hosting 235B parameters takes several GPUs whatever the token rate, and if those GPUs idle between scoring batches their hours count. A team that cannot keep the teacher busy should estimate from reserved GPU hours, not FLOPs, and that can push the ratio toward the low end of the band.

**What changes the comparison more than the arithmetic.**

- *No teacher exists.* Both columns assume a strong model is already trained. If your task has no model better than your student, the distillation column must include building the teacher, and RL on the student may be the cheaper path.
- *Tokenizers differ.* Per-token scoring needs the teacher's log-probability of each student token. A teacher with another tokenizer cannot score the student's tokens directly, and the on-policy option disappears.
- *You need to beat the teacher.* Distillation's target is the teacher; RL against a verifier can exceed it. The cheaper method is irrelevant if the goal is above the ceiling.
- *Your task is narrow.* The 17,920 hours are for competition maths and code from a general checkpoint. A narrow task with a cheap verifier may need far fewer RL steps.

**How this estimate fails.** It assumes the RL run's step count transfers from Qwen3 to your task and that distillation reaches the same gain in proportionally few steps. Neither is published for any task other than the report's benchmarks. The estimate is a planning number; the decision should be made after a pilot of both methods on a few hundred steps, comparing gain per GPU hour on your own evaluation.

## Follow-ups

- The pilot shows distillation reaches 90% of the teacher's score at a seventh of RL's hours, but RL at full budget beats the teacher by two points on your verifier. Which do you ship, and what do you tell the team that asked for "best possible"?
- Your inference team can lend the teacher's GPUs only at night. How does that change the hours, the calendar time and the ratio you report?
- What would you need to see in the pilot to abandon distillation for this task?

## Common mistakes

- Treating 17,920 / 1,800 = 10 as the ratio for any team, without asking what the 1,800 contain.
- Costing the MoE teacher by total parameters for FLOPs.
- Forgetting that both runs start from a checkpoint already distilled from a strong teacher.
