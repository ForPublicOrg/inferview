## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"On an H100 SXM, how many tokens does a BF16 matmul need in its batch dimension before it stops being limited by memory bandwidth? Show the arithmetic."
</Callout>

The matmul multiplies activations X of shape B × D by weights W of shape D × F, both BF16, writing a B × F output. Choose the peak throughput and bandwidth you will use, and choose D and F (or argue that they do not matter). Enter the B at which the matmul's time on the arithmetic units equals its time moving bytes from HBM.

Then answer two short questions in words: does the threshold change if the matmul runs in FP8 instead of BF16, and in which part of a mixture-of-experts training step would you expect matmuls to fall below it?

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>About 300 tokens. The matmul does 2BDF FLOPs and moves 2(BD + DF + BF) bytes, so its intensity is BDF / (BD + DF + BF), which is about B when B is much smaller than D and F. The H100 SXM's dense BF16 rate over its bandwidth is 9.89 × 10<sup>14</sup> / 3.35 × 10<sup>12</sup> ≈ 295 FLOPs per byte, so B must exceed about 295. With D = F = 8,192 the exact crossover is 318; with D = F = 4,096 it is 345. FP8 leaves the threshold where it is, and MoE expert matmuls are where training falls below it.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Intensity.</b> FLOPs: 2BDF. Bytes at 2 per element: 2BD for X, 2DF for W, 2BF for the output. Intensity = BDF / (BD + DF + BF). For B much smaller than D and F the weight term DF dominates the bytes, and the intensity reduces to about B.</p>
<p><b>The machine's ratio.</b> The product page lists 1,979 BF16 TFLOPS with the footnote "With sparsity". Dense is half, 989.5 TFLOPS. With 3.35 TB/s of HBM bandwidth the ratio is 9.89 × 10<sup>14</sup> / 3.35 × 10<sup>12</sup> ≈ 295 FLOPs per byte, the figure the scaling book uses.</p>
<p><b>The crossover with finite D and F.</b> Setting BDF / (BD + DF + BF) = 295 with D = F gives BD / (2B + D) = 295, so B = 295D / (D − 590). At D = 8,192: 295 × 8,192 / 7,602 ≈ 318. At D = 4,096: 295 × 4,096 / 3,506 ≈ 345. The smaller the layer, the larger the batch must be, because the activation and output bytes are no longer negligible against the weight.</p>
<p><b>The dominant term and what flips the conclusion.</b> Below the threshold, the weight read dominates: every token in the batch shares the same DF bytes, and B is how many times they are reused. In FP8 the tensor-core rate doubles (3,958 TFLOPS with sparsity, so 1,979 dense) and each element is one byte, so the intensity becomes 2BDF / (BD + DF + BF) ≈ 2B against a ratio of 1,979 / 3.35 ≈ 591: B must still exceed about 295. What flips the conclusion for a training step is the number of tokens a matmul sees. Dense projections with micro-batches of thousands of tokens are far above it. A mixture-of-experts layer splits the micro-batch: as a scenario, 4,096 tokens routed top-2 across 64 experts gives each expert 4,096 × 2 / 64 = 128 tokens on average, below 295, so the expert matmuls are bandwidth-bound unless experts are batched across micro-batches or sharded so each sees more tokens.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li>Your MoE layer's experts see 128 tokens each. Do you raise the micro-batch, reduce the number of experts per GPU, or accept bandwidth-bound experts, and what does each cost?</li>
<li>The same matmul on an H100 NVL (835.5 dense BF16 TFLOPS, 3.9 TB/s): does the threshold go up or down, and by how much?</li>
<li>Why does the threshold not depend on D and F once they are large, and when does that stop being true?</li>
</ul>

Any answer from 280 to 360 tokens is accepted: 295 from the dense ratio with D and F large, 318 or 345 from exact crossovers at common hidden sizes, or 333 from the preliminary 1,000 TFLOPS and 3,000 GB/s in NVIDIA's architecture blog.

### Which answers are unreasonable

- About 590: the sparse 1,979 TFLOPS divided by 3.35 TB/s. Dense training matmuls do not get the 2:4 sparsity speedup, so this doubles the threshold.
- About 240: the TPU v5e's ratio from the scaling book, not the H100's.
- A batch of 1 or a handful: counting sequences instead of tokens. In a training matmul, B is sequences times sequence length.

## Follow-ups

- A teammate reports a 512-token matmul at 60% of peak and concludes the kernel is poor. What else would you check before agreeing?
- How does the backward pass's weight-gradient matmul, Xᵀ dY, compare in intensity with the forward matmul it belongs to?

## Common mistakes

- Using the spec-sheet BF16 figure without reading its sparsity footnote.
- Counting 4 bytes per element for a BF16 matmul, which halves every intensity.
- Treating the threshold as a property of the GPU alone. It also depends on the dtype's bytes per element relative to its FLOP rate, and on how many tokens each matmul actually sees.
