## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="As asked">
"We serve a 13B model we pretrained ourselves, on a schedule that decayed to its minimum. Legal has 40B tokens of contracts, filings and case text, and wants the model to know that domain. Re-training from scratch on everything is not in the budget; we can afford roughly 60% of the domain corpus's token count again in extra compute. The model's general behaviour is relied on by six other product teams, and the run must finish in six weeks. Design the continued-pretraining run."
</Callout>

Produce: the schedule, the data mix, the forgetting checks, the stop rule, and a one-page run plan with rollback criteria. Choose and defend the replay share and the re-warmed maximum learning rate.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether the original pretraining mix is still available and at what token count.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask the six product teams which behaviours they would notice first if they regressed, and whether an eval exists for each.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Re-warm over about 1% of steps to a maximum below the original pretraining maximum, decay with a schedule whose decay phase can start on demand, and replay the original pretraining mix at 25% of each batch. The budget's reading sets the size: if it means 64B tokens in total, all 40B domain tokens at 25% replay (53B) fit; if it means 24B, the run carries 18B domain tokens and I choose which. Gate on held-out loss on the original mix and on the six teams' evals, with a written regression budget per eval; stop and decay when domain loss flattens or any budget is breached. What I give up is part of the domain corpus and some domain gain, in exchange for a bound on regressions others depend on.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Read the budget first.</strong> "60% of the corpus again" is ambiguous, and the reading changes the design. If the total is 1.6 x 40B = 64B tokens, a 25% replay share over all 40B domain tokens is 53.3B, with 10B left for pilots and a restart. If the extra is only 24B tokens, then at 25% replay the run can carry 18B domain tokens, under half the corpus, and the choice is which half: deduplicated, most recent, or the document types legal ranks highest. I would get the reading in writing before choosing.</p>
<p><strong>Replay share: two defensible answers.</strong> 5% replay spends almost the whole budget on domain tokens and bets that legal English is a weak shift from the original mix; Ibrahim et al. found 5% enough for an English-to-English shift at 405M and 10B. 25% replay was what the strong English-to-German shift needed, and spends a quarter of the budget protecting behaviour. Contract and case text is the same language with a very different distribution; I would pilot both on about 2B tokens each and, without a clear pilot result, run at 25%, because six teams depend on the general behaviour and the cost of a regression lands on them, not on the legal team.</p>
<p><strong>Schedule.</strong> The current checkpoint is at its minimum rate, so the run must re-warm to adapt, and re-warming alone raises loss on the original data even when the data does not change. A lower re-warmed maximum forgets less and adapts less; pilots at half and at the full original maximum pick the point. A cosine decay ties the schedule to a token count fixed before the run, which conflicts with a stop rule; I would hold a constant rate after warmup and run a decay phase when the stop rule fires or the budget nears its end.</p>
<p><strong>Checks and the stop rule.</strong> Every few billion tokens: held-out loss on the original mix, held-out loss on a legal set the run never trains on, and the six teams' evals. Stop and decay when legal loss improves less per billion tokens than the pilots predicted, or when any team's eval regresses past its written budget after the decay phase. Loss on the original mix is expected to rise while the rate is high, and the decay recovers part of it, so a mid-run read overstates the final regression. To judge a budget mid-run, branch a short decay from the current checkpoint and evaluate the branch; a breach on the branch triggers a restart with a lower maximum or more replay.</p>
<p><strong>The written artefact.</strong> A run plan listing replay share and source, the maximum rate and warmup, the decay trigger, per-team regression budgets with an owner each, the rollback rule (the deployed checkpoint stays in production until every budget passes), and the six-week timeline with pilot results due at week one.</p>
<p><strong>How this fails.</strong> The six teams' evals cover what they test. Ibrahim et al. saw the 5% replay model trail the union baseline on MMLU while matching on average, and an average can hide that kind of loss in a capability nobody evaluates. The plan's defence is per-team evals, not one average; its gap is any behaviour without an eval.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *The original pretraining mix was deleted for licensing reasons. What do you replay, and how do you check that it protects what the original did?*
- *Halfway through, legal adds 10B tokens of newer filings. Do you extend this run or plan a second continuation, given re-warming forgets even on unchanged data?*
- *The deployed model was instruction-tuned after pretraining. Which checkpoint do you continue from, and what do you redo afterwards?*

## Common mistakes

- Continuing at the checkpoint's final, minimum learning rate, which adapts little to a real shift.
- Re-warming to the original maximum without replay and treating the forgetting as the data's fault.
- A cosine schedule sized to the full corpus combined with an early stop rule, which stops mid-decay.
- One averaged general benchmark as the only forgetting gate.
- Reading a regression during warm-up as final and stopping the run, or ignoring it and continuing.
