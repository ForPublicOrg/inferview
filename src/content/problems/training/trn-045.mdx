## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Explain DPO and how it differs from PPO-based RLHF. Why does it not need a reward model, and what does it give up? Then: you have an SFT model and a budget for preference data. Specify the DPO dataset: the fields of an example, where chosen and rejected responses come from, how you handle ties and noisy labels, and what you filter."
</Callout>

As a scenario: the SFT model is a 7B assistant, the goal is better instruction following, and you can pay for 40,000 human comparisons or generate pairs and label them with a judge model. Choose how many pairs come from each source and defend the split.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what the compute budget is for generation, separately from training.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask for the agreement rate between two annotators on the same pairs, and between the judge and the annotators.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask how different the current model's outputs are from those of the model that produced any existing preference data.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>PPO-based RLHF fits a reward model, then samples from the policy, scores with the reward model, estimates advantages with a value model and updates under a KL penalty. DPO uses the closed-form optimum of that KL-constrained objective to rewrite the reward as β log(π/π_ref) plus a per-prompt constant that cancels in a pairwise comparison, so it trains the policy directly with a logistic loss on preference pairs. It needs a frozen reference and fixed pairs, with no reward model, no value model and no sampling during training. It gives up on-policy exploration and an explicit reward you can inspect and reuse. The dataset: prompts rendered through the chat template, two responses sampled from the current SFT model (and a stronger model for some), labelled with strength; drop ties and weak preferences, balance length, filter near-identical pairs, and cache reference log-probs.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The objective.</strong> Maximise expected reward minus β times KL from the reference. The optimum is π* ∝ π_ref · exp(r/β), so r = β log(π*/π_ref) + β log Z(x). In the Bradley-Terry likelihood only reward differences appear, Z cancels, and the loss is −log σ(β[(log π(y_w) − log π_ref(y_w)) − (log π(y_l) − log π_ref(y_l))]). The gradient weights each pair by σ of how wrong the implicit reward currently is.</p>
<p><strong>What each costs.</strong> PPO holds a policy, a reference, a reward model and a value model, and generates during training; it can explore and keeps improving against a fixed reward model until it over-optimises it. DPO holds a policy and a reference (whose log-probs can be precomputed), never generates, and is as good as its pairs: it cannot learn about responses nobody sampled. Llama 3 chose DPO because it needed less compute for large models and did better on instruction following in their experiments; that is one lab's measurement on its setup.</p>
<p><strong>Fields.</strong> Prompt as rendered (system prompt, history, template version); chosen and rejected texts; the model, checkpoint and sampling settings behind each; label, strength and labeller (annotator id or judge model and version); cached reference log-probs and token counts for both responses; a split marker so held-out pairs never enter training.</p>
<p><strong>Sources.</strong> Sample both responses from the current SFT model at a temperature that gives real differences, so pairs are on-policy. Add a minority of pairs where one side comes from a stronger model, to show the policy responses it does not yet produce, accepting that those pairs also teach the stronger model's style.</p>
<p><strong>Ties and noise.</strong> DPO has no tie term, so a tie is either dropped or becomes noise. Keep only clear preferences, as Llama 3 did with its significantly better or better filter. Random disagreement only caps the margin: with a fraction p of labels one way, the loss's optimum is a margin of log(p/(1 − p)). Systematic bias is what hurts: measure the length and format difference between chosen and rejected across the set and rebalance before training.</p>
<p><strong>Filters.</strong> Exact and near-duplicate prompts; pairs whose responses are nearly identical, which carry little preference signal and are the pairs Razin et al. tie to the chosen response's probability falling; pairs where either response is truncated; prompts that leak evaluation sets.</p>
</Fragment>

### The split you were asked to choose

There are two defensible answers. **Mostly human** (all 40,000 human comparisons on on-policy pairs, judge pairs only to fill coverage gaps) costs money and calendar time and buys labels that match what users prefer. **Mostly judge** (tens of thousands of judge-labelled pairs per round across two or three on-policy rounds, with a few thousand human comparisons spent on calibrating the judge and on a held-out set) costs generation compute and imports the judge's biases, and buys pairs that track the policy as it changes. The second is reasonable only if judge-human agreement on a calibration sample is close to human-human agreement; if it is well below, the judge's labels train a different preference than the product wants.

The constraint conflict is label quality against coverage: human labels are better and fewer, and their pairs go stale as the policy moves away from the model that produced them.

### What goes wrong with the recommended answer

On-policy sampling from the SFT model gives pairs that are close together, because two samples from one model often differ in a few phrases. Those close pairs are where the chosen response's probability tends to fall during training, and the cheap filter for them (drop pairs whose responses are too similar) also removes most of the data in narrow domains. Monitor the chosen log-prob and the held-out behaviour, and add an NLL term on the chosen response (Llama 3 used 0.2) if it falls.

## Follow-ups

- Your reference log-probs were cached from the SFT checkpoint before a chat-template fix. What changes in the loss at step 0, and how would you notice?
- The policy's implicit reward ranks 95% of training pairs correctly after one epoch and 61% of held-out pairs. What does that gap tell you about the dataset rather than the model?

## Common mistakes

- Saying DPO has no reward model at all. It has an implicit one, β log(π/π_ref), and its quality depends on the pairs exactly as an explicit reward model's would.
- Treating the reference as optional. Without it the loss is a pure likelihood contrast, and nothing anchors the policy to the SFT model's distribution.
- Keeping ties with a random label "for volume". They add gradient in random directions and dilute the clear pairs.
- Building pairs from two different model families without noticing that the label then predicts the family's style.
