## Answer

**The cause is the norm.** The old `forward` disabled autocast and cast to FP32 before computing anything. The new one runs on BF16 tensors: `x * x` is an elementwise multiply and `mean` is a reduction, neither on autocast's float32 list, so `(x * x).mean(...)` and `mu * mu` are BF16 tensors, each rounded to 8 significant bits, and the variance is their difference. `rsqrt` is on the float32 list, which is what made the change look safe in review; it receives a variance that was already destroyed.

The arithmetic: for a row with mean μ and standard deviation σ, E[x²] = μ² + σ², and BF16 rounds each of E[x²] and μ² by up to half a unit in the last place, 2^−8 relative. When σ² is smaller than about 2^−8 × μ², roughly |μ| / σ above 16, the rounding error is as large as the variance itself. With μ = 300 and σ = 1, E[x²] = 90,001 and μ² = 90,000; between 65,536 and 131,072 BF16 values are 512 apart, so both round to 90,112 and the variance is 0. The norm then multiplies (x − μ) by 1/√eps, which is the rows above 300. When the rounding goes the other way the variance is −512, and `rsqrt` returns NaN, which is the 3 NaN rows.

The condition is a row whose mean is large relative to its spread, and the FP32 replay shows the model state itself is fine: the same weights and data produce no NaN when the subtraction happens in FP32.

**The others, ranked.**

1. *Parameters cast to BF16 with no master copy* is the next most important. `model.to(torch.bfloat16)` makes AdamW update BF16 weights in place: a step of order 3 × 10^−4 on a weight near 1 is below half of BF16's 2^−8 spacing just under 1.0 and is rounded away. That is the gap that opens after step 1,500 and reaches 0.22 by step 6,000. It does not cause the NaN, and fixing the norm alone leaves a run that quietly underperforms. The fix is to keep the parameters in FP32 and let autocast cast them for the matrix multiplies.
2. *GradScaler under BF16* is unnecessary, not harmful here. Its scale doubled every 2,000 steps without an overflow, which is what the defaults do when nothing overflows; BF16 has FP32's range. It costs a multiply and leaves a code path that will skip steps if a gradient ever does go non-finite, which hides the next problem rather than reporting it. Remove it.
3. *Cross-entropy under BF16 autocast* is the tempting non-defect: `cross_entropy` autocasts to float32, and the anomaly replay places the first NaN in a norm, upstream of the loss.

**What to collect, and what to ship without it.** Collect the per-row mean and standard deviation at the input of each norm for the bad rows and a sample of good ones, and which tokens those rows belong to; if |μ| / σ is in the hundreds on those rows, the mechanism is confirmed, and if it is near 1, look elsewhere. Without it, ship the FP32 norm today: restore the autocast-disabled block, and compute the variance as the mean of (x − μ)² in FP32, which has no cancellation at all. It costs one cast per norm and changes nothing for healthy rows.

**The quantity you choose: the alert threshold.** Log |μ| / σ per norm input, sampled every few hundred steps. The cancellation bound is about 16 for this formula in BF16; an alert at 8 gives a factor of two of warning, and an alert at 16 fires only when the damage has begun. A threshold below 4 alerts on healthy layers in many models and will be muted within a week.

**How the fix fails.** FP32 statistics fix the norm, not the cause of the large offsets. A residual stream whose per-row mean keeps growing will eventually hurt the BF16 matrix multiplies that read it, where nothing is recomputed in FP32. The alert above is there to catch that drift, and an offset that keeps growing is a modelling problem to take to the people who own the architecture, not a numerics patch.

## Follow-ups

- *The FP32 norm costs 2% of step time on your profile, and the team that owns the kernel wants to keep the BF16 version with a two-pass variance instead.* Which do you accept, what does each give up, and what test would you require before merging either?
- *After the fix, the BF16 run is still 0.04 above the FP16 run at step 20,000.* What would you need to see to conclude BF16 is worse for this model and go back to FP16 with loss scaling?
- *Two engineers disagree: one wants every custom op wrapped in an FP32 block, the other wants a numerics review only for reductions.* How do you decide without deciding by rank, and what do you write down?

## Common mistakes

- Blaming the loss function, which autocast already runs in FP32.
- Fixing the NaN and missing the plateau, which is a separate defect with a separate cause.
- Reading `rsqrt` on the float32 list as protection for the whole norm.
- Raising `eps` in the norm, which turns NaN rows into rows scaled by a smaller but still wrong factor.
