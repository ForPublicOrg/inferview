## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Scenario">
You need to fine-tune a 7B model that was pretrained in BF16. The pool available this week has GPUs whose tensor cores accelerate FP16 but not BF16; the BF16-capable pool frees up in ten days, and the deadline is in twelve.
</Callout>

Why does BF16 usually train more stably than FP16? Given that, do you run the fine-tune this week in FP16, in FP32 on the same pool, or wait for BF16? Say what you would measure in the first hour of whichever you choose.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask for the largest absolute activation and gradient values the pretrained model produces on a few hundred batches of the fine-tuning data.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how long the fine-tune takes in FP32 on this week's pool, measured on a few hundred steps.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>BF16 has FP32's 8-bit exponent, so anything FP32 can represent it can represent with fewer significant bits; FP16 has a 5-bit exponent, tops out at 65,504 and underflows below about 6 × 10^−8, so it needs loss scaling and still overflows on large activations. For a model pretrained in BF16, I would not start in FP16 without measuring its activation range first; if the maxima are near FP16's limit, I run FP32 this week if it fits the deadline, and wait for BF16 otherwise.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The formats trade range for precision in opposite directions. FP16's 10 mantissa bits give finer spacing than BF16's 7, but its exponent limits values to 65,504 at the top and about 6 × 10^−8 at the bottom. Gradients fall below the bottom, which is why FP16 training needs a loss scale; activations and gradients of a model trained without that constraint can exceed the top, which no loss scale fixes. The PyTorch AMP documentation warns about exactly this case: BF16-pretrained models may not fit FP16's range, their gradients may overflow rather than underflow, and the scale can fall below 1. BF16 removes both problems and pays in precision, which the FP32 master weights and FP32 reductions absorb.</p>
<p>Three options, each with a cost. FP16 this week: fastest on this pool, but the first overflow becomes skipped steps or NaN, and a scale below 1 means gradients are being shrunk to fit, which reintroduces underflow for small ones. FP32 this week: no range problem, but the tensor cores' FP16 throughput is unused, so it runs slower by that GPU's FP16-to-FP32 throughput ratio; whether that fits two days of slack is the measurement in hint 2. Wait: no risk from the format, and no slack left if the fine-tune needs a second attempt.</p>
<p>The quantity I choose is the headroom that makes FP16 acceptable: if the measured maxima of activations and of gradients after scaling stay below about 1/16 of 65,504, roughly 4,000, there is room for the loss scale to move by a factor of 16 without overflow, and FP16 with a dynamic scaler is defensible. Within a factor of 2 of the limit, it is not.</p>
<p>How the recommendation fails: maxima measured on a few hundred batches miss rare long-tail inputs, and an FP16 run that looked safe can overflow on step 5,000. I log the skipped-step count from the scaler and abort to FP32 if it exceeds a few per thousand steps.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li>The measured activation maximum is 30,000 in one layer and under 500 everywhere else. Can you run that one layer in FP32 and the rest in FP16, and what does it cost?</li>
<li>FP32 fits the deadline with one day to spare. A product manager wants FP16 so the day is kept for evaluation. What do you accept in writing?</li>
<li>The scaler skips 3 steps in the first 1,000 and then none. What would make you stop the run anyway?</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

The worked answer above turns on one asymmetry. FP16's failure is in range, so it shows up as infs, NaNs and skipped steps, and a loss scale moves the window without widening it. BF16's failure is in precision, so it shows up as small updates rounded away and inexact sums, and the standard recipe already handles it with FP32 master weights and FP32 reductions. That is why BF16 is the default for pretraining on hardware that supports it, and why moving a BF16-trained model into FP16 is the risky direction.

## Follow-ups

- *Two pools, one deadline: the BF16 pool can be had in five days if another team's job is pre-empted.* Who decides, and what do you put in the request?
- *Your FP16 run's loss matches a BF16 reference to within noise, but the scaler sat at 1 for most of the run.* What has it been doing to small gradients, and how would you check?

## Common mistakes

- Answering "BF16 has more precision", which is backwards: it has fewer mantissa bits than FP16.
- Assuming a loss scale fixes overflow; it can only shrink everything, which moves small values toward underflow.
- Treating a model's pretraining dtype as irrelevant to the fine-tuning dtype.
