## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Reported prompt">
A candidate report for a staff machine learning engineer loop at Cohere lists two design rounds, "design knowledge distillation of models" and "design an LLM post-training pipeline" covering data collection, SFT and RL. Cohere has not confirmed the questions. The version below combines them and is written for this site.
</Callout>

Your company has a large in-house model, post-trained for retrieval-augmented answering and tool use in enterprise settings. Several regulated customers want a model of under 10B parameters they can run on their own hardware. Design the pipeline that produces it: the distillation method, the post-training stages, and the evaluation gate after each stage that decides whether the next one runs.

Operating constraints: one team of five, a release date ten weeks out that sales has already announced, customer data that cannot be used for training, and a tokenizer for the small model that the hardware partner has already validated, which differs from the large model's.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what the large model exposes to the training cluster: text only, top-k log-probabilities, or full logits.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask which customer workloads the release must not regress on, and whether an evaluation for each exists today.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what the small model's latency budget is on the customer's hardware.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The tokenizer mismatch rules out logit and on-policy distillation from the large model in ten weeks, so the backbone is SFT on filtered teacher samples generated inside our cluster, followed by preference tuning or short RL against verifiable tasks (tool-call validity, citation grounding). Each stage has a gate on four suites (retrieval answering with citations, tool use, refusals and safety, and length and latency on the target hardware) plus a contamination check, and a stage that fails its gate is rolled back, not patched forward. What I give up: on-policy distillation's efficiency, and some headroom the student would have had with a matched tokenizer.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Stage 0, evaluation before data. Build or confirm the four suites with held-out items written or reviewed by people, plus the teacher's scores on each so every gate is expressed as a fraction of the teacher. Decontaminate the training data against these suites by exact match and n-gram overlap. This is week one and blocks everything else.</p>
<p>Stage 1, teacher data. Prompts from public and licensed sources and from synthetic generation over the customer task taxonomy, never customer data. The teacher generates several samples per prompt; filters are execution for tool calls (does the call validate against the schema and return the expected result in a sandbox), citation checks for retrieval answers (does each cited span support the sentence), and a reward model only where neither applies. Measure the filter's yield by task stratum and resample to keep the hard strata.</p>
<p>Stage 2, SFT of the student. Gate: each suite at or above an agreed fraction of the teacher's score, and output length within the latency budget on the target hardware, because distilled students inherit the teacher's length (DeepSeek-V3's R1 distillation doubled MATH-500 response length, 769 to 1,510 tokens).</p>
<p>Stage 3, preference tuning or RL on what SFT cannot fix: tool-call validity and grounding have verifiers, so short RL there is cheap; tone and refusals use DPO on teacher-ranked pairs. Gate: no suite regresses beyond its margin against stage 2, since general-purpose stages cost specialist scores (Qwen3's thinking-mode AIME'24 fell from 83.8 to 81.4 across its last two stages).</p>
<p>Stage 4, average checkpoints from the stage 3 runs with different seeds or data versions, as Llama 3 does at each post-training stage, and run the full gate again on the merged model.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li>Pushback: in week seven, stage 2 passes every gate except latency, where the student is 30% over budget because of response length. Sales will not move the date. What do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?</li>
<li>Reversal: what would you need to see in the stage 2 results to spend two of the remaining weeks building a tokenizer bridge for on-policy distillation instead of running stage 3?</li>
<li>Two senior engineers disagree on whether the safety suite gate should be "match the teacher" or "match the previous small model". How do you decide without deciding by rank?</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

**Decision with two defensible answers.** The central choice is the distillation method under the tokenizer constraint.

- *SFT on samples, then a short RL or DPO stage.* Works across tokenizers, fits ten weeks, and lets one filtered dataset serve several student sizes. Cost: distribution mismatch (the student never trains on its own mistakes), the teacher's verbosity, and an SFT dataset that has to be large because each sample carries one sequence of tokens rather than a full distribution.
- *Train a tokenizer-matched teacher, then on-policy distillation.* Fine-tune a copy of the large model, or a mid-sized one, with the student's tokenizer, then distil per token. Qwen3's table suggests on-policy distillation reaches more per GPU hour than RL from the same start. Cost: weeks of teacher adaptation the calendar does not have, and a teacher that may be weaker than the original after the tokenizer swap.

I choose the first for this release and write the second down as the next release's plan, with the stage 2 results as the evidence that decides it.

**What you give up, written down.** A decision memo for the release: the distillation method and why, the four gate suites with each margin and its owner, the contamination procedure, the latency budget on named hardware, and the rollback rule (any gate failure returns to the previous stage's checkpoint; no stage ships without passing its gate). It records that the student is capped near the teacher on every suite, and that customer workloads not represented in the suites are untested.

**How this design fails.** Every gate is relative to suites the team built in week one. A customer workload that the suites miss, such as a document format or language the teacher handles and the filter rarely kept, regresses without any gate firing. The mitigation is a pre-release period where two or three design-partner customers run the student on their own data and report failures on their side, since the data cannot come to you; it costs two of the ten weeks and is the first thing the schedule pressure will try to remove.

## Follow-ups

- The legal team approves using anonymised customer prompts, without responses, for the next release. Where in the pipeline does that change the most, and what new risk does it add?
- A customer reports the student fabricates tool arguments the teacher never fabricates. Which stage do you suspect first, and what do you measure?
- After a domain-specific continued pretraining of the student, its instruction following drops. Which tool from this module repairs it without losing the new domain knowledge?

## Common mistakes

- Choosing logit or on-policy distillation without checking that the teacher can score the student's tokens.
- Gates on the mean score across suites, which hides a regression in the one a customer depends on.
- No length or latency gate, so the distilled model passes quality and fails on the customer's hardware.
- Adding the contamination check after the scores look surprisingly good.
