## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"The run plan says: 'Chinchilla-optimal is 20 tokens per parameter, so 50B parameters on 1T tokens.' The model will be the default for an internal assistant with heavy traffic. We have two weeks before the cluster reservation starts, and leadership will give at most 5% of the run's compute to anything that is not the run. How sure are you about 20, and what would you size the run with instead?"
</Callout>

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which data mix, tokenizer and learning-rate schedule the 20 was measured on, and which ones this run uses.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how many tokens the model is expected to serve over its life.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask how many unique tokens of acceptable quality exist for this run.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Moderately sure that about 20 is the compute-optimal ratio for Chinchilla's recipe, and sure that it is the wrong objective for this model. The 20 is the ratio of one trained model (1.4T over 70B), consistent with two of the paper's three methods; the third method's published fit was wrong, and a corrected fit lands near 20 with an error bar that moves it by a quarter. But it minimises training compute only, and a model with heavy traffic should be smaller and trained longer. With 5% of compute and two weeks, I would run a small IsoFLOP sweep on our recipe to anchor the loss curve, and choose the size from that plus the serving volume.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>What 20 is.</b> 1.4T tokens over 70B parameters, the model Chinchilla trained at Gopher's budget. Approaches 1 and 2 gave near-equal scaling (a = 0.50 and 0.49). Approach 3, the parametric fit, implied far more tokens per parameter with an 80% interval of 0.454 to 0.455 on a; Besiroglu et al. re-fitted it on about 240 points read off the paper's plots and got a = 0.513, about 20 tokens per parameter, with a standard error of 0.02 and a ratio of 25.6 if five outliers stay in. They traced the original to averaged Huber losses that stopped the optimiser early. So the number survived; its precision did not.</p>
<p><b>What it does not cover.</b> Chinchilla's data, tokenizer and schedule. Kaplan and Chinchilla disagreed partly because of a schedule-length detail, which shows how recipe choices move the fit. And the objective: lowest loss per training FLOP. This model will serve heavily, and Sardana et al. show that adding 2N per inference token to the objective moves the optimum to smaller models on more tokens.</p>
<p><b>The decision, with two defensible answers.</b></p>
<ul>
<li><b>Spend the 5% on a sweep.</b> The run is 6 × 50e9 × 1e12 = 3e23 FLOPs; 5% is 1.5e22, enough for IsoFLOP profiles at three or four budgets up to about 1e21 with several sizes each. Extrapolating from 1e21 to 3e23 is two and a half orders of magnitude, closer than Llama 3's three and a half. Cost: two weeks, 5% of compute, and a fit that is still an extrapolation.</li>
<li><b>Skip the sweep and size by inference.</b> Take about 20 as the compute-optimal anchor, then go smaller and longer based on the serving volume, for example 25B on 2T tokens at the same compute. Cost: no measurement of how much loss the smaller model gives up on our recipe, so the quality target becomes a hope.</li>
</ul>
<p>I would run the sweep, because it is the only way to price the move to a smaller model on our data. Its result is not "the optimal N" but a loss curve against N and D, from which both the compute-optimal point and the loss penalty of the smaller, inference-friendly size can be read.</p>
<p><b>Failure mode of the recommendation.</b> A rushed sweep repeats Kaplan's mistake: if every small run uses a learning-rate schedule sized for the largest token count, the short runs look worse than they are, and the fit leans toward bigger models. Each run's schedule has to match its own token count. The second failure is the one the replication found: an optimiser that stops early on a rescaled objective produces confident, wrong coefficients. Fit with two methods and check that they agree before trusting either.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li>Leadership refuses the two weeks: the reservation starts on schedule. What do you size with, and what risk do you write down?</li>
<li>Your IsoFLOP minimum lands at 12 tokens per parameter on this data. Do you believe it? What would make you trust it over the published 20?</li>
<li>The sweep says the 25B model costs 3% more loss than the 50B at the same compute, and the serving team says 25B halves their fleet. Who decides, and on what number?</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

Llama 3's team ran its own sweep (6e18 to 1e22 FLOPs, 40M to 16B parameters) instead of borrowing the ratio, and its extrapolation (402B on 16.55T) is what it built around. Its report also notes that IsoFLOP curves flatten near the minimum as compute grows, which is why a size within a factor of about two of the optimum is usually a small loss penalty and an inference-driven move down is cheaper than it looks.

## Follow-ups

- If the model will be distilled into a smaller student, does the teacher's size move toward compute-optimal or further away, and why?
- The data team says only 1.2T unique tokens exist. How does repetition change what the scaling fit tells you?

## Common mistakes

- Quoting 20 as a law rather than a ratio from one trained model and two of three methods.
- Treating the published Approach 3 coefficients as reliable after the replication.
- Sizing a heavily served model by training compute alone.
- Running a sweep with one learning-rate schedule length for every token count.
