## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Design a chatbot that answers user questions in real time. Cover how you collect training data, which RL tasks and rewards you use, where the rewards are weak, and how you would detect the model exploiting them."
</Callout>

The scenario: a news-and-sports assistant whose questions are often about things that happened in the last day. A retrieval service owned by another team returns the top passages from a continuously updated index with publish timestamps; you cannot change it this quarter. Your team is four ML engineers with ten weeks. Legal allows training on logged conversations only where the user's consent flag is set. The product lead's metric is answer accuracy on fresh questions; the trust and safety lead's is the rate of confidently wrong answers. You choose the reward values for answering, being wrong and declining, and defend them.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask the trust and safety lead how many confidently wrong answers per thousand they would accept in exchange for how many fewer declines.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask the retrieval team for the fraction of logged questions where the top passages contain the answer at all.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask whether a sample of logged answers can be graded by people each week, and how many.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Train the model to answer from retrieved passages, not from memory. SFT on verified, cited answers generated by a stronger model plus consented logs. RL on four task families with mostly rule-based rewards built from frozen index snapshots: short-answer accuracy, citation support, declining when the passages lack the answer, and preferring the newer of conflicting passages. A small generative judge covers tone and completeness. Each reward has a known exploit (hedged multi-answers, citation stuffing, over-declining, judge length bias), so I track a counter-metric per exploit and a weekly human-graded set against KL from the SFT model, and stop at the human score's peak.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Serving, briefly.</strong> Question in, retrieval returns passages with timestamps, the model answers with citations to passage ids or declines. Freshness lives in the index; the model's job is to read, compare dates and cite. That decision shapes all training: the model is trained to trust the context, so every task feeds it retrieved passages.</p>
<p><strong>SFT data.</strong> Questions from consented logs and from templates over recent articles. A stronger model writes K candidate answers per question from the same passages; a citation checker keeps answers whose every sentence is supported by a cited passage; a person reviews a sample. Include questions where the passages lack the answer, with a decline as the target, and pairs of conflicting passages where the newer one wins.</p>
<p><strong>RL tasks and rewards.</strong> Build tasks from frozen snapshots of the index so the reward is reproducible:</p>
<ul>
<li><em>Accuracy.</em> Questions whose short answer is extractable from the snapshot (scores, dates, names). Reward from normalised exact match on a single answer field; zero for more than one candidate answer.</li>
<li><em>Grounding.</em> Each sentence must cite a passage that supports it. A judge model with written reasoning checks support, in the style of DeepSeek-V3's model-based reward that includes its chain of thought.</li>
<li><em>Declining.</em> Questions where the snapshot's top passages do not contain the answer. Reward values chosen below.</li>
<li><em>Recency.</em> Conflicting passages with different timestamps; a rule checks the answer matches the newer one.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Policy optimisation.</strong> A group-relative method in the GRPO family over these rewards, which needs no value model and suits rewards that are mostly binary.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li>The weekly human grading costs as much as your RL compute. Which half do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?</li>
<li>Two weeks after launch, declines rise from 6% to 15% of answers and accuracy on answered questions rises. What would you need to see to call that reward hacking rather than better calibration, and to reverse the decline reward?</li>
<li>The trust and safety lead and the product lead disagree on the wrong-answer penalty. How do you decide without deciding by rank?</li>
</ul>

### The reward values you were asked to choose

Give +1 for a correct answer, −w for a wrong one and 0 for a decline. A policy that believes its answer is right with probability p gains p − w(1 − p) by answering, so it should answer when p > w / (1 + w). With w = 1 the threshold is 0.5; with w = 3 it is 0.75; with w = 9 it is 0.9. Reasonable choices sit where the trust and safety lead's tolerance puts them, roughly w between 1 and 4. A w of 0 is unreasonable because declining then never pays and the model guesses; a very large w is unreasonable because declining becomes the best response to any doubt, and the accuracy metric on answered questions rises while usefulness falls.

The constraint conflict is exactly this threshold: the product lead's metric improves with answering, the trust and safety lead's with declining, and one number trades them.

### Two defensible designs

**Rule-heavy (recommended for ten weeks).** Rule rewards for accuracy, declining and recency, with the judge only for grounding. Cost: the model learns little about tone and completeness, and questions without a short extractable answer get weaker training. It follows DeepSeek-R1's reasoning that rule rewards are harder to hack than a neural reward model.

**Reward-model-heavy.** A pairwise reward model trained on human comparisons of full answers, plus the accuracy rule. Cost: the comparisons take weeks to collect, the reward model needs retraining as the index drifts, and it will be over-optimised: Gao et al. measured the gold reward rising and then falling as the policy moves away from its start, while the proxy keeps rising. It covers open-ended questions the rules cannot.

### Where each reward is weak, and the counter-metric

| Reward | Exploit | Counter-metric tracked per checkpoint |
|---|---|---|
| Exact match | several hedged answers in prose around one answer field | answers per response, from a parser over the prose |
| Citation support | citing every passage, or citing without reading | citations per sentence; judge agreement with people on a weekly sample |
| Declining | declining whenever retrieval looks thin | decline rate on questions people verified as answerable |
| Recency | always preferring the newest passage, even when it is about something else | accuracy on conflict cases where the older passage is the right one |
| Judge | length and confident tone | length-controlled scores; length distribution per checkpoint |

Snapshot memorisation is the exploit common to all of them: the policy learns the answers in the frozen snapshots. Evaluate every checkpoint on a snapshot taken after training began.

### Detecting exploitation and the stopping rule

Every checkpoint: the counter-metrics above, KL from the SFT model on held-out prompts, and the rule rewards on the post-training snapshot. Weekly: people grade a fixed sample of answers on fresh questions. Plot the human score against KL. Stop at its peak, not at the reward's, and treat a widening gap between rule reward and human score as the trigger to inspect transcripts.

**Failure mode of the recommended design.** Its weakest signal is the judge for grounding, and the RL run optimises against that judge for thousands of steps. If judge-human agreement on the weekly sample drops, the grounding reward has been learned around; freeze the run at the previous checkpoint and retrain or replace the judge.

### What you give up

Open-ended questions (explanations, opinions about a match) get little RL signal and stay at SFT quality this quarter. The decline threshold is a product decision written down with the w that implements it.

### Rollout plan with rollback triggers

1. Shadow the RL checkpoint on consented traffic for a week; people grade a sample against the SFT model.
2. Ship to a small share of users if the human score beats SFT and the confidently-wrong rate does not rise.
3. Roll back automatically if, on the daily sample, the confidently-wrong rate exceeds the SFT model's by more than the threshold the trust and safety lead set, or declines on answerable questions exceed theirs by more than the threshold the product lead set.

## Follow-ups

- The retrieval team says the passages contain the answer for only about half of logged questions. Does that change your decline reward, the task mix, or the product promise?
- How would you build the recency task so that the model cannot learn "newest wins" as a shortcut?
- What changes if the assistant must answer in four languages and the judge was trained mostly on English?

## Common mistakes

- Training the model to answer real-time questions from its weights; the facts change faster than any training run.
- A reward of 0 for wrong answers, which makes guessing free and declining pointless.
- Using the training snapshot for evaluation, so memorised answers look like accuracy.
- Watching only the training reward. It keeps rising after the human score turns.
