## Question

Your team runs RL with verifiable rewards on a 14B open model for an internal theorem-checking and code-repair product. After the last run, pass@1 on your eval went up substantially, and two researchers disagree about what to do with next quarter's budget, which covers one of two plans:

- **More RL**: longer runs, a larger problem set, and multi-turn episodes with the proof checker in the loop.
- **A teacher**: license API access to a frontier model that is stronger on your eval, generate and filter traces, and distil (SFT on samples; the provider exposes no log-probabilities).

One researcher cites Yue et al. to argue RL only sharpens what the base model can already sample, so it has hit its ceiling. The other says that paper does not apply to your setup. Decide which plan you fund, what evidence you collect first, and what would change your decision. The product team needs a decision in two weeks.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Measure before choosing: pass@k curves for the base and the RL model at k up to a few hundred on our eval, and the set of problems neither solves. If the RL curve sits above the base at small k and meets or falls below it at large k, and many problems are solved by neither, the run has sharpened rather than extended, and the teacher plan is the one that can raise the ceiling; I fund it, provided the licence permits training on outputs. If the RL model solves problems the base never does at large k, or our multi-turn checker setup differs enough from the paper's single-turn setting, RL keeps the budget.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>What Yue et al. show: across model families, RL algorithms and maths, code and visual benchmarks, RLVR models win at small k and their base models win at large k. On AIME24 at k = 1024 the paper finds 0.0% of problems solved only by the RL model and 13.3% only by the base. Training longer made it sharper and narrower: on Omni-MATH, pass@1 rose from 26.1 to 42.5 between steps 150 and 450 while pass@256 fell from 66.3 to 64.3. Distillation behaved differently: R1-Distill-Qwen-7B's curve sat above its base across the tested k. Qwen3's Table 21 shows the same split in one setting, with RL leaving pass@64 at 90.0 and on-policy distillation raising it to 93.3.</p>
<p>Why it might not transfer: the paper studies single-turn RLVR as practised in 2025 and lists multi-turn agentic RL as a direction that may behave differently, and its authors note strong pipelines are proprietary. Our plan's multi-turn checker loop is exactly that untested case. Large-k pass@k also gives the base credit for lucky answers on short-answer benchmarks; our checker verifies proofs, which removes most of that luck, so our curves are more trustworthy than a maths benchmark's.</p>
<p>The two-week plan: sample both models at k = 1, 8, 64 and 256 on the eval, with the checker as judge. Report the crossover point if any, and three sets: solved by both, only by RL, only by base. Then a small distillation pilot, a few thousand filtered teacher traces, to see whether the student's large-k curve moves above the base's. Whichever plan moves the large-k curve gets the quarter.</p>
</Fragment>

<ul>
<li>Pushback: the pilot shows distillation lifts pass@256 by four points but pass@1 by less than RL did, and the product only ever samples once. Which number decides, and what do you write in the decision note?</li>
<li>Reversal: what result from the multi-turn RL pilot would make you move the budget back to RL after funding the teacher?</li>
<li>The licence forbids training models that compete with the provider. Legal says your product might qualify. How do you decide without waiting a quarter for a legal answer?</li>
</ul>

**The decision, with both sides costed.**

- *Fund the teacher.* Distillation is the evidence-backed way to add reachable solutions, and DeepSeek-R1's control shows the size of the gap that can exist: on Qwen-32B-Base, SFT on R1 samples reached 72.6 on AIME 2024 where over 10K steps of RL reached 47.0. Cost: licence fees and data-handling terms, a student capped near the teacher on whatever the teacher does well, the teacher's verbosity, and a dependency on a provider whose model or terms can change.
- *Fund more RL.* It keeps the pipeline in-house, works against your own verifier, and is the only option that can exceed every available teacher. Cost: if Yue et al.'s finding holds for your task, a quarter of compute sharpens pass@1 while the set of problems the model can solve at all stays flat or shrinks, and you discover this at the end.

My recommendation is the measurement first, then the teacher if the curves show sharpening, with a small multi-turn RL pilot kept alive because that is the setting the paper did not test. The constraint conflict is the two-week deadline against the cost of large-k sampling: k = 256 on the eval is 256 times the inference of a normal evaluation, so I would sample a stratified subset of a few hundred problems rather than the whole eval.

**How the recommendation fails.** pass@k at large k measures what the model can reach, not what it will produce in one sample. A product that samples once is served by pass@1, and RL's sharpening is exactly what such a product wants. If the measurement shows sharpening and I fund the teacher, I may trade a pass@1 the product needs for coverage it never uses. The decision note has to name which metric the product depends on before the measurement comes back.

## Follow-ups

- A third option appears: on-policy distillation from an open-weight teacher with your tokenizer, weaker than the frontier model but scoring every token. How does it compare with the two plans?
- Your eval has 400 problems. How do you tell a two-point change in pass@256 from noise?

## Common mistakes

- Reading Yue et al. as "RL does not work", when its own data shows RL raises pass@1.
- Comparing pass@1 only, which cannot distinguish sharpening from new capability.
- Assuming single-turn findings carry over to multi-turn RL with a tool in the loop.
