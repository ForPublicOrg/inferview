## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Here are four integrations for one agent. For each, A2A, MCP, or no protocol at all?"
</Callout>

Your team owns a procurement agent, deployed as one service. It needs four integrations. The systems and their owners belong to this scenario.

1. **Inventory.** Part counts in tables in the service's own Postgres database. Only this agent reads them, and a query returns in milliseconds.
2. **Supplier.** A supplier company runs its own quoting agent. A quote can take two days, and the quoting agent sometimes stops to ask for a drawing or for a buyer to sign in. The supplier will not show you how its agent works, and will expose whichever standard protocol you pick.
3. **Archive.** The contract archive, owned by the legal-platform team, is read by eleven agents across four teams and from engineers' coding-agent sessions.
4. **Compliance.** A second agent your team wrote, with its own prompt and tools, that reviews each purchase order before it is submitted. It ships in the same service and the same deploy.

Which mapping fits best?

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>For each system, ask who owns it, who else calls it, and whether either side can change without the other's release.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask the supplier for the longest quote they have run, and what their agent does while it waits for something from your side.</p></details>

## Answer

The rule that sorts all four: a protocol boundary pays for itself where an ownership boundary already exists, and the protocol is chosen by what is on the other side. A data source or capability that many clients share goes behind MCP. A peer agent run by another party, doing work that has its own lifecycle, is reached over A2A. Inside one team and one deploy, neither.

**Supplier: A2A.** The A2A specification describes agents as potentially opaque, collaborating without access to each other's internal state, memory or tools, and its appendix on MCP describes A2A as how agents partner or delegate work as peers. The task lifecycle carries exactly what the quote needs: submitted, working, then completed, failed, canceled or rejected, with input required for the drawing and auth required for the buyer's sign-in. You discover the supplier's agent from its Agent Card at `/.well-known/agent-card.json`, and updates arrive over a stream or as push notifications to your webhook, so nothing in your service waits on a socket for two days.

**Archive: MCP.** It is a shared data source owned by another team, with many clients, some of them coding agents you do not control. An MCP server gives the legal-platform team one place for authorisation, audit and rate limits, and every client gets the same tools.

**Inventory and compliance: no protocol.** The inventory query is a function in the same codebase. The compliance agent is a sub-agent: call it in process with the purchase order and read its verdict. A protocol between two components that ship together adds a hop, a serialisation format, an auth story and a second place to look when a trace goes wrong, and buys no independence, because neither side can release without the other.

**What this answer costs.** The in-process compliance agent couples its release to the procurement service. If the risk team later takes it over, or a second agent needs it, it has to be extracted behind a boundary, and that is the moment to choose a protocol. The A2A integration has its own failure mode: a task that outlives your process. Persist every task ID with its state, and after a restart resubscribe to each open task or rely on the webhook; otherwise a deploy in the middle of a two-day quote orphans it on your side while the supplier keeps working.

**Why the wrong picks are tempting.** "Agents speak A2A, tools speak MCP" sounds like the rule, so the first option applies it by category and ignores ownership. One protocol everywhere is attractive to operate, and a blocking tool works for the quick quotes, which is how it passes the demo. And "another team owns it, so it gets an agent protocol" confuses the organisational test with the protocol choice: the archive is something agents read, not something that negotiates.

## Follow-ups

- *The 2026-07-28 MCP revision moved tasks into an extension with polling and client-to-server input. Does that make MCP good enough for the supplier, and what would you still be missing?*
- *You need a cancellation policy for supplier tasks. What timeout do you set, from what data, and what happens to a quote the supplier finishes after you cancelled?* Something tied to the supplier's own quote times, such as a few times their slowest recent quote, is defensible; a timeout shorter than their normal two days cancels good work, and none at all leaves orphaned tasks.
- *Your push-notification webhook receives a notification for a task ID you do not recognise. What does the A2A specification ask you to do, and what attack is it guarding against?*

## Common mistakes

- Deciding by the kind of thing (agent or data) and never asking who owns it.
- Wrapping a long-running partner agent as a synchronous tool and finding the timeout in production.
- Putting a network protocol between two components that share a repository and a deploy.
- Calling A2A a Google protocol today. Google started it; the Linux Foundation hosts it.
