## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt, paraphrased from a public question bank">
A production agent calls a broken tool 400 times in five minutes. Which patterns prevent it at the orchestrator, at the tool layer and at the cost guard?
</Callout>

The incident, as a scenario. A customer-support agent runs on Claude Sonnet 5.5 in an orchestrator your team wrote and owns. Its tools reach partner APIs through an HTTP gateway owned by the platform team.

- At 14:02 a shipping partner deploys a change, and `get_shipment(tracking_id, carrier)` starts returning HTTP 400 `unknown carrier code` within milliseconds for every request.
- From 14:02 to 14:07, 37 conversations make 400 calls to `get_shipment`. Each conversation's model tries other carrier spellings (`"UPS"`, `"ups"`, `"UPS Ground"`). The tool wrapper returns `{"error": "HTTP 400"}` for any non-2xx response.
- Your orchestrator stops a run after the same tool with the same arguments fails 3 times; it fired in 4 of the 37 runs. A 40-turn cap ended the worst run with a generic error and no answer.
- At 14:06 the partner rate-limits your account. That also breaks the order-status page, which uses the same partner account and no model.
- The per-run spend cap ($2) and the org spend alert ($50 an hour) did not fire.

Constraints: your team is three engineers; the platform team's next gateway release is in three weeks; the support lead points out that about 2% of `get_shipment` calls normally fail with HTTP 503 and succeed on retry, and objects to any control that makes the agent give up on shipment questions.

Say which control at each layer would have stopped this incident and at what point, choose the thresholds, decide what ships this week, and write the rollout note.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The cost layer would not have stopped it: the worst run cost under a dollar and the whole incident about $5 to $13. This is a partner-protection and user-experience failure. At the tool layer, the wrapper should classify errors (a 400 for this tool is not retryable, a 503 is retried inside the wrapper and never shown to the model) and return an error that says what failed; a fleet-wide breaker keyed on tool and error class would have cut the incident to the first twenty or so calls. At the orchestrator, a per-run budget of non-retryable errors per tool, keyed on the error and not on the arguments, ends each run after two failures with a final tool-less turn and a handoff, which caps this incident at 74 calls. This week I would ship the wrapper's error classes and the per-run budget, and run a breaker in our wrapper only if the partner's rate limit sits below what 74 calls in a burst would hit.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Pricing the incident first.</b> Scenario token counts: a 6,000-token prefix, about 250 tokens added per failed call, 150 output tokens per call. The worst run made 40 calls: 40 × 6,000 + 250 × (0 + 1 + … + 39) = 240,000 + 195,000 = 435,000 input tokens, $0.87 uncached at $2 per million, plus 6,000 output tokens, $0.06. With the cache holding, the same run is about $0.14. All 400 calls together send between 400 × 6,000 = 2.4 million and 400 × 15,750 = 6.3 million input tokens, $4.80 to $12.60 uncached, plus $0.60 of output. Neither the $2 per-run cap nor the $50 hourly alert could fire. The question bank's model answer calls runaway loops "first-and-foremost a billing event"; this one is not, and a design that leads with spend caps misses it.</p>
<p><b>Why the existing guard missed.</b> The repeat detector keys on exact arguments, and the model kept changing the carrier, as Claude is documented to do: it retries an invalid call 2 to 3 times with corrections. An opaque <code>HTTP 400</code> reads like an invalid call, so the model corrected and retried. The guard needs a key that does not include the arguments.</p>
<p><b>Tool layer.</b></p>
<ul>
<li>Error classes in the wrapper: 503, 502 and timeouts are retried inside the wrapper with backoff and only reach the model after the wrapper gives up; 400 and 422 return <code>is_error</code> with <code>"retryable": false</code> and the partner's message (<code>unknown carrier code</code>), so the model can tell the customer something true.</li>
<li>A breaker per (tool, error class), shared across runs. Thresholds to choose: I would open it on 20 non-retryable errors from at least 5 different runs within 60 seconds, and probe again after 30 seconds. At this incident's rate (400 calls in 300 seconds, about 1.3 a second) it opens in roughly 15 seconds. Requiring several runs keeps one customer's mistyped tracking numbers from opening it for everyone. Counting 503s only after the wrapper's own retries are spent keeps independent blips out of it.</li>
</ul>
<p><b>Orchestrator.</b></p>
<ul>
<li>A per-run budget of 2 non-retryable errors per tool, keyed on (tool, error class). On the third, the run takes one final tool-less turn and hands off to a human with the conversation attached. Here that caps each run at 2 calls, 74 across 37 runs.</li>
<li>The turn cap stays as the backstop and keeps one turn for the final answer, so a capped run never ends on a generic error again.</li>
</ul>
<p><b>Cost layer.</b> Per-run and per-tenant spend caps stay, for the failure they do catch (long runs with large contexts), and get a sibling: an alert on tool-error rate per tool, which is the signal this incident produced.</p>
<p><b>The decision: what ships this week.</b> Two answers are defensible.</p>
<ul>
<li><b>Per-run controls now, the fleet breaker in the gateway in three weeks.</b> Cost: until then a partner break still sends up to 2 calls per active run at once, 74 here, and whether that trips the partner's rate limit depends on a number we have not been given.</li>
<li><b>Per-run controls now plus a breaker in our own wrapper, on the Redis we already run.</b> Cost: a second breaker that duplicates the gateway's in three weeks, shared state on the request path with a fail-open or fail-closed choice to make, and the same three engineers spending time on it instead of on the classification work.</li>
</ul>
<p>I would take the first if the partner's limit sits comfortably above peak concurrent runs times two, and the second if it does not, because the order-status page shares the account and has no fallback.</p>
<p><b>The rollout note.</b> Week one: the wrapper logs its error classes and the per-run budget runs in shadow, recording which conversations it would have ended. Enforce after the support lead has reviewed a sample of would-be handoffs. Rollback trigger: the handoff rate on shipment conversations rising above the shadow-week baseline by a margin agreed with the support lead before enforcement, measured daily. Owner: our team for the orchestrator, platform for the gateway breaker, with the partner's rate limit recorded in the note.</p>
<p><b>What you give up.</b> Some conversations now end in a handoff that a third or fourth attempt would have answered, and the model no longer gets to explore around a non-retryable error. The support lead's objection is partly right: that is the price.</p>
<p><b>How this design fails.</b> Classification by status code trusts the partner's codes. A partner that answers 200 with an error body, or a 400 that is actually transient, walks straight past it. A per-run budget of 2 also ends conversations where the customer mistyped a tracking number twice, which on a normal day may be the main source of 400s from this tool; ask for that rate before enforcing. And a shared breaker becomes a dependency: if its store is down, the wrapper has to choose between failing open (back to this incident) and failing closed (every shipment question gets a handoff).</p>
</Fragment>

<ul>
<li><em>In the first week of enforcement the breaker opens four times, three of them for partner blips under a minute, and about 600 customers get a handoff instead of an answer. The support lead wants it gone. Which threshold do you loosen, and what risk do you accept in writing?</em></li>
<li><em>What would you need to see in production to remove the per-run error budget once the gateway breaker ships?</em> A quarter of partner incidents in which the gateway breaker opened before any run reached two errors, and a handoff rate on mistyped tracking numbers that the budget alone was causing.</li>

## Follow-ups

- *The partner changes again, and now returns HTTP 200 with `{"status": "error"}`. Which of your controls still fires?*
- *A tenant's integration sends malformed tracking numbers on every request. What stops it from opening the breaker for every other tenant?*
- *Where does the per-run error budget live when a conversation moves to another worker mid-run?*

## Common mistakes

- Leading with spend caps for a failure that cost a few dollars and broke a partner integration.
- Keying the stuck-run guard on exact arguments, which a model that corrects its calls never repeats.
- Showing transient 503s to the model, so it learns to retry everything, instead of retrying inside the wrapper.
- A breaker counted per run, which cannot see 37 runs failing at once.
- Ending capped runs with a generic error instead of a final answer and a handoff.
- Shipping the guards in enforce mode on day one without a shadow period or a rollback trigger agreed with the people who own the customer conversation.
