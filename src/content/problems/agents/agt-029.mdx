## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 40 minutes">
"We are launching an agent that books business travel through airline and hotel websites. How do you evaluate it before launch?"
</Callout>

What you learn by asking (scenario):

- The agent drives a browser for employees: flights and hotels on five sites, two airline sites, one hotel chain, one online travel agency and the company's corporate travel tool. It pays with the employee's corporate card and must follow the travel policy: economy on flights under six hours, and a nightly hotel cap per city.
- Only the corporate travel tool has a vendor sandbox. The other four are live sites, where a booking costs real money and some fares are non-refundable.
- Measured on 30 hand-run tasks: an episode averages 38 browser actions, $0.60 of model calls and 9 minutes.
- The eval budget is $3,000 a week of model spend and 40 parallel browser workers. Four engineers, six weeks to the launch decision.
- The product lead wants one success rate on the launch slide.

Cover the environment, what counts as success, how side effects are measured, how many tasks and how many trials of each, and the launch gate. State what you give up, and write the launch gate as you would send it.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what a wrong booking costs to undo today when a person makes it, by fare type.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how often each site changes its booking flow, and whether anyone has measured it.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what a user does after a failed booking: retry the agent, or call the travel desk.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Three environments: the vendor sandbox, self-hosted clones of the two highest-volume booking flows with a resettable database, and the live sites in read-only runs that a harness stops before payment. Success is checked on state, not on the transcript: after an episode, the reservation store holds exactly the intended booking within policy and nothing else, and infeasible tasks succeed only when the agent stops and says why. Side effects are a separate metric over the whole state diff, with zero-tolerance classes reported as an upper bound. Reliability is pass^k: I spend the budget on 400 tasks at 3 trials plus 60 high-value tasks at 8. The slide gets pass^1, pass^3 and the side-effect bound, not one number. What I give up: automated measurement of live payment, and full flows on two of the four live sites.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Environment.</strong> Live sites cannot be the eval: a booking costs money, a run is not repeatable, and a non-refundable fare is a side effect that cannot be undone. WebArena's answer is self-hosted sites delivered as containers with a reset to a deterministic initial state, and success checked by programs that read that state. I would build that for the two booking flows with the most volume, and four engineers in six weeks is the limit: two clones, not four. The corporate tool's vendor sandbox is the third environment. The remaining live sites get read-only runs: the agent works up to the payment page, and the harness blocks the payment submission at the network layer, not by telling the agent to stop.</p>
<p><strong>Success, on state.</strong> On a clone or the sandbox, the check reads the reservation store after the episode: the right traveller, dates, flight or hotel, a fare class and price within policy, and no other reservation. On a live read-only run, the check parses the itinerary and price on the payment page and compares them with the expected booking. About one task in ten is infeasible on purpose (no fare meets the policy, the hotel is full) and succeeds only if the agent stops and says why. WebArena labels such tasks N/A and found that telling agents to stop on impossible tasks made them give up on feasible ones, so I score both directions: false completions on infeasible tasks and early stops on feasible ones. Free-text answers ("which fare did you pick and why") go to an LLM judge, as WebArena did for fuzzy matches, validated on a labelled sample before its scores count.</p>
<p><strong>Side effects, as their own metric.</strong> Diff the whole clone state, not only the reservation table: holds left open, extra reservations, profile and loyalty changes, saved cards, messages in the clone's mail sink. Three classes are zero-tolerance: a payment the user did not confirm, a booking for the wrong traveller, a non-refundable fare where policy required refundable. A clean run does not prove zero. With none observed in 1,680 episodes, the rule of three bounds the rate at 3 / 1,680 ≈ 0.18% per episode at 95% confidence, and that bound is what goes in the launch memo.</p>
<p><strong>Reliability: how many tasks, how many trials.</strong> An employee who books the same kind of trip every month cares whether it works every time. tau-bench's pass^k is the chance that all k independent trials of a task succeed, averaged over tasks, estimated per task as C(c, k) / C(n, k); its gpt-4o agent was above 60% average success on retail and below 25% at pass^8. The budget sets the episodes: $3,000 / $0.60 = 5,000 a week, and 1,200 episodes take 1,200 × 9 / 60 / 40 = 4.5 hours on 40 workers. At 1,200 episodes a run there are two defensible plans:</p>
<ul>
<li><em>150 tasks × 8 trials.</em> Per-task reliability measured directly. If a task's true success is 0.9, its pass^8 is 0.9^8 ≈ 0.43, and across 150 tasks the estimate's standard error is about √(0.43 × 0.57 / 150) ≈ 0.04. The cost: 150 tasks cover few combinations of site, trip type and edge case (multi-city, loyalty numbers, sold-out fares).</li>
<li><em>400 tasks × 3 trials.</em> More of the task space, and pass^3 at 0.9^3 ≈ 0.73 has a standard error near √(0.73 × 0.27 / 400) ≈ 0.022. The cost: a task that fails one time in eight passes all three trials with probability 0.875^3 ≈ 0.67, so most flaky tasks look fine.</li>
</ul>
<p>I would run 400 tasks at 3 trials weekly and the 60 highest-value tasks (the routes and hotels that make up most bookings) at 8, which is 1,680 episodes, $1,008 and 1,680 × 9 / 60 / 40 ≈ 6.3 hours a week, leaving budget for the live read-only runs. Reasonable choices run from k = 3 to 8; k = 1 measures average success and says nothing about consistency, and k = 32 on 40 tasks buys precision on a handful of trips. This is the constraint conflict: coverage against per-task reliability, under a fixed budget.</p>
<p><strong>The number on the slide.</strong> The product lead's single number hides the two things the launch depends on. The slide gets three: tasks completed on a single try (pass^1), tasks completed on all three tries (pass^3), each with its interval, and the side-effect bound. One number is available if it must be: pass^3 on the top 60 tasks, because it is what a repeat traveller experiences.</p>
<p><strong>The launch gate, as sent.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Launch to 200 employees with confirmation before every payment if: pass^3 on the weekly 400 is at or above the bar product and the travel desk agree on before the first run; no zero-tolerance side effect in the last two weekly runs; the live read-only runs reach the right payment page on at least the same share of tasks as the clones.</li>
<li>Roll back to the travel desk if, in production: any booking differs from what the user confirmed; any payment executes without a confirmation; rebooking or cancellation tickets from agent bookings exceed those from desk bookings for two weeks.</li>
<li>What we have not measured: the live payment step, and full flows on the hotel chain and the travel agency.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>What I give up.</strong> An automated measure of the live payment step; full-flow coverage of two live sites; reliability beyond three trials for most tasks; a single headline number.</p>
<p><strong>Where this design fails.</strong> Clones go stale. When an airline changes its checkout, the clone still passes and production fails; the eval measures last month's site. The read-only live runs catch changes up to the payment page and nothing after it, so the clones need an owner and a refresh trigger: a drop in the live runs' payment-page success that the clones do not show. The second failure is the task set: tasks written by the team are cleaner than what employees type. Once the pilot starts, new tasks come from a sample of real requests, rewritten only to remove personal data.</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: finance cuts the eval budget to $1,000 a week. Which do you drop, trials, tasks or the live read-only runs, and what risk do you write down for the one you drop?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in the first two months of production to switch the gate from pass^3 to pass^1?* Measured retry behaviour: if users nearly always retry a failed booking and the retry succeeds, single-try success is what they experience; if they call the desk after one failure, consistency is the product.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants recorded page replays instead of clones because they take days to build rather than weeks, the other says replays cannot test a booking that changes state. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Build both for one flow, run the same 50 tasks through each and through the live read-only harness, and compare which agrees with live on where the agent fails.
- *One airline redesigns its checkout two weeks before launch. Do you rebuild the clone, drop the site from the launch, or launch it with confirmation on every step, and who signs off?*

## Common mistakes

- Evaluating on live sites with real payments, or with an instruction to the agent to stop before paying.
- Scoring the transcript or the agent's final message instead of the state it left behind.
- Reporting success with side effects folded in, so a booking that also left a hold open counts as a pass.
- Reporting "zero unsafe actions" from a clean run instead of an upper bound.
- Running each task once and calling the average a reliability figure.
- Leaving infeasible tasks out, so the agent is never tested on stopping.
