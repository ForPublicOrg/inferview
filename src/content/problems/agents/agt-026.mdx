## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design, 50 minutes">
A procurement team wants a research assistant that answers vendor due-diligence questions ("compare three payment processors on PCI DSS scope, data residency and pricing") with a cited report. The design is an orchestrator that splits the question and delegates to research workers. Write the delegation contract between the orchestrator and a worker: what every delegation states, what comes back, how the budget is enforced and how the result is verified. Then choose the worker count and per-worker budget, and defend them.
</Callout>

The operating constraints, a scenario given by the interviewer:

- Three engineers, eight weeks to the first procurement analysts using it. The company's internal document search is an existing service the team cannot change.
- Finance caps model spend at $1.50 per report. Legal requires every factual claim in a report to link to a page that supports it.
- The product lead wants up to 12 workers on complex questions, because a blog post said more agents scored better.
- The orchestrator runs on Opus 5.5 and the workers on Sonnet 5.5. A worker's system prompt and brief come to about 4,000 tokens; each tool call adds about 300 tokens of model output and 2,500 tokens of results; a worker's final answer is about 1,500 tokens.

End with the contract as you would write it, a decision memo on the 12-worker request, a rollout plan with rollback triggers, and what you give up.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Every delegation is a typed object, not prose: an objective, what is out of scope because another worker owns it, the allowed tools and sources, a budget the harness enforces (tool calls, output tokens, wall-clock), an output schema of findings that each carry a claim, a verbatim quote and a URL, plus gaps and a stop reason, and the verification the result must pass. Workers are agents called as tools, so the orchestrator owns the report and each worker sees only its contract. With prompt caching, a worker making 10 tool calls costs about $0.14, so the cap admits 6 workers at 15 calls or 12 workers at 6 calls; I would ship 6 deep workers, decline the 12-worker request for now, and verify every quote with code before a cheap model checks that it supports the claim.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Why a contract.</strong> Anthropic's research system found that short, vague delegations led workers to misread tasks and duplicate each other's searches, and that each subagent needs an objective, an output format, guidance on tools and sources, and task boundaries. MAST's first failure category is the same problem in other systems: workers that disobey or never received a clear task and role specification, and that do not know when to stop. A contract makes each of those a field the orchestrator must fill and the harness can check.</p>
<p><strong>The pattern.</strong> Agents as tools rather than handoffs: the orchestrator owns the final answer, and in the OpenAI Agents SDK, <code>Agent.as_tool()</code> takes structured parameters, so the contract is the tool's input schema, and <code>max_turns</code> bounds the worker's loop. The worker does not inherit the orchestrator's conversation, which is what you want: if a fact is not in the contract, the worker does not have it.</p>
<p><strong>Budgets in the harness, guidance in the prompt.</strong> The prompt carries effort guidance in the shape Anthropic published (a fact lookup needs a few calls, a comparison more). The harness enforces the ceiling: when a worker reaches its tool-call or token budget, the next request is made without tools and with an instruction to answer from what it has, and the result carries <code>stopped_because: budget</code>.</p>
<p><strong>Verification in two stages.</strong> Code first: every finding's quote must appear, after whitespace normalisation, in the page fetched from its URL; findings that fail are dropped and counted. Then Haiku 5.5 reads each surviving claim with its quote and answers whether the quote supports the claim. Forty claims with 3,200 tokens each of page excerpt and prompt cost 40 × 3,200 × $0.10 / 1,000,000 + 40 × 100 × $0.50 / 1,000,000 = $0.015.</p>
</Fragment>

<ul>
<li><em>The quote check and the support check together cost less than two cents, but the support check's false accepts are unmeasured. Legal asks for a number before launch. How do you get one, and what do you accept in writing until you have it?</em></li>
<li><em>Two workers return contradictory findings, each with a valid quote from a different page. Whose job is it to resolve that, and what does the contract need to say?</em></li>
<li><em>What would you need to see in production to give workers the right to spawn their own workers?</em></li>

### The contract

```yaml
delegation:                       # the as_tool input schema; one per worker per report
  task_id: r-2291/w3              # re-dispatch with the same id returns the stored result
  objective: PCI DSS scope of Processor B's hosted checkout for a card-not-present merchant, as of 2026
  out_of_scope: [pricing, data residency, Processors A and C]     # owned by other workers
  context: Merchant stores no card data; integration is by redirect.   # facts the worker cannot find itself
  tools: [web_search, fetch_url, internal_search]
  sources: prefer the vendor's own docs and the PCI Security Standards Council; no forums
  budget: { tool_calls: 15, output_tokens: 6000, wall_clock_s: 240 }
  returns:
    findings: [{ claim, quote, url, retrieved_at }]   # quote is verbatim from the page
    gaps: [string]                # what the objective asked that the worker could not establish
    conflicts: [string]           # sources that disagree, with both URLs
    stopped_because: done | budget | blocked
  verification:
    - quote appears in the fetched page at url (code); else the finding is dropped
    - claim is supported by quote (Haiku 5.5); else the finding is dropped and logged
  on_failure: return partial results with stopped_because; never retry silently
```

### Choosing the worker count and budget

One worker making N tool calls sends N + 1 requests, and request i carries 4,000 + 2,800 × i tokens of context. At N = 10 that is 198,000 input tokens in total.

- Without caching: 198,000 × $2 / 1,000,000 = $0.396, plus 4,500 output tokens × $10 / 1,000,000 = $0.045, so $0.44 a worker.
- With caching, each segment is written once and read on every later request: 32,000 tokens written × $2.50 / 1,000,000 = $0.080, 166,000 read × $0.10 / 1,000,000 = $0.017, plus $0.045 output, so $0.14 a worker.

The orchestrator on Opus 5.5 plans with 6,000 input and 1,000 output tokens ($0.044) and synthesises from 6,000 plus 1,500 tokens per worker, writing 3,000 tokens. Totals per report, with caching and the $0.015 citation check:

| Workers x tool calls | Per worker | Report | Under $1.50 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 x 10, no caching | $0.44 | $2.38 | no |
| 5 x 10 | $0.14 | $0.88 | yes |
| 6 x 15 | $0.21 | $1.44 | yes |
| 12 x 6 | $0.09 | $1.31 | yes |
| 12 x 10 | $0.14 | $1.91 | no |
| 8 x 15 | $0.21 | $1.87 | no |

Caching is not optional at this cap: without it, five workers already cost $2.38. The reasonable choices are on the frontier between depth and breadth, and two are defensible.

- *Six deep workers at 15 calls ($1.44).* Each subquestion gets the call budget Anthropic's rules give a comparison. The cost: a question with more than six separable parts gets merged subquestions, and the cap has $0.06 of headroom, so a long synthesis or a re-dispatch pushes a report over.
- *Twelve shallow workers at 6 calls ($1.31).* Breadth for questions with many vendors or criteria. The cost: six calls is enough to find a page and rarely enough to reconcile two conflicting ones, more workers means more duplicated searches to prevent with <code>out_of_scope</code>, and the orchestrator's synthesis input doubles.

Unreasonable: a fixed 12 workers at 15 calls, which costs about $2.74 a report and spends the cap on every question regardless of its size; or any plan priced without caching.

**Decision memo on the 12-worker request.** Ship with the orchestrator choosing 2 to 6 workers per question and a harness ceiling of 15 calls each. The cited result was the vendor's own internal eval at a different cost point, and Anthropic attributes most of the variance it saw to token usage, so more workers at a fixed cap is not the same experiment. Revisit 12 shallow workers once the ten past questions show how many separable subquestions procurement actually asks, and run both configurations on them against the analysts' reports.

**What you give up.** Breadth on the largest questions, a report that finishes faster than its slowest worker (the orchestrator waits for every worker, as Anthropic's lead agent does), and about $0.06 of headroom per report. Code-first verification gives up claims supported by pages the fetcher cannot read, such as PDFs behind a login, which come back as gaps.

**Rollout.** Weeks 1 to 3: contract schema, the as-tool worker, budget enforcement and the quote check, run on the ten past questions. Weeks 4 to 6: support check, cost tracking per report, side-by-side review by two analysts on 30 questions. Weeks 7 and 8: the procurement team, with every report marked as draft until an analyst signs it.

**Rollback triggers** (thresholds are choices to revisit after a month): more than 5% of findings in a day failing the quote check sends reports to analyst review before anyone sees them; any report over $1.50 lowers the worker ceiling by one until the cause is found; more than a third of workers ending with <code>stopped_because: budget</code> over a week means the budgets are wrong for the questions, and the effort guidance is redone before more users join.

**The failure mode of this design.** The harness cap turns an unfinished investigation into a short, confident answer. A worker that hits 15 calls halfway through reconciling two pages returns the findings it has, every quote verifies, and the orchestrator writes a report that omits what the worker never reached. <code>gaps</code> and <code>stopped_because</code> exist for this, but <code>gaps</code> is self-reported by the model that ran out of budget. The orchestrator must treat <code>budget</code> as incomplete, either re-dispatching once with the gaps as the new objective or printing them in the report, and the cost table has room for only one of those per report at the 6 x 15 setting.

## Follow-ups

- *Finance halves the cap to $0.75 next quarter. Which half of the design do you cut: the worker count, the call budget, or the Opus orchestrator, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants code to fix the plan per question type (always three workers for a three-vendor comparison), the other wants the orchestrator model to plan. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Run both on the past questions and compare cost variance, duplicated searches and the analysts' gap counts; the code plan wins on predictability, so the model plan has to win on coverage to justify itself.
- *Sonnet 5.5 is retired in sixty days and its replacement uses more output tokens per call on your traces. What do you re-measure first, and who signs off on the new budgets?*

## Common mistakes

- Delegating with one sentence of prose and no out-of-scope list, which Anthropic's post reports produces duplicated work.
- Enforcing budgets only in the prompt.
- Pricing a multi-turn worker as if each request carried only its newest tokens, or without caching.
- Checking that a URL resolves rather than that the quote is on the page.
- Treating a worker that stopped on budget as finished.
- Accepting the 12-worker request, or rejecting it, without the cost table.
