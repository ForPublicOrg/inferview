## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Concept, 30 minutes">
"Which actions should need a human's approval, and how do you keep approvals from becoming a reflex click?"
</Callout>

The interviewer gives you a case (scenario). A subscription business runs a support-operations agent for 14 operators. It reads tickets and customer email, queries a read replica, and can send email to the customer, issue refunds up to $1,000, change the customer's plan and close accounts. Today every write asks the operator. Two weeks of telemetry, per day:

| Action | Prompts | Approved | Median decision time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Send email | 300 | 99.1% | 0.9 s |
| Refund | 220 | 97.3% | 1.4 s |
| Change plan | 90 | 98.9% | 1.0 s |
| Close account | 6 | 83% | 11 s |
| Other writes (tags, notes) | 54 | 96% | 1.2 s |

Of the 220 refunds a day, 170 are under $50, 42 are from $50 to $250 and 8 are over $250. Of the 300 emails, 210 are an approved template sent unchanged. Plan changes take effect at the next billing date and can be reverted until then. The operations lead wants no prompts at all; the security lead wants every write approved. A compliance review in 30 days needs the policy in writing.

Decide which actions keep a human gate, what replaces the gate elsewhere, and how you will know the remaining gates still work.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which of these actions can be undone, by whom, and how long after.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the reasons operators gave on the 17% of account closures they rejected.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what the operator sees on the approval screen today, and who wrote that text.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A human gate belongs on an action that cannot be cheaply undone, whose effect leaves the system (money, a message, data to a new party), and where the person at the prompt knows something the policy does not. Where one of those fails, use a structural control instead: allow with a cap, hold with an undo window, or deny. Here that keeps the gate on account closures and on refunds above a threshold I set at $250, with per-customer caps so refunds cannot be split under it; plan changes and notes run without prompts because they are reversible; templated emails send, and free-text emails pass a content check and a short hold. Prompts fall from 670 a day to about 14. Then each remaining gate is measured: a class approved nearly every time in about a second is not a control, and planted wrong actions show whether operators still catch them.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The test for a gate.</strong> OpenAI's agent guide rates each tool by read or write access, reversibility, account permissions and financial impact, and sends the high-risk ones to a human. That is necessary and not sufficient: a gate also needs a person who can judge the action better than a rule can. The telemetry shows where that holds. Account closures take a median 11 seconds and operators reject 17% of them: people are reading, and the rejections are information. Emails take 0.9 seconds at 99.1% approved: nobody is reading, and Anthropic's published 93% approval rate for coding-agent prompts, with experienced users auto-approving about twice as often as new ones, says this drift is the normal outcome, not a training gap.</p>
<p><strong>Action by action.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><em>Close account</em>: keep the gate. Irreversible for the customer, and operators demonstrably add judgement. The 17% rejection rate is also a defect report against the agent, which proposes the wrong closure about one time in six; fixing that is separate from the gate.</li>
<li><em>Refund</em>: gate above a threshold, cap below it. The threshold is the quantity to choose. At $250 the gate sees 8 refunds a day; at $50 it sees 42 + 8 = 50. Below the threshold, a refund executes if the customer's auto-approved refunds over 30 days stay under the same threshold and the day's total stays under a cap set from last quarter's 99th-percentile daily total. Without the per-customer cap, a threshold invites splitting: five refunds of $240 each pass a $250 gate.</li>
<li><em>Change plan</em> and <em>other writes</em>: no gate. Reversible until billing, and the reversal is an action an operator or the customer can take. Log them and sample.</li>
<li><em>Send email</em>: templated sends go without a gate. Free text is the hard case, because the agent writes it after reading customer email, which is where injected instructions arrive.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>The decision with two defensible answers: free-text email.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><em>Keep a gate on all 90 free-text emails a day.</em> Every irreversible message gets a human. The cost: 90 prompts a day on a class operators already approve at 99.1% in under a second; the gate will be as reflexive tomorrow as it is today, so it buys the appearance of review.</li>
<li><em>Content check plus hold.</em> Free text that contains no link, no attachment, no account data beyond the customer's own and no promise of money is held 10 minutes, then sent, with one in ten sampled for review after the fact; anything the check flags goes to the gate. The cost: some bad emails go out, bounded to what the check lets through, and the check has to be maintained as the agent's writing changes.</li>
</ul>
<p>I pick the second. With it and a $250 threshold, gates fall from 670 a day to 6 + 8 = 14 plus whatever the email check flags, about one per operator per day. With a $50 threshold it is 6 + 50 = 56, four per operator. Both are defensible; $50 puts more money behind a human, $250 makes each prompt rarer and more likely to be read. The conflict is the operations lead's autonomy against the security lead's control, settled by where a human adds information, and written down before the compliance review.</p>
<p><strong>What the approval screen shows.</strong> Rendered from the tool call's arguments, not from the agent's description of them: the amount, the customer, the refunds that customer received in the last 90 days, and the ticket text that led to the action, marked as customer-written. An agent steered by an injected instruction would also write a misleading summary; the arguments are what will execute. Friction scales with risk: a closure asks the operator to choose a reason, not to click Allow.</p>
<p><strong>A classifier as a middle tier.</strong> Anthropic's auto mode puts a classifier where prompts were, and its published numbers say where it fits: 0.4% false positives on real traffic, and 17% of real overeager actions let through. That is good enough to replace a reflexive prompt on reversible actions and not good enough to replace the gate on a closure, which the vendor itself says of high-stakes infrastructure. Copy its escalation rule as well: after a run of blocks, the agent stops and asks a person rather than trying variations.</p>
<p><strong>Proving a gate still works.</strong> Per class, every week: approval rate, median decision time, and the rejection reasons. Then planted errors: about one prompt in fifty is a deliberately wrong action that will never execute (a refund to the wrong customer, a closure on an active account), and the catch rate is the measure of the gate. A class whose approval rate stays above 98% with decisions under two seconds, or whose operators catch fewer than 80% of planted errors over a quarter, gets redesigned: automate it with a cap, or make the screen ask for a decision instead of a click. Those thresholds are my choice; anything from 95% to 99% on approval and from 70% to 90% on catch rate is reasonable, as long as the threshold is written down before the data arrives.</p>
<p><strong>Where this fails.</strong> Rare gates are unfamiliar gates. At one prompt per operator per day, an operator sees a refund over $250 a few times a week, and the first planted error may show that rare prompts are approved as fast as common ones were, for the opposite reason: no practised judgement. The other failure is the cap. A customer, or an injected instruction in a customer's email, can stay under every cap for months and collect up to the threshold every 30 days; the caps bound the loss, they do not detect it, so refunds without a matching product fault need a weekly review.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why not put the auto-mode-style classifier on refunds and drop the gate entirely?</em> On reversible actions the classifier's miss rate costs a reversal; on a refund the money is gone, and the published miss rate on overeager actions is one in six.</li>
<li><em>What if planted errors annoy operators?</em> Tell them the programme exists and how often, as with phishing drills; telling them does not make reading optional.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the operations lead accepts everything except the closure gate, because closures wait up to an hour for a free operator and customers complain. What do you cut instead, and what risk do you accept in writing if you lose?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see over a quarter to raise the refund threshold from $250 to $1,000?* Planted-error catch rates holding, refund disputes on auto-approved refunds no higher than on gated ones, and no splitting pattern in the weekly review.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants every email gated because email is the injection channel, the other says a gate at 99.1% approval is no gate. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Plant injected instructions in a sample of test tickets and measure how many reach a sent email under each design.
- *The compliance reviewer asks you to prove that a human approved every refund over $250 last month. What does the log need to contain, and what does "approved" mean if the decision took 0.8 seconds?*

## Common mistakes

- Gating every write, so the gates on dangerous actions are approved as fast as the harmless ones.
- Removing all gates because the approval rate is high, without replacing them with caps, holds or undo.
- Showing the agent's summary of the action on the approval screen instead of the arguments that will execute.
- Setting a refund threshold with no per-customer cap, which invites splitting.
- Measuring a gate by its approval rate alone and never testing whether reviewers catch a wrong action.
