## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"If each of 10 steps succeeds 95% of the time, what is end-to-end success? And how reliable is your agent, really?"
</Callout>

Warm-up, worked for you: ten independent steps at 95% each succeed together 0.95 to the power 10 = 0.599 of the time, so about 40% of runs fail somewhere.

Now the real question. You ran your support agent 8 times on the same "change my flight" task. It succeeded 6 times. A customer will hit this task 4 times over the next month. Estimate pass^4, the probability that all 4 of the customer's attempts succeed, as a percentage, using the unbiased estimator from tau-bench.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>pass^k is "all k succeed", the opposite end from pass@k, "at least one of k succeeds".</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>tau-bench estimates it as the number of ways to choose k successes from your c successes, divided by the number of ways to choose k trials from all n.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>C(6, 4) = 15 and C(8, 4) = 70.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>About 21%. pass^4 is estimated as C(6, 4) / C(8, 4) = 15 / 70 = 0.214. A customer who hits this task four times has roughly a one-in-five chance of every attempt working, even though the single-run success rate looks like 75%.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The estimator. From n trials with c successes, tau-bench estimates pass^k as C(c, k) / C(n, k): pick k of your n recorded trials at random without replacement, and ask how often all k are successes. With n = 8, c = 6, k = 4: 15 / 70 = 0.214.</p>
<p>The tempting shortcut. Plugging the success rate into a power, 0.75 to the power 4, gives 0.316. That is the right formula if you knew the true success rate, but with eight trials the plug-in estimate is biased upward; the unbiased version is lower.</p>
<p>The contrast. pass@4, at least one success in four, is 1 here: with only two failures in eight, any four trials include a success. Reporting pass@k for a customer-facing agent hides exactly the unreliability customers feel.</p>
<p>What to do. Report pass^k next to the average, run more trials per task so the estimate is stable, and find the failure modes in the two failed traces; for a multi-step agent, a verification step before the irreversible action is a lever to try before changing models.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>How many trials do you need?</em> Enough that adding a few more barely moves the estimate for the tasks you care about. Eight per task gives a coarse estimate; report it with that caveat.</li>
<li><em>Why are steps not really independent?</em> One wrong assumption early breaks every later step, and a good agent can recover from a bad step. Independence is a first approximation.</li>
<li><em>How does this change for a multi-agent system?</em> Each agent's steps multiply in, and handoffs add failure modes of their own, so end-to-end pass^k is the number to track.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

Worked out in full: C(6, 4) = 6! / (4! × 2!) = 15 and C(8, 4) = 8! / (4! × 4!) = 70, so the estimate is 15 / 70 = 0.214, or 21.4%. Any answer from 19% to 24% counts. The plug-in answer of 31.6% falls outside the band on purpose: the point of the question is to notice that four-in-a-row reliability is much lower than single-run accuracy, and that small samples flatter you.

The warm-up matters for the same reason. Adding failure rates (10 × 5% = 50%) overstates failure; multiplying success rates gives 59.9% success, 40.1% failure. Either way, a 95% step is not a 95% agent.

## Follow-ups

- *What would you change to raise pass^4?* Look at the failures: if both failed at the same step, fix that tool or prompt; if they failed at random steps, add a verification step before the irreversible action.
- *How would you report this to a product manager?* "One run succeeds about 3 times in 4; a customer who does this 4 times has about a 1 in 5 chance of it always working."
- *pass@k has its own unbiased estimator. What is it?* 1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k): the chance that k trials drawn from your n are not all failures.

## Common mistakes

- Reporting pass@k for a customer-facing agent.
- Using the plug-in estimate (success rate to the power k) on a handful of trials.
- Adding per-step failure rates instead of multiplying success rates.
- Treating eight trials as a precise measurement.
