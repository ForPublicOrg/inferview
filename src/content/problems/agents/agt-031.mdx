## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 30 minutes">
"Your browser agent reads untrusted pages all day. Using a published attack success rate, how many successful prompt injections should you expect a month, and what does that number do to the scopes you grant?"
</Callout>

The product (scenario): an enterprise browser agent on Claude Opus 4.7.

- 50,000 users run 20 tasks a month each; a task reads 10 pages on average.
- The security team sampled 20,000 page reads from last month's logs. Two reviewers found 6 pages carrying text addressed to an AI agent that tried to change its task.
- The mail connector is linked, and on by default, in 35% of tasks. Task labels show 5% of tasks need it.

Published rates, each the vendor's own measurement:

| Source | Model and condition | Attack success |
|---|---|---|
| Anthropic, 2026-05-25 | Opus 4.7, Gray Swan Agent Red Teaming, single attempt | about 0.1% |
| Anthropic, 2026-05-25 | Opus 4.7, same benchmark, after 100 adaptive attempts | about 5 to 6% |
| Anthropic, 2025-11-24 | Opus 4.5 in Chrome with safeguards, adaptive Best-of-N attacker, 100 attempts per environment, per attack encountered | 1.4% |

Choose the rate and the prevalence you will use and defend them. Estimate successful injections a month, then how many of those land in a task with the mail connector on, today and if the connector were on only when the task needs it.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether the 6 injected pages looked aimed at your product or were generic text left for any agent.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask each vendor figure what the denominator is: one attack, one environment, or one attacker's campaign.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Request whether the sample of 20,000 included pages read from the user's own inbox.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Page reads: 50,000 × 20 × 10 = 10 million a month. Prevalence from the sample: 6 / 20,000 = 0.03%, so about 3,000 injected pages a month. At the single-attempt rate of about 0.1% that is 3 successful injections a month; with the 1.4% adaptive figure as a conservative rate it is 42; with the upper confidence bound on prevalence, 6 and 83. With the mail connector on in 35% of tasks, 1 to 29 a month of those land where a mailbox is reachable; on only when needed, 0.15 to 4. The dominant term is the per-encounter rate, which the published figures put more than a factor of ten apart. No published rate gets connector-exposed successes under one a year with today's defaults, so scopes, not the model, have to bound the damage.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Exposure.</strong> 50,000 users × 20 tasks × 10 pages = 10,000,000 page reads a month. The unit is the page read, not the task: each page is a separate chance to meet an injection.</p>
<p><strong>Prevalence, the first choice.</strong> The point estimate is 6 / 20,000 = 0.03%, which gives 10,000,000 × 0.0003 = 3,000 injected page reads a month. Six events is a small count. The one-sided 95% upper bound for a Poisson count of 6 is about 11.8, so prevalence could be as high as 11.8 / 20,000 ≈ 0.059%, or about 5,900 injected reads. Either is defensible: the point estimate for planning, the bound for a risk note.</p>
<p><strong>Rate, the second choice, and the dominant term.</strong> The three published figures answer different questions.</p>
<ul>
<li><em>About 0.1%, single attempt, the model we run.</em> A generic injection on a random page is one attempt by an attacker who never saw our agent. This is the closest match for most of the 3,000.</li>
<li><em>1.4%, Opus 4.5 under an adaptive Best-of-N attacker, per attack encountered.</em> An older model under a stronger attacker. Using it treats every injected page as the work of an attacker who iterated against the model, which is conservative and defensible for a risk note.</li>
<li><em>About 5 to 6% after 100 adaptive attempts.</em> The chance that a campaign of 100 tries lands at least once. It is a per-campaign figure, and applying it per page is the unreasonable choice.</li>
</ul>

<p>Reasonable answers run from about 3 to about 83 a month. The rate moves the answer by a factor of 14 and the prevalence by a factor of 2, so the rate is the dominant term, and the assumption that flips the conclusion is which attacker the pages represent.</p>
<p><strong>What it does to scopes.</strong> Ask what rate would make today's defaults acceptable, taking "acceptable" as fewer than one connector-exposed success a year: (1 / 12) / (3,000 × 0.35) ≈ 0.008% per encounter, more than ten times below the best published figure. With the connector on only when needed, the bar is (1 / 12) / (3,000 × 0.05) ≈ 0.056%, which the single-attempt rate still misses. So scoping alone does not reach the bar either, and the remaining factor has to come from a second layer: confirmation before any send, rendered from the action rather than the agent's description, and egress limits so a success cannot reach an attacker's host. That matches what both vendors publish: OpenAI says prompt injection is unlikely ever to be fully solved and advises turning off connectors a task does not need and using logged-out mode where possible.</p>
<p>The decision has two defensible answers. Turning the connector off by default and enabling it per task cuts exposure by 35 / 5 = 7 times and costs friction for the 5% of tasks that need mail, plus some tasks that fail because the user did not enable it. Keeping it on, read-only, with every send confirmed keeps the product smooth and costs a mailbox's contents being readable by every injected task; a success that reads and leaks through some channel the confirmation does not cover is still a breach. I would turn it off by default, because 7 times fewer exposed tasks is a reduction no model update has published.</p>
<p><strong>Where this estimate fails.</strong> The sample is of pages the agent chose to read. An attacker who wants these users does not wait to be found: an email to the user puts an injection straight into any task that reads the inbox, with a success rate from the adaptive rows, not the single-attempt row. The 6-in-20,000 prevalence says nothing about that channel, and the security team should sample inbox reads separately before this number goes in a risk note.</p>

### Choices that are not reasonable

- Using the 5 to 6% after 100 attempts as a per-page rate: 3,000 × 0.055 ≈ 165, a campaign success rate applied to single encounters.
- Counting tasks instead of page reads: 1,000,000 × 0.0003 × 0.001 = 0.3 a month, which hides nine of every ten encounters.
- Taking 0% because no successful injection has been reported yet: with this volume, the absence of reports measures detection, not attacks.
- Using a model-alone figure (Opus 4.5 at 8.0%) for a product that runs the vendor's safeguards, or the reverse.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: product says turning the mail connector off by default will cut weekly active use, and asks you to keep it on and rely on the model. What do you accept in writing, and which number in your table do you put next to your name?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see to turn the connector back on by default?* A published single-attempt rate under the 0.008% bar for the model you run, or measured confirmation and egress controls that stop the leaks the table counts, tested with planted injections.
- *Operating: the vendor retires Opus 4.7 in sixty days, and the replacement has no published injection figure yet. Do you ship it on schedule, and what do you measure in the first week?*

## Common mistakes

- Quoting a vendor's attack rate without its denominator or attacker model.
- Treating a small rate as small risk without multiplying by exposure.
- Estimating successes and stopping there, without asking which of them can reach private data or an outbound channel.
- Using the sampled prevalence as if attackers who target the product were in the sample.
