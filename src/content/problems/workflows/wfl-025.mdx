## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The round">
"Accounts payable wants an agent that reads invoices, matches them to purchase orders and releases the payments. Design the approval part: what the reviewer sees, timeouts and escalation, four-eyes rules and replay protection."
</Callout>

The operating facts are a scenario for this problem:

- About 1,200 payments a day. In a two-week pilot a reviewer took 90 seconds per payment on average.
- Five finance reviewers, each with about six hours a day for reviews.
- Payment value: roughly 70% of payments are under $1,000, 25% between $1,000 and $25,000, and 5% above $25,000.
- Payments leave in the ERP's daily payment run, whose bank file closes at 16:00. The ERP cannot be changed; the agent releases a payment through the ERP's existing API, which accepts a client reference on each release.
- The controller wants two approvers on every payment. The CFO wants most payments to need no one.

Produce the approval design and a one-paragraph risk note the controller could sign. Choose the amount bands yourself and show that they fit the reviewers' day.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Approve the payment object, not the agent's description. The reviewer sees the canonical ERP payment, the bank-detail history and the match result, and the approval is bound to a hash of the exact fields that will be released. Bands: below a threshold, payments with a clean three-way match and unchanged bank details are released by policy; above it one reviewer; at the top two. Timeouts escalate, then defer to the next run; nothing is approved by silence. Each approval is single-use, the release carries the payment ID as its reference, and an uncertain release is reconciled, never retried blind.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The capacity arithmetic decides the shape. Five reviewers with six hours each is 30 hours, or 108,000 seconds; at 90 seconds per review that is 1,200 reviews a day. One review per payment uses the whole team every day. Two per payment needs 2,400 reviews, double the capacity. The controller's rule cannot be staffed, and the CFO's rule removes the human check that OpenAI's guide on building agents names for payments.</p>
<p>With a policy band under $1,000, one reviewer from $1,000 to $25,000 and two above: 300 single reviews plus 60 payments times two is 420 reviews, or 37,800 seconds, 10.5 hours. That leaves room for policy-band payments that fail their match and drop into review, and for peak days.</p>
<p>What I give up: payments under $1,000 with a clean match go out unseen. The controls on that band are the match, a bank-detail freshness rule, a per-vendor daily cap so that splitting one invoice into several small ones does not stay under the threshold, and a weekly sample reviewed after the fact.</p>
</Fragment>

### What the reviewer sees

The card is built from the ERP's payment record, the record that will be released, never from the agent's output. Payee legal name, bank name and the last four digits of the account, the date the bank details last changed and who changed them, amount, currency, value date, the invoice image, the purchase order and goods receipt match result with any variance, and this vendor's last few payments for comparison. The agent's rationale appears below the facts, labelled as written by the model, with its text escaped; the OpenAI Agents SDK docs treat tool arguments as untrusted display content, and an invoice is input from outside the company.

The approval record stores a hash of payee, account, amount, currency and value date. If any of them changes between approval and release, the approval is void and the payment returns to the queue. Approving a sentence and releasing different numbers is the failure this prevents.

### Four-eyes rules

Identity comes from the single sign-on session. The approver may not be the person who uploaded or edited the invoice, nor the person who last changed the vendor's bank details. For dual approval the two approvers are different people and one holds the controller's delegation. The agent's tools can read vendor records but cannot edit bank details at all: a model that could change the account and propose the payment would be the requester for both halves.

A payment whose bank details changed within a set window leaves the policy band and needs a reviewer whatever the amount, and the card shows the change first. The window is a quantity to defend: it should cover the time it takes a vendor to notice a payment they did not receive, so a month is reasonable and a day is not.

### Timeouts and escalation

At a set time before the cut-off (for example 14:00), unapproved payments go to the backup approver and the reviewer's lead. Half an hour before the cut-off anything still pending is deferred to the next payment run and the vendor contact is told if the payment is due. Nothing is approved because it waited. The cost is late payments on busy days, so the design lets finance mark critical vendors whose payments are reviewed first, not approved automatically.

### Replay protection

An approval is a row that moves from `pending` to `approved` by compare-and-set and from `approved` to `released` when the release call is made; it is valid for one payment run. A second click, a retried request or an old link finds no row in the right state. The release call carries the payment ID as the client reference. Before releasing, the step asks the ERP whether the payment was already released, the check-before-act order the Cloudflare Workflows rules give for a charge, because a workflow step may run more than once. The OpenAI Agents SDK docs say approval does not give exactly-once execution; when a release times out and the ERP cannot say whether it went, the payment is marked unknown and reconciled against the bank file, and no retry fires on its own.

### The risk note

"From go-live, payments under $1,000 whose invoice matches its purchase order and goods receipt, whose vendor bank details have not changed in 30 days, and whose vendor has received less than a set daily total are released without a reviewer. We accept the risk of a fraudulent or mistaken payment in that band from a vendor whose own systems are compromised. Each week finance reviews a sample of these payments after release. The band is suspended on any confirmed fraud in it, or if the share of policy-band payments that fail matching changes sharply from the pilot."

### Where this design fails

Rubber-stamping. Four hundred reviews a day by five people, each with the model's rationale on screen, drifts toward approving what the rationale says, and in dual approval both people read the same rationale, so their errors are correlated. Measure it: approval rate per reviewer, time per review, and a small number of known-bad payments placed in the queue by finance, with the result reported to the controller. If the second approver keeps agreeing in a few seconds, show them the facts with the rationale hidden.

## Follow-ups

- *Your verification costs more reviewer time than the team has at quarter end. Which band do you widen for those three days, and who signs that in writing?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants the approval bound to the field hash, the other to an ERP record version number. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*
- *The ERP vendor announces that the release API will stop accepting client references in a year. What changes in your replay protection?*

## Common mistakes

- Showing the reviewer the agent's summary as the main content. It is the one field an attacker who wrote the invoice controls.
- Auto-approving on timeout to protect the payment run.
- Treating the approval as the idempotency key for the payment. A payment that is re-proposed and approved again gets a new approval; the payment ID does not change.
- Leaving bank-detail changes outside the design. Redirecting a payment needs only a changed account, and that change is not on the invoice the reviewer reads.
