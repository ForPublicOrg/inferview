## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"We extract product attributes with a model and want people to check only the doubtful ones. We have no labels. How do you score confidence, where do you put the threshold, and what does that threshold cost?"
</Callout>

The situation (scenario): a marketplace extracts 30 attributes (size, sheet count, allergens, organic claim, colour and so on) from product text and images. The pipeline produces 400,000 values a week. Auditors check a value against the listing in about 20 seconds. Nothing has been labelled yet, and the ops lead wants a staffing number by Friday.

Explain how you would score each value, how you would choose the threshold below which a value goes to an auditor, and what your threshold costs in auditor hours a week.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether the model API you use returns log probabilities for the first output token.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask what one wrong accepted value costs for each attribute: a wrong allergen against a wrong colour.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Request the auditors' measured seconds per value for two or three attributes, not one average.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Without labels you can rank values but not choose a threshold. Score with a second, yes-or-no verification call and take the probability of "yes" on the first token, as Instacart's PARSE does, with rule checks (format, units, allowed values) as a veto. Then buy a small labelled sample, stratified by score, and pick the threshold from the review-rate against error-rate curve for each high-risk attribute. At 20 seconds a value, review costs 400,000 × r × 20 / 3,600 hours a week: 333 hours (about eight auditors) at a 15 percent review rate, 111 hours at 5 percent. The threshold is a staffing decision.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Scoring without labels, three signals. A verification call: give the model the source and the extracted value and ask whether the value is correct, answering yes or no first; read the first token's log probabilities, add up the variants of yes and of no, and renormalise. It costs a second call per value, and it needs an API that returns token log probabilities. Agreement: extract k times or with two models and score by agreement; it costs k calls and gives coarse scores with many ties. Rules: format, units, allowed sets, cross-field checks such as a sheet count consistent with the pack size; they are almost free and catch a different class of error. Rules veto; the verification score orders what passes.</p>
<p>Why labels are still needed. A score orders values; it does not say what share of values above 0.9 are wrong. Choosing a threshold for a target error rate needs that number. So label a sample, stratified by score bucket: 10 buckets × 60 values = 600 labels, at 20 seconds each about 3.3 auditor hours. That is the quantity to choose and defend: more labels per bucket narrows the estimate; more attributes with their own sample cost the same again each.</p>
<p>Reading the curve. For each candidate threshold, the review rate is the share below it and the error rate is the share of wrong values above it. Pick the lowest threshold whose accepted error rate meets the target. A check on the accepted side: with zero errors among n audited accepted values, the 95 percent upper bound is about 3 / n, so a 1 percent target needs about 300 clean audited values.</p>
<p>The cost. 400,000 × 15% = 60,000 reviews × 20 s = 333 hours a week, about 8.3 auditors at 40 hours. At 5 percent: 111 hours, 2.8 auditors. Where the curve sits between those decides the headcount.</p>
<p>The constraint conflict. Thirty attributes with their own thresholds need 30 × 600 = 18,000 labels, 100 auditor hours, before the first threshold ships. Per-attribute samples for the attributes where a wrong value is expensive (allergens, organic claims) and one shared threshold for the long tail is the compromise.</p>
<p>The failure mode. The threshold is fitted to one model and one prompt. A model or prompt change moves the score distribution, and the old threshold quietly accepts more errors. Keep the labelled sample as a fixed set, re-score it on every change and re-pick the threshold before the change ships. A second one: a verifier that sees only text says no to a value printed only on the packaging (Instacart's example is a sheet count visible in the image and absent from the text), which inflates the review queue with correct values.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why not ask the model for a confidence number in the output?</em> You can, but until it is checked against labels it is another uncalibrated score, and it costs no less to check.</li>
<li><em>The API you use returns no log probabilities. What now?</em> Agreement across samples or models, plus rules; budget the extra calls and expect coarser thresholds.</li>
<li><em>Does the same model make a good verifier of its own output?</em> It shares the extractor's blind spots; a different prompt, input (image as well as text) or model reduces that, at a cost per call.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

Accuracy figures published by others do not transfer as thresholds. Uber reports 90 percent overall accuracy on invoices, yet only 35 percent of invoices at 99.5 percent and 65 percent above 80 percent, so the per-item spread matters more than the average when sizing a queue.

## Follow-ups

- *The ops lead can staff two auditors, not eight. What changes, and what do you put in writing?* Two auditors are about 80 hours, a 3.6 percent review rate at 20 seconds; the threshold drops, the expected accepted error rate rises, and the written risk names that rate per attribute and who accepted it.
- *What would make you drop the verification call?* Labelled evidence that rules plus agreement reach the same review-rate and error-rate point at lower cost per value.
- *Next month's model update shifts every score up by a few points. How do you notice before customers do?* The fixed labelled set re-scored on every change, plus a weekly audit of accepted values whose error rate is charted against the target.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the threshold as a round number (0.9) instead of from a labelled curve.
- Quoting a review rate without the hours it costs.
- One threshold for every attribute, when the cost of a wrong value differs by attribute.
- Treating the verifier's probability as a calibrated probability without checking it against labels.
