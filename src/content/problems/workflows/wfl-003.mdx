## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimation">
"Here are offline numbers for two designs of the same ticket pipeline. Which one ships?"
</Callout>

Every number below is a scenario, measured on the team's own labelled tickets. Per-step figures are the share of tickets the step handles correctly, the p50 latency and the cost per call.

**Design A, a six-step chain.** Every ticket goes through all six steps.

| Step | Success | p50 latency | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 extract | 0.99 | 0.8 s | $0.004 |
| A2 summarise | 0.98 | 1.2 s | $0.006 |
| A3 policy lookup | 0.97 | 1.5 s | $0.009 |
| A4 entitlement check | 0.99 | 0.9 s | $0.003 |
| A5 draft | 0.98 | 1.1 s | $0.005 |
| A6 format | 0.99 | 0.6 s | $0.002 |

**Design B, a router and two branches.** A router (0.4 s, $0.001) sends each ticket to the billing or the technical branch. On 400 labelled tickets it routed 95 percent correctly; treat a misrouted ticket as failed.

| Branch step | Success | p50 latency | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Billing B1 extract | 0.99 | 1.0 s | $0.005 |
| Billing B2 draft | 0.98 | 1.3 s | $0.006 |
| Technical T1 extract | 0.98 | 1.2 s | $0.006 |
| Technical T2a diagnose, in parallel with T2b | 0.97 | 1.5 s | $0.009 |
| Technical T2b entitlement, in parallel with T2a | 0.99 | 0.9 s | $0.004 |
| Technical T3 draft | 0.99 | 0.7 s | $0.003 |

Last quarter's labels put the billing share of tickets anywhere from 55 to 75 percent depending on the month. Pick a billing share and defend it.

Estimate end-to-end success, typical latency and cost per ticket for both designs. The self-check takes design B's end-to-end success, in percent.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether the per-step failures were measured on the same tickets, and whether the tickets that fail one step tend to fail the others.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask what the 95 percent routing figure would look like on 400 different tickets.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>With a 65 percent billing share and independent step failures: A succeeds on about 90.4 percent of tickets, takes about 6.1 s and costs $0.029; B succeeds on about 90.9 percent, takes about 3.1 s and costs about $0.016. Success is a tie inside the measurement error of the router, so B ships on latency and cost. The dominant term in B's success is the router's 0.95; below about 94.5 percent routing accuracy, A wins on success.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Design A.</strong> Success is the product of the six steps: 0.99 × 0.98 × 0.97 × 0.99 × 0.98 × 0.99 = 0.904. Latency is the sum, 0.8 + 1.2 + 1.5 + 0.9 + 1.1 + 0.6 = 6.1 s. Cost is the sum, $0.029.</p>
<p><strong>Design B, per branch.</strong> Billing: 0.99 × 0.98 = 0.970, latency 1.0 + 1.3 = 2.3 s, cost $0.011. Technical: 0.98 × 0.97 × 0.99 × 0.99 = 0.932 (both parallel steps must succeed), latency 1.2 + max(1.5, 0.9) + 0.7 = 3.4 s, cost $0.006 + $0.009 + $0.004 + $0.003 = $0.022.</p>
<p><strong>Design B, end to end, with billing share b.</strong> Success = 0.95 × (b × 0.970 + (1 − b) × 0.932). At b = 0.65: 0.95 × (0.631 + 0.326) = 0.95 × 0.957 = 0.909. Across the stated range, b = 0.55 gives 0.905 and b = 0.75 gives 0.913. Latency at b = 0.65: 0.4 + 0.65 × 2.3 + 0.35 × 3.4 = 3.1 s. Cost: $0.001 + 0.65 × $0.011 + 0.35 × $0.022 = $0.016, about 55 percent of A.</p>
<p><strong>Choosing b.</strong> Any share in the measured 55 to 75 percent range is reasonable, and the answer barely moves across it (90.5 to 91.3 percent), which is itself worth saying. A 50/50 split ignores data you have; using the best month alone picks the most flattering number.</p>
<p><strong>The dominant term and the flip.</strong> The router multiplies everything in B, so its accuracy dominates B's success. B matches A when 0.95 is replaced by 0.904 / 0.957 = 0.945 at b = 0.65 (0.941 to 0.949 across the range). The 95 percent came from 400 tickets; its standard error is √(0.95 × 0.05 / 400) ≈ 0.011, so a 95 percent interval runs from about 92.9 to 97.1 percent, which contains the break-even. On success alone the data cannot separate the designs. B ships because it halves latency and cuts cost by about 45 percent at equal measured success, and the decision flips if production routing accuracy settles below about 94 percent and a failed ticket costs more than the latency and spend B saves.</p>
<p><strong>What the arithmetic hides.</strong> Multiplying step success assumes failures are independent. They usually are not: a garbled ticket fails extraction and then everything after it, so the product understates both designs' success, and A, with more steps, more so. Summing p50 latencies is not the p50 of the sum, and the max of two parallel p50s understates the p50 of the max, so measure B's technical branch end to end rather than trusting 3.4 s. Misroutes may also not be uniform: if they fall mostly on technical tickets, B's technical success is lower than the product says.</p>
</Fragment>

Answers from 90 to 92 percent count. The common wrong answers are 95.7 percent, which forgets the router's misroutes, and 85.9 percent, which multiplies the router by both branches as if every ticket ran through both.

## Follow-ups

- *Add a deterministic gate after A3 that catches half of its failures and retries once with the same success rate. What happens to A's success?* A3's effective success becomes 0.97 + 0.03 × 0.5 × 0.97 = 0.985, and A rises to 0.904 / 0.97 × 0.985 = 0.918, ahead of B; the retried tickets pay another 1.5 s, which moves A's tail latency more than its median.
- *Pushback: product requires p95 under 4 seconds. A cannot meet it and B's technical branch is close. Which half of B's checking do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?* Measure the branch's p95 first; if it is over, the candidate cut is T2b (entitlement) moving to a cached lookup in code, accepting stale entitlement for the cache lifetime and writing that window down.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in production to switch back to A?* Routing accuracy on a fresh labelled sample below the break-even, or misroutes concentrated on a class where a failure is expensive.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving the router's accuracy out of B's success.
- Adding the parallel steps' latencies instead of taking the slower one.
- Treating a 95 percent accuracy from 400 tickets as exact and declaring a winner on a half-point difference.
- Comparing designs on success alone when they tie and differ by half on latency and cost.
