## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round">
"Design an agentic system that takes a one-page premise and produces a one-hour film."
</Callout>

The scenario's numbers, invented for this problem: the licensed video model produces clips of at most 10 seconds and charges $0.40 per generated second; a 5-second clip takes about 2 minutes to generate, and the account allows 40 generations at once. The production budget is $12,000 per film for everything (video, audio, model calls for writing and judging), and a film is due five days after the premise arrives. The director insists on approving every scene before the next one is built on it, and has about four hours a day for review.

Design the orchestration, the state that keeps characters, places and story consistent across an hour, the quality gates, and what happens when generation fails. Choose how many takes you budget per shot and defend it. Say what you give up, and write the decision memo you would send the director.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask how the video model takes conditioning: a reference image, the last frame of the previous clip, or text only.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the share of generated clips a human rejected in the last pilot, split by shot type.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what the director wants to see at each approval: a script, a storyboard or a cut.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Build it as a workflow with model steps and bounded orchestration, not a free agent: premise to treatment to scene list to shot list, each a structured artefact the director can approve, then generation and gates per shot. A story bible (characters with reference sheets, locations, wardrobe per scene, timeline) is the continuity state every prompt reads. Budget about four takes per shot on average, more for hard shots, from the cost arithmetic. Human review, not compute, is the bottleneck, so the director approves scripts and storyboards before any video is generated and then approves cuts per scene.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The budget arithmetic.</strong> One hour is 3,600 seconds, at least 360 clips at 10 seconds; with an average shot of 5 seconds it is about 720 shots. If each accepted second costs k generated seconds, video costs 3,600 × k × $0.40 = $1,440 × k. Holding back 20 percent of $12,000 for audio, writing and judge calls leaves $9,600, so k can be at most 6.7. A budget of 4 takes per shot on average ($5,760) leaves room for re-shooting whole scenes after the director rejects them. Reasonable choices sit between about 3 and 6; fewer than 2 assumes a first-take acceptance the pilot data will not show, and more than about 6.7 overspends before any scene is redone. Allocate takes by shot type rather than evenly: a static establishing shot may need one or two, a two-character dialogue close-up with hands and faces many more.</p>
<p><strong>Orchestration.</strong> The writing half is a chain with human gates: premise, treatment, scene list with durations that sum to 60 minutes, then a shot list per scene with camera, characters, location, action and duration. Each artefact is model output validated in code (durations sum, every character in a shot exists in the bible, every location has a reference) before the director sees it. The production half is orchestrator-workers per scene: an orchestrator turns the approved shot list into generation jobs, workers generate takes, gates choose among them. The orchestrator may re-plan a shot (simpler framing, fewer characters) after its takes fail, but it may not change an approved script; that boundary keeps the director's approvals meaningful.</p>
<p><strong>Continuity state.</strong> A versioned story bible: each character's reference images and description, wardrobe per scene, each location's reference, props, time of day, and a timeline of what each character knows. Every shot job is built from the bible plus the shot list, never from the conversation so far, so a long run does not drift with its context. Accepted shots write back their last frame and any new facts (a torn sleeve, a broken window).</p>
<p><strong>The decision with two defensible answers.</strong> Within a scene, generate shots in sequence, conditioning each on the previous accepted shot's last frame; or generate all shots in parallel from the bible's reference images. Sequential gives better continuity of lighting, position and wardrobe, but a bad accepted shot propagates, and a one-minute scene of 5-second shots, 12 of them, takes 12 rounds of about 2 minutes plus gating. Parallel finishes a scene in one round and lets a failed shot be redone alone, at the cost of more drift between cuts. Generation is not the binding constraint: 720 shots × 4 takes × 2 minutes ÷ 40 concurrent generations is 144 minutes for the whole film. So sequence within a scene for dialogue and continuous action, and use parallel generation across scenes and for cutaways.</p>
<p><strong>Gates, in order of cost.</strong> Code checks first: duration, resolution, no black or frozen frames. Then model checks: face and identity similarity of each character to the bible's references, wardrobe and prop presence, and a vision model scoring each take against the shot description with a rubric. Then the director, per scene, on an assembled cut. Every shot has a take cap; when it is reached, the shot goes to re-plan once and then to a needs-human list rather than generating forever.</p>
<p><strong>Failure handling.</strong> Every artefact and accepted take is checkpointed, and a crash resumes from the last accepted shot, not the start of the scene. Provider errors retry with backoff and a per-scene spend cap; a scene that exhausts its cap pauses and asks the director whether to simplify it or move budget from another scene.</p>
<p><strong>Where this design fails.</strong> The gates reward what they can measure. Identity similarity and rubric scores favour slow, front-facing, low-motion shots, so a film that passes every gate can be consistent and lifeless; and a judge may rate a well-lit take highly while the story beat is missing. Track the share of accepted shots per shot type against the shot list and have the director rate pacing on the assembled cut, not only per shot.</p>
<p><strong>What you give up.</strong> Free-form re-plotting after a scene is approved, takes for low-value shots, and some speed, since the director's review gates the schedule.</p>
<p><strong>Written down: the memo to the director.</strong> "Decision: approve script and storyboard before any video; then one assembled cut per scene. Budget: about four takes per shot on average, weighted to dialogue scenes, with $2,400 held back for audio, writing and re-shoots. Within a scene, shots are generated in order, so a rejected shot can force regenerating the shots after it. Risk: automated checks favour static framing; please flag scenes that feel flat, not only those with errors. Review load: with scenes of about a minute, 60 scenes in five days at four hours a day is about 12 scenes a day, about 20 minutes each, so every scene approved late moves the delivery date."</p>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: verification (identity checks, rubric judging, the director's cuts) now takes more time than generation. Which half do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?* Keep the code checks and identity similarity, drop rubric judging for shots without dialogue or named characters, and write down that cutaways may carry continuity errors the director will catch only in the assembled cut.
- *Reversal: what would you need to see to drop sequential generation within scenes?* Pilot scenes where parallel generation from references gets director acceptance on the first cut as often as sequential, measured on the same shot types.
- *Two engineers disagree: one wants a single planning agent that writes, generates and revises freely; the other wants this staged pipeline. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Run both on two pilot premises with the same budget and compare director approvals on the first cut, spend per accepted minute and the number of approved decisions the agent later overturned.
- *The provider ships a new video model halfway through a film. Do you switch?* Not mid-film: the bible's references and accepted shots were produced by the old model, and mixing models inside a film is a continuity risk. Pin the version per film and evaluate the new one on the next premise.

## Common mistakes

- Letting the agent regenerate until a judge is satisfied, with no take cap or spend cap.
- Keeping continuity in the conversation history instead of a versioned bible every job reads.
- Spending the review budget on per-shot approvals and leaving no director time for assembled scenes.
- Assuming compute is the bottleneck without doing the generation arithmetic.
- Letting the orchestrator change an approved script.
