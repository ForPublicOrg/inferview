## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"One step in this workflow emails the customer, and you cannot unsend an email. Structure the workflow so that a failure in any later step still leaves the system consistent."
</Callout>

Scenario. A B2B onboarding workflow runs after a contract is signed:

1. Create the tenant (can be deleted).
2. Charge the first invoice (can be refunded).
3. A model drafts a welcome email with the login link and a summary of the contracted limits, and the workflow sends it to the customer's admin.
4. Provision a dedicated search index in the customer's region. Regions sometimes hit capacity, and provisioning can then take hours.
5. Configure single sign-on with the customer's identity provider. This fails when the customer's metadata is wrong, and only the customer can fix it.

Today the steps run in this order. Sales has promised customers the welcome email within five minutes of signature. Last month a region was at capacity for a day, and customers logged in through the emailed link to an empty workspace.

Restructure it. Say which steps can be undone, what happens after each possible failure, and what you trade away.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>For each step, ask what undoing it means and whether the customer would notice the undo.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask which later failures are permanent and which only take time.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask sales what the five-minute email has to contain for the promise to count.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Classify the steps: tenant creation and the charge can be compensated, the email cannot, provisioning eventually succeeds, and single sign-on can fail permanently. A saga can only roll back up to the email; past it, every step must go forward until it succeeds or a human takes over. So move everything that can fail permanently before the email, or make the early email promise only what is already true. I would split it: a five-minute email that says the contract is received and the workspace is being prepared, and the login email after provisioning succeeds. Single sign-on becomes a forward step with a customer-facing task and an escalation timer, never a reason to refund. The model-drafted content gets a check or an approval before sending, and the send gets a key, because the step can run twice.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The saga as Garcia-Molina and Salem define it.</strong> A long-running process is a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensating transaction; if step k fails, the compensations for steps k − 1 down to 1 run in reverse order. A compensation is an approximation, not a restore: a refund leaves a charge and a refund on the customer's statement. That is backward recovery. Forward recovery instead retries until the process reaches its final state.</p>
<p><strong>The email is the point of no return.</strong> Its "compensation" would be a second email saying "ignore the first", which is a new message to the customer, not an undo. Any step after it therefore cannot be compensated backwards without contradicting what the customer was told: refunding the invoice after a welcome email is a new event the customer sees, not a rollback. So the steps after the email must be forward-only: retried until they succeed, with a timer that escalates to a person when they do not.</p>
<p><strong>Applying that here.</strong> Provisioning is forward-recoverable: capacity returns. Single sign-on is not, because it depends on the customer, and the customer can sit on it for weeks. Order the workflow: create tenant, charge, provision (with retries and a deadline), send the login email, then single sign-on as a forward step that sends the customer a setup task and escalates to their account manager after a deadline. A failure before the login email compensates in reverse: refund, then delete the tenant. A failure after it never compensates; it escalates.</p>
<p><strong>The conflict with the five-minute promise.</strong> Provisioning can take hours, so "login email last" breaks sales' promise on exactly the days the region is full. Two defensible answers. One: keep the email last and renegotiate the promise. Cost: a commercial commitment changes, and most customers wait for no reason on normal days. Two: split the email. The five-minute email says only what is already true (contract received, workspace being prepared, what happens next), and the login email goes out when provisioning succeeds. Cost: two templates to maintain, and the first email's content must stay true even if the saga later compensates, so it may not contain limits or links. A third option, sending the login now and apologising later, is what produced last month's empty workspaces. I would pick the split, because it keeps the promise sales made and moves the irreversible commitment after the step that can stall.</p>
<p><strong>The model-drafted content.</strong> The email summarises contracted limits, and a model wrote it. Put the check before the irreversible step: compare the stated limits against the contract record, and route mismatches to a human approval. Approval does not make the send exactly-once; the step itself can run twice after a crash, as any durable step can, so the send needs an idempotency key from the tenant and template, or a sent-record checked before sending. In a LangGraph node, the send goes after the interrupt or in its own node, because a resumed node restarts from its beginning.</p>
<p><strong>The quantity you choose.</strong> The provisioning deadline before the workflow stops waiting and compensates. Too short, and a slow region refunds customers who would have been fine an hour later; too long, and a customer has paid for days with nothing to show. A reasonable choice is a deadline the account team can explain to a customer, with an escalation well before it, so a human decides whether to compensate.</p>
<p><strong>Where the recommended answer fails.</strong> The split assumes the early email can be written so that it is true whatever happens next; once marketing adds the contracted limits to it, the irreversible step has moved back to minute five. Forward-only steps after the login email can wait forever on the customer, so the workflow needs an owner and a closing state other than "done", or it accumulates open runs. And the compensation for the charge is itself an external call that can fail, so compensations need the same retries and keys as forward steps.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Pushback.</em> Sales says two emails look unprofessional and wants the single email back at minute five. What risk do you accept in writing, and what would you measure to revisit it?</li>
<li><em>Reversal.</em> What would you need to see in provisioning latency to move the login email back before provisioning?</li>
<li><em>Two senior engineers disagree.</em> One wants single sign-on failures to refund the invoice after 30 days; the other says a signed contract is never refunded by a workflow. How do you decide without deciding by rank?</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

The written artefact is a step table the team keeps with the workflow: for each step, its compensation or "forward only", its idempotency key, its retry policy and deadline, and who is paged when the deadline passes. The table makes the point of no return visible in review, which is where the next engineer would otherwise add a "send a confirmation SMS" step before provisioning.

## Follow-ups

- The charge succeeds, the process dies before recording it, and the workflow resumes. What runs, and what does the payment provider see?
- How would you test that no compensation ever runs after the login email?
- A payout to a contractor has the same shape as the email. What changes when the irreversible step moves money?

## Common mistakes

- Writing a compensation for the email and calling the workflow consistent.
- Running compensations in the order the steps ran rather than in reverse.
- Refunding after the welcome email has gone out, which contradicts what the customer was told.
- Treating a human approval as a guarantee that the send happens once.
- Treating every later failure the same, when provisioning only needs time and single sign-on needs the customer.
