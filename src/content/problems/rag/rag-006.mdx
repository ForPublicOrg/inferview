## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Field technicians ask a support assistant questions about a 200-page equipment service manual. How do you chunk it, and what do you measure to know the chunking is right?"
</Callout>

What you learn when you ask:

- The manual has numbered headings four levels deep, two-column pages, and about 120 tables (torque values, fault codes, part numbers), some of which continue across a page break with or without a repeated header row.
- The PDF has a text layer. Your parser's output on it is about 600 tokens per page.
- Each answer gets a context budget of 6,000 tokens; the system prompt, the question and the instructions take about 800 of them.
- Three more manuals of the same family follow next quarter.
- You have one week, and a support engineer who can write 40 questions with the page and table each answer comes from.

Give your chunking defaults with numbers, and the measurement that would make you change them.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask for five real technician questions and check where in the manual each answer sits: a paragraph, a table row, or a comparison across a whole table.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the parser's output for the three worst pages before choosing any size.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what one of the 40 questions is worth in recall points.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Parse first, then split on the heading tree, pack paragraphs inside a section to about 300 tokens with no overlap, and write tables as their own chunks: one per group of rows, each repeating the caption and the header row. Every chunk carries its heading path and page. Retrieval matches on those small chunks and returns parents (the enclosing subsection or whole table) up to the 5,200-token budget. To know it is right, the 40 questions are scored by whether the gold page or table row is inside the context actually sent, per question class, with tables over-represented. With 40 questions, one question is 2.5 points, so I only act on differences of several questions, read case by case.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Parsing decides the ceiling.</strong> A two-column page read in raw glyph order interleaves the columns, and a table becomes a stream of cells. Check the parser on the three worst pages (a two-column page, a table across a page break, a page of fault codes) before tuning anything; no splitter recovers a row whose cells were reordered.</p>
<p><strong>Units.</strong> Prose: split on headings, then pack paragraphs to a limit counted in tokens. LangChain's TextSplitter measures with <code>len</code> by default, so a 4,000 default is characters, and its recursive splitter has no idea a table exists. Tables: one chunk per 5 to 10 rows with the caption and header row repeated, plus the whole table as a parent. A continuation table without a repeated header is merged with its first half at parse time, by matching column count and position.</p>
<p><strong>The size, chosen.</strong> I pick 300 tokens for prose children. Chroma's comparison (472 queries, five retrieved chunks) puts recursive 400/0 at 89.5 recall and 200/0 at 88.1, with IoU 3.6 and 6.9: smaller chunks waste less of the context on text that does not answer. With standard deviations near 30 recall points across queries, those averages do not pick a winner for this manual; they rule out the large overlapped default (800/400: lowest IoU, 85.4 recall). Anything from 200 to 500 tokens is a reasonable start. A 4,000-character default is not a choice at all: it is the library's constructor default, it counts characters, and nobody measured it on this manual.</p>
<p><strong>The budget, computed.</strong> 6,000 − 800 = 5,200 tokens. Eight 300-token children with their heading paths fit in about 2,800, leaving room for two parents of about 1,000. Or three parents of about 1,700 and no children. Which one wins is the measurement below, not a preference.</p>
<p><strong>Context for the chunk.</strong> The heading path ("7 Hydraulics > 7.3 Pump > Torque values") prepended to every chunk is free. Contextual retrieval goes further: a model writes a sentence of context per chunk. At the vendor's own estimate of $1.02 per million document tokens, 200 pages × 600 = 120,000 tokens is about $0.12 per manual; Anthropic reports top-20 failures falling from 5.7% to 3.7% on its own data. Late chunking gets a similar effect at embedding time with a long-context embedding model and no extra calls. Either is worth trying only after the heading path is in and measured.</p>
<p><strong>The measurement.</strong> For each of the 40 questions, did the gold page or table row reach the model, inside the 5,200 tokens actually sent? Report it separately for prose, single-row table lookups and whole-table questions ("which pumps need more than 40 Nm"), because a chunker can win on the first two and fail the third. Compare two chunkers on the same questions and count the questions that change; with 40 questions, two or three flips are noise, and every flipped question gets read.</p>
<p><strong>Where this answer fails.</strong> Row-group chunks with repeated headers are good at "what is the torque for part 4471" and bad at questions that need the whole table: the retriever returns two row groups, the model answers from them, and the answer is confidently incomplete. Returning the whole table as the parent fixes it only when the table fits; a 300-row fault code table does not. Those questions need a different path (a structured lookup over the parsed table), and the eval only finds them if whole-table questions are in it.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>At 120,000 tokens the manual fits in one prompt. Why retrieve at all?</em> Anthropic's rule of thumb puts knowledge bases under about 200,000 tokens in the prompt. Retrieval wins when technicians ask many questions per day without a warm cache, when four manuals follow, and when the 5,200-token budget is a latency target rather than a price.</li>
<li><em>Would overlap help questions whose answer straddles two chunks?</em> Only if the eval shows questions failing at chunk boundaries; Chroma's 400/200 scored below 400/0 on recall.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *The 40 questions show no difference between 300-token and 600-token children, and the next three manuals are in a different layout. Which do you ship, and what do you re-measure?*
- *A table spans pages 88 to 90 and the parser drops the header row on page 89. How does the eval tell you, and how does the pipeline?*
- *The support engineer wants the chunker to split on every numbered step in procedures. What does that do to questions that span steps?*

## Common mistakes

- Choosing size and overlap before reading what the parser produced.
- Splitting tables row by row without the caption and header, so a row of numbers matches nothing.
- Counting chunk size in characters and calling it tokens.
- Scoring retrieval at top-k without the context budget, so a chunker that returns large chunks looks better than what the model is actually sent.
- Treating a one-question difference on a 40-question set as a result.
