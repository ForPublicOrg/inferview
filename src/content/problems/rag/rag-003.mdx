## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Here are three retrieval workloads. For each, pick flat search, IVF or HNSW, name the parameter you would tune first, and tell me what you give up."
</Callout>

The workloads are a scenario. Vectors are float32 unless stated.

1. **Help centres.** 3,000 customers, each with 2,000 to 20,000 chunks of 1,024 dimensions. Every query is filtered to one customer. Articles are edited throughout the day and edits must be searchable within a minute.
2. **Support tickets.** 30 million chunks of 1,024 dimensions in Postgres, 300,000 inserts a day, and deletion requests that must be honoured within 30 days. The target is recall@10 of 0.95 against exact search on the same queries.
3. **Image archive.** 800 million embeddings of 512 dimensions, appended in one batch a month, searched from nodes with 128 GB of RAM each. Two nodes are budgeted.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask whether deletes must leave the index or only the results, and whether the index may be rebuilt offline.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Help centres: no ANN index. Filter to the customer and compute exact distances; the largest customer is 20,000 rows, so the thing to tune first is the size at which a customer gets its own index. Tickets: HNSW in pgvector, because it takes inserts without retraining and pgvector removes deleted rows on vacuum; tune <code>hnsw.ef_search</code> first, since it needs no rebuild. Image archive: IVF with product quantization, because 800 million float32 vectors are 1.6 TB against 256 GB of RAM and Faiss's authors say IVF is the only option once compression is needed to fit; tune <code>nprobe</code> first. Each choice gives up something different: latency headroom, delete cost and build time, and recall.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Help centres.</strong> The filter is the index. The largest customer has 20,000 x 1,024 x 4 = 81.9 MB of vectors, a scan the database can do from memory, and an exact scan has perfect recall and no build step, so a one-minute freshness requirement costs nothing. One shared HNSW index is the wrong choice here: pgvector applies the filter after the scan, and a customer with 2,000 rows in a shared table of tens of millions would see almost no candidates. The parameter is a threshold you choose: the row count above which a customer moves to a partial index or its own partition. Set it from a measured p95 of the exact scan against the latency budget, not from a rule of thumb.</p>
<p><strong>Tickets.</strong> HNSW, because inserts land without retraining, the speed-recall trade-off is the better one in pgvector, and a delete becomes a dead tuple that vacuum removes from the graph. IVFFlat would need its lists trained on data, and the Faiss paper notes IVF efficiency degrades when additions or removals shift the distribution, which 300,000 inserts a day will do. Tune <code>hnsw.ef_search</code> first: it is a session setting, so a sweep from 40 upward against exact top-10 on a few hundred real queries finds the cheapest value that reaches 0.95 without touching the index. <code>m</code> and <code>ef_construction</code> need a rebuild, and Faiss reports that graph construction time is the limit beyond 10 million vectors, so they are the last resort. Run pgvector 0.8.3 or later for the HNSW vacuum corruption fix; 0.8.4 fixed two more vacuum errors, and the README shows 0.8.7 as of 2026-10-28.</p>
<p><strong>Image archive.</strong> 800,000,000 x 512 x 4 bytes = 1.64 TB at full precision against 256 GB across both nodes, so compression is not optional. Faiss's guidance is that graph indexes suit cases with no memory constraint and that IVF is the only option when compression is needed to fit; product quantization at 64 bytes per vector gives 800,000,000 x 64 = 51.2 GB of codes, which fits with room for ids and centroids. The number of lists is a quantity you choose: pgvector's starting rule of the square root of rows gives about 28,000, while the Faiss paper reports an optimum of 15 to 20 times the square root for its benchmarks, about 420,000 to 570,000. Either is defensible as a start; a list count chosen without a recall sweep is not. Tune <code>nprobe</code> first, because it trades recall for latency per query with no retraining. The monthly batch is the right moment to retrain centroids.</p>

### What each choice gives up

| Workload | Choice | First knob | What you give up |
|---|---|---|---|
| Help centres | Exact search per customer | Rows per customer before a dedicated index | Latency grows linearly with the customer; one large customer makes the p95 |
| Tickets | HNSW in pgvector | `hnsw.ef_search` | Build time and memory; slow vacuums after bulk deletes |
| Image archive | IVF with product quantization | `nprobe` | Recall, which no `nprobe` fully recovers without rescoring against full vectors |

**The constraint conflict** differs per workload. For tickets it is freshness and deletes against recall: every vacuum after a bulk delete costs I/O, and the pgvector README suggests reindexing first to speed it up, which means a window with a second index on disk. For the archive it is memory against recall: 64-byte codes fit with room to spare, while larger codes recover recall and eat the headroom that ids, centroids and the operating system need.

**The failure mode of the recommended answers.** The help-centre design fails quietly when a customer grows: nothing breaks, the p95 for that customer climbs until someone notices. Alert on rows per customer against the threshold. The archive's centroids are trained on the data that existed at training time, so a month of new images from a different camera or style lands in a few overfull lists, `nprobe` reads more vectors per query and recall at the same `nprobe` falls. Track list-size skew after each batch and retrain when it moves. For tickets, a 30 million row HNSW graph takes long enough to build that a rebuild is a planned operation, so the delete path has to work through vacuum, which is why the version floor matters.

**What is unreasonable.** HNSW for the archive at full precision needs more RAM than the budget before the graph links are counted; hnswlib's own estimate for those is about 8 to 10 bytes per element per unit of M. A single IVF index trained once for the tickets ignores the insert rate. And any recall figure quoted without saying it was measured against exact search on the same queries says nothing.

## Follow-ups

- *Why not one HNSW index per help-centre customer?* 3,000 indexes, each needing a build, a vacuum and memory, for customers an exact scan already serves; it is worth it only above the threshold you set.
- *Your archive recall at the chosen `nprobe` is 0.80 and the product wants 0.90. Do you raise `nprobe`, rescore a shortlist against full vectors on disk, or move to larger codes? Price each in latency or memory before choosing.*
- *Two engineers disagree: one wants Faiss's list count, the other pgvector's square root. How do you settle it without deciding by rank?* Build both on one month's batch and compare recall and p95 at equal `nprobe` cost.

## Common mistakes

- Putting every customer of the help centre in one ANN index and filtering afterwards.
- Treating `m` as the first recall knob when `ef_search` needs no rebuild.
- Sizing the archive from the codes alone and forgetting ids and centroids.
- Quoting pgvector's defaults as properties of HNSW in general.
- Training IVF centroids once and never checking list sizes as the data shifts.
