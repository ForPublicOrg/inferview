## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"When does GraphRAG earn its indexing cost over the hybrid retrieval we already run? Then: a VP forwards Microsoft's LazyGraphRAG post with the line 'indexing at 0.1% of GraphRAG's cost and 700 times cheaper queries, let's adopt it'. Reply to the VP."
</Callout>

What you can learn by asking (scenario):

- The corpus is 4,000 postmortems and design documents, about 2 million tokens. About 3 percent of documents change each week.
- Today's assistant runs hybrid retrieval with a reranker. Most questions are lookups: "what was the root cause of the March payments outage?"
- Leadership has started asking questions such as "what are the recurring causes of our sev-1 incidents over three years?", and the assistant answers them from whichever eight chunks rank highest.
- Extraction would run on Claude Sonnet 5.5 at $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens (checked 2026-10-28).

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask for last month's question log and how many questions could be answered from a single document.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>For each ratio in the post, ask what it is measured against and whether we run that thing today.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>GraphRAG earns its cost when a measurable share of questions are about the corpus as a whole and the corpus is stable enough that its community summaries stay true between re-indexes. Here the indexing bill is about $100 a run, so dollars are not what decides; the global question share, build time, entity-resolution quality and a second system to operate are. To the VP: the 0.1 percent is against full GraphRAG, and the same post says LazyGraphRAG indexes at the cost of vector RAG, which is what we pay now; the 700 times is against GraphRAG Global Search, which we do not run. Neither ratio says what it costs or gains against our system, so I would test the few global questions we have against a cheap baseline first.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Why graphs help some questions.</strong> Top-k retrieval returns the chunks most similar to the question. "What are the recurring causes" is not similar to any chunk; its answer is an aggregation over hundreds. GraphRAG pays at index time to make that aggregation possible: an LLM extracts entities and relationships per chunk, the graph is partitioned into communities, and each community gets a written summary. Global Search maps over the summaries and reduces. Edge et al. report comprehensiveness win rates over vector RAG of 72 to 83 percent on their podcast corpus, judged by an LLM on LLM-generated global questions; that judge setup favours longer, broader answers, which is the property being measured.</p>
<p><strong>The indexing bill, computed.</strong> With the paper's 600-token chunks and 100-token overlap, the stride is 500 tokens, so 2,000,000 / 500 = 4,000 chunks. Assume an extraction prompt of 2,000 tokens with its examples (measure yours) and 500 tokens of output: (2,600 × $2 + 500 × $10) / 1M = $0.0102 per call. One gleaning round, which re-asks for missed entities with the first answer in context: about (3,150 × $2 + 200 × $10) / 1M = $0.0083. Per chunk $0.0185, for 4,000 chunks $74. Microsoft's docs put graph extraction at roughly 75 percent of indexing cost, so a full index is about $74 / 0.75 ≈ $100. A weekly re-index is $100 a week. At 2 billion tokens the same arithmetic is about $100,000 a run, and then the dollars decide. At our size, what costs is build time (the paper's 1-million-token podcast index took 281 minutes on its setup), operating a second index, and the quality of entity extraction.</p>
<p><strong>The vendor's ratios.</strong> The post states that LazyGraphRAG's indexing cost is identical to vector RAG and 0.1 percent of full GraphRAG. Against our hybrid index the saving is zero; the ratio is against an index we never built. The 700 times is query cost against GraphRAG Global Search at comparable quality on global questions; LazyGraphRAG moves the LLM work to query time, so against our current per-query cost it is more expensive, by an amount set by its relevance-test budget (the post tests 100, 500 and 1,500). The evaluation is Microsoft's own, on 5,590 AP news articles and 100 synthetic queries, half of them global. It is evidence that deferring LLM work is a good trade against eager GraphRAG. It is not evidence about postmortems or about our baseline.</p>
<p><strong>The decision, two defensible answers.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><em>Build a graph index for global questions only.</em> Standard GraphRAG or the cheaper FastGraphRAG over the postmortems, a router that sends corpus-wide questions to Global Search, hybrid retrieval for the rest. Cost: about $100 a run, hours of build, a router that will misroute, and a graph whose entities (services, teams, vendors) must be merged correctly for "recurring" to mean anything.</li>
<li><em>No graph yet.</em> Write a 200-token structured summary per postmortem at ingestion (cause, service, trigger, fix), and answer global questions by map-reduce over the 4,000 summaries, 800,000 tokens, in batches. Cost: about 4,000 × 200 × $2 / 1M = $1.60 of input per global question uncached, minutes of latency, and summaries that only contain what the summary prompt asked for.</li>
</ul>
<p>The quantity to choose is the global-question rate at which the graph pays. On dollars alone, $100 a week of re-indexing against $1.60 a question breaks even at about 60 global questions a week. The question log says how many there are; if it is a handful, the summaries win on cost and on not running a second system. Dollars are not the whole decision: if the eval shows map-reduce over summaries missing patterns the graph finds, a few hundred dollars a month is cheap.</p>
<p><strong>The conflict.</strong> Comprehensiveness on a small, visible class of questions against cost, freshness and operating load, with a sponsor who has already chosen.</p>
<p><strong>Where the recommendation fails.</strong> Per-document summaries lose what no single document states. If the same flaky dependency is called "the auth proxy" in one postmortem and "edge-gw" in another, map-reduce over summaries counts two causes; an entity graph with good resolution counts one. That is exactly the "recurring causes" question. If the eval shows it, the summaries option is wrong for this corpus and the graph is earned.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>How do you evaluate a global answer with no single right answer?</em> Build a small labelled set from the incident database's structured fields (cause category per sev-1), so "recurring causes" has a ground truth, and score both systems against it rather than with pairwise judge preference.</li>
<li><em>Does the graph need a graph database?</em> No. The index is tables of entities, relationships and community reports; storage is an implementation choice.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the VP has already announced GraphRAG at the all-hands and your summaries option takes two weeks. Do you build the graph anyway, and what do you put in writing about the risk you accept?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in the question log or the eval to build the graph after all?* A global-question rate past the break-even, or an eval where summary map-reduce splits one recurring cause into several because the documents name it differently.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants FastGraphRAG for its cost, the other standard extraction for a cleaner graph. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Index a 10 percent sample both ways, measure entity-merge errors on services you can list from the service catalogue, and price both at full scale.
- *The corpus will grow to every design document in the company, about 2 billion tokens, next year. What changes in your answer?* The indexing bill becomes about $100,000 a run, so full re-indexing on change is out; incremental extraction on changed documents and community re-summarization only where membership changed become requirements, or deferral in the LazyGraphRAG style.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting LazyGraphRAG's ratios as savings against vector RAG; the post's own baseline for both is GraphRAG.
- Arguing about GraphRAG's indexing bill without computing it for the corpus in front of you.
- Treating GraphRAG as an upgrade for lookup questions, where hybrid retrieval with a reranker is cheaper and at least as good.
- Citing the paper's win rates without saying they are LLM-judged comprehensiveness and diversity on generated global questions.
- Ignoring entity resolution, the step that decides whether "recurring" counts correctly.
