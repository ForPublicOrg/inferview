## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Users say our assistant feels dumb. The team wrote this plan and the VP wants it live by the end of the month. Review it: what is wrong, what would you change, and what ships?"
</Callout>

The plan below is constructed for this problem. Read it once as an approver would, list the flaws and rank them, decide how many flagged conversations you would read before choosing a fix, then open the checklist.

### Assistant quality plan, sprint 41

**Problem.** Since August, internal users report that the HR and IT assistant "feels dumb". The support channel has 15 examples, which we use as our test set.

**Fixes, all shipping on the 30th.**

1. Upgrade the model to the vendor's larger tier, which costs 5 times as much per token. It is better at reasoning, so it will use the context better.
2. Extend the system prompt from 800 to 2,000 tokens: answer only from the provided context, be thorough, quote the policy, never guess.
3. Raise retrieval `top_k` from 5 to 20 chunks of about 300 tokens, so the model has more to work with.
4. Add an automated quality score: the upgraded model grades each answer from 1 to 10 for helpfulness and accuracy.

**Success criteria.** The average quality score on the 15 examples rises from today's 5.8 to at least 8, and the thumbs-up rate rises in September.

**Cost.** Higher per query, justified by quality. Finance has been told to expect an increase.

**Rollback.** Revert the deployment if the thumbs-up rate falls.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how many of the flagged answers were refusals, and how many cite a document that has since changed.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what one query costs today in input tokens, and what it will cost after items 1 to 3.</p></details>

## Answer

Rank by how much each flaw lets a wrong change through, then by how hard it is to undo.

**1. Nothing in the plan locates the failure.** Every fix acts on generation (items 1 and 2) or adds input to it (item 3), and nobody has checked whether the evidence reaches the prompt. "Feels dumb" can be Missing Content (the policy is not indexed or is stale), a retrieval miss, a chunk dropped before the prompt, or a model that had the answer and did not use it; Barnett et al. list seven such points. Label each flagged conversation by its first failing stage, then run oracle context on the failures: questions that become right with the gold chunks in the prompt are retrieval or assembly problems, and a bigger model will not reach them. This is the flaw an approver misses, because each item is a plausible improvement on its own.

**2. The eval set is the 15 complaints.** Tuning against the cases you are judged on makes the score rise whether or not the system improves, and a set made only of failures cannot show a regression on questions that work today. It also has no unanswerable questions, so item 2's refusals cannot be scored as right or wrong.

**3. The judge is the shipped model with an uncalibrated scale.** A 1-to-10 score with no anchors, graded by the model being shipped, with no human-labelled sample to measure its agreement. How much the judging procedure matters is measurable: on WikiEval, the Ragas paper reports agreement with human faithfulness labels of 0.95 for its procedure and 0.54 for a ranking prompt on the same data. An average target of 8 hides whether one answer improved a lot or all improved a little.

**4. "Answer only from the context" with no retrieval fix.** When retrieval misses, the instruction turns a wrong answer into a refusal. Users report both as dumb. Without refusal rate by stage the plan cannot tell whether item 2 helped.

**5. top_k from 5 to 20.** This is a judgement call, not a flat error. More context can help: Barnett et al. list, among their lessons, more accurate answers with 8K of context than 4K. It can also hurt: Chroma's 2025 report found that even a single distractor reduces performance and that performance degrades with input length. Raising k is acceptable if gold-in-prompt and correctness are measured at 5, 10 and 20 and a reranker cuts the context to a budget; as written it adds 15 unranked chunks.

**6. The cost is never computed.** Today: 800 tokens of system prompt, 5 chunks of 300 and about 50 for the question, 2,350 input tokens. After: 2,000 + 6,000 + 50 = 8,050, which is 3.4 times the tokens at 5 times the price, about 17 times per query. "Finance has been told" is not a sign-off on a 17-fold increase for an unmeasured gain.

**7. Everything ships on one date, with a rollback on thumbs.** If quality moves, no change can be credited or blamed, and the only rollback reverts all four. Thumbs-up rate is sparse and arrives weeks later, so it cannot trigger a rollback in time.

**8. Freshness is not on the list.** An HR assistant that "got dumb since August" may be answering from a policy that changed in August. Hint 2 checks it in an hour.

**How many conversations to read first.** This is your number to choose. Fifty flagged conversations is a reasonable floor: a stage behind 30% of failures shows up about 15 times, enough to rank it against the others. Reading 15 is too few to rank four stages; reading 500 before acting is unreasonable when the first 50 usually show which stage dominates.

### What ships by the end of the month

Two defensible answers.

- *Measure for one week, then ship the change the triage points at.* Build a set of about 100 labelled questions from real traffic (the 15 complaints, currently-correct questions, unanswerable ones), triage, run oracle context, and ship the fix for the stage that loses the most questions. Cost: a week with no visible change while the VP watches the deadline, and the fix may be retrieval work that takes longer than the month.
- *Ship the model upgrade now behind a flag on 10% of traffic, and measure in parallel.* Cost: about 17 times the per-query spend on that slice if items 2 and 3 come with it, a result that cannot be read until the labelled set exists, and the risk that the flag becomes the default before anyone reads it.

I would measure first and ship by the 30th whatever the triage supports, because the plan's own items cannot be evaluated without the labelled set and every day of the 17-fold cost is spent on a guess. The failure mode of measuring first: the 100 questions are written by the team, so they reflect what the team thinks users ask; a set built from what is easy to label understates the hard questions that produced the complaints. Draw at least half from logged queries, and refresh the set monthly from new flags.

## Follow-ups

- *The triage takes two engineers a week and the VP says the deadline does not move. Which half of the measurement do you cut, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *What would you need to see to approve the larger model?* An oracle run in which most failures happen with the evidence in the prompt, and a flagged slice where correctness on the labelled set rises by more than the set's noise at the stated cost.
- *The team lead says the bigger model is obviously better and measuring is a delay. Two senior engineers disagree; how do you decide without deciding by rank?* Run both models on the labelled set with oracle context, so the comparison isolates the one thing the upgrade changes.
- *The triage says 40% of failures are stale documents. What changes in the plan, and who owns the fix?*

## Common mistakes

- Approving each item because each is reasonable alone.
- Accepting a judge score as the success metric without a human-labelled sample.
- Calling the 15 complaints a test set.
- Treating top_k as a free improvement, or as always harmful.
- Leaving the cost as "higher" without computing it.
