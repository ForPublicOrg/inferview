## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The round">
"Our assistant answers from the company drive, the wiki and the ticket system. Permissions change all day, and our full re-crawl and re-embed takes about a day. Design retrieval so that nobody gets an answer built from a document they cannot open, and tell me how long after a revocation that is true."
</Callout>

The constraints, as a scenario:

- 900,000 documents, 6 million chunks, one pgvector table. The connectors re-crawl and re-embed everything in about 22 hours, and the team of three that owns them cannot change them this quarter.
- Permission changes: up to 20,000 per hour at peak, most of them group membership changes; a share change on a folder applies to everything under it. The drive and the wiki emit permission-change events; the ticket system does not.
- Security wants a revocation to take effect in retrieval within 15 minutes and will sign off on nothing weaker without a written exception.
- The source systems offer a permission check: 50 document ids per call, about 80 ms, 600 calls per minute per tenant.
- Peak load is 30 questions per second. The retrieval budget is 400 ms at p95.
- Some users can see most of the corpus; contractors can see a few hundred documents.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask which systems can tell you a permission changed, and how quickly the event arrives.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the distribution of how many chunks each user can see, especially the smallest tenth.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Permissions never go through the 22-hour content path. Chunks carry a document id; a separate ACL table maps document to allowed groups and users, kept fresh from permission-change events where the source has them and from an hourly diff where it does not. Retrieval resolves the user to their groups and filters inside the search; small visible sets get an exact search instead of a filtered approximate one. On top of that, the documents behind the final context are checked live against the source, but only where the index cannot meet 15 minutes on its own (the ticket system and anything labelled confidential), because a live check on every question needs 1,800 calls a minute against a limit of 600. On error the check fails closed. The written artefact is a risk note stating the revocation window per source.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Separate the two freshness problems.</strong> Content can be a day stale and the assistant is merely out of date. Permissions a day stale is a disclosure. So the chunk rows keep the document id only, and permissions live in <code>doc_acl(doc_id, principal)</code>, joined at query time. A revocation is a few row deletes, not a re-embed, and the connectors are untouched.</p>
<p><strong>Group expansion at query time.</strong> The ACL table stores groups as the source does. The user's group list comes from the identity provider and is cached; that cache TTL is part of the revocation window. A folder share change becomes one event and a fan-out over the folder's documents in the ACL table, which is where the 20,000-per-hour peak lands.</p>
<p><strong>Two defensible designs for enforcement.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Labels in the index, kept fresh by events, live check only where needed.</strong> Retrieval filters on the user's principals; the live check runs on the final documents only for the ticket system (no events) and for confidential labels. Cost: you trust event delivery. A dropped webhook or an expired subscription is a leak until the hourly reconciliation diff finds it, and the 15-minute promise holds only for sources whose events arrive.</li>
<li><strong>A live check on every answer, with a coarse prefilter in the index.</strong> The index filters by tenant and department; the final 10 to 20 documents per answer are checked against the source every time. Always fresh, no ACL replication to get wrong. Cost: 30 questions per second is 1,800 batched calls a minute against 600 allowed, so it needs a decision cache that absorbs two thirds of calls, and a cache with a TTL is itself a revocation window. It adds about 80 ms per answer and makes the assistant's availability the permission API's.</li>
</ul>
<p>I would take the first, with the live check scoped so it fits the rate limit, and write the gap down. The conflict is revocation freshness against latency, availability and the API budget, and the second design spends all three to buy freshness the events already give for two of the three sources.</p>
<p><strong>The filtered search, sized.</strong> pgvector filters after the scan: at 10 percent selectivity and <code>ef_search</code> 40 about 4 rows survive. Iterative scans keep going up to <code>max_scan_tuples</code>, 20,000 by default. A user who sees 0.5 percent of 6 million chunks expects 100 matches in 20,000 scanned, enough for k = 10. A contractor who sees 300 documents, about 2,000 chunks, is 0.03 percent: about 7 expected matches in 20,000, an under-filled answer that looks like "nothing relevant". For users whose visible set is under a threshold, skip the approximate index: fetch their chunk ids through the ACL join and compute exact distances. I would set the threshold at 50,000 chunks; an exact scan of that many vectors is a cost to measure on this hardware, and the threshold moves with it.</p>
<p><strong>The revocation window, per source.</strong> Drive and wiki: event delivery lag plus fan-out time plus the group cache TTL. With a 5-minute group cache, events must land and fan out within 10 minutes. Tickets: no events, so the live check carries the promise; with a 5-minute decision cache, that is the window. Confidential labels: live check on every answer, no decision cache.</p>
<p><strong>Other leak paths.</strong> A response or semantic cache keyed on the question serves one user's answer to another: key it by the user's principal set or do not cache. Traces and logs hold retrieved text: protect them like the source. The answer when a restricted document matched is the same as when nothing matched. OWASP lists sensitive information disclosure (LLM02:2025) and vector and embedding weaknesses (LLM08:2025) as named risks; Microsoft documents the same rule for Copilot, that it only surfaces data the user has at least view permission to.</p>
<p><strong>Where this design fails.</strong> The fan-out. A share removed from a top-level folder with 200,000 documents under it is one event and 200,000 ACL updates; at the rate the rest of the peak leaves, the 15 minutes is gone, and every document under that folder is visible to the removed group until the fan-out finishes. Model folder inheritance in the ACL table (document to folder chain, folder to principals) so one revocation is one row, and accept a more expensive join at query time.</p>
<p><strong>What you give up.</strong> New grants are slower than revocations feel: a user who was just given access may not find the document until the next event or diff, and support will hear about it. Contractors pay for exact search with latency. When the permission API is down, ticket answers stop.</p>
<p><strong>Written down.</strong> A risk note for security: the window per source (drive and wiki: event lag plus 5 minutes; tickets: 5 minutes; confidential: none), the fail-closed policy, the hourly reconciliation and the alert on its diff count, and the folder fan-out case as an accepted exception until inheritance is modelled. Rollout: shadow the ACL filter for two weeks, logging every document the old path would have returned that the new one blocks and the reverse; roll back if any result appears that the source denies on a live check.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why not an index per user?</em> A document shared with thousands is stored thousands of times, and every share change rewrites many indexes.</li>
<li><em>How do you index "anyone with the link" documents?</em> Treat them as visible to users who have opened the link, or exclude them; indexing them as public turns an obscure link into a search result.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the exact-search path and the live checks add more latency than the 400 ms budget allows for contractors. Which do you cut, and what risk does security accept in writing?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see from the drive's event stream over a month to drop the hourly reconciliation diff?*
- *Security and the product lead disagree: security wants the live check on every answer, product will not accept the latency. How do you settle it without deciding by rank?*
- *A user loses access mid-conversation. What happens to chunks from that document already in the chat history?*

## Common mistakes

- Putting permissions on the content path, so a revocation waits for a re-crawl.
- Post-filtering the top-k and shipping, then finding contractors get empty answers.
- Promising a revocation window without adding the group cache TTL and the fan-out time.
- A live check on every answer without doing the arithmetic against the permission API's rate limit.
- Answering "there is a document you cannot see", which discloses that it exists.
