## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
You are building search for a community Q&A site. For each question in your offline eval set, moderators have labelled exactly one accepted answer. Product research says users read the first result and rarely scroll. Which single offline metric should the team optimise?
</Callout>

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>How many relevant items does each query have, and does the metric care about position?</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Which metric drops by half when the answer moves from rank 1 to rank 2?</p></details>

## Answer

MRR. For each question, take 1 divided by the rank of the first relevant result (0 if none is returned), then average over questions. Three questions whose accepted answers sit at ranks 1, 2 and 4 give (1 + 0.5 + 0.25) / 3 = 0.583. Moving any of them to rank 1 raises the score; moving one from rank 1 to rank 2 halves that question's contribution, which matches a reader who looks only at the top.

Why the others are tempting. recall@100 is the right metric for a first-stage retriever that feeds a reranker, because there you only care that the answer survives into the candidate set; it is the wrong metric for what the user sees. precision@k shines when many results are relevant, such as a product search page, and is capped at 1/k with a single relevant answer. Hit rate@k is a good coarse alarm ("did we find it at all?") and is often mislabelled as recall@k, but it is blind to position inside the window.

nDCG would also work and becomes the better choice once labels are graded (perfect, partial, off-topic). With one binary label per question it adds little over MRR and is harder to explain to a product team.

## Follow-ups

- *Your system has a retriever and a reranker. Which metric for which stage?* recall@k of the retriever at the reranker's input size, and MRR or nDCG of the reranked list.
- *What if some questions have several acceptable answers?* MRR still only counts the first; nDCG with graded labels, or MAP, rewards placing all of them high.
- *How many labelled questions do you need?* Enough that the difference you care about is larger than the variation between random samples of your set; bootstrap the eval set to see that variation.

## Common mistakes

- Reporting hit rate and calling it recall.
- Averaging ranks instead of reciprocal ranks, which lets one bad query dominate.
- Choosing nDCG by default without graded labels to feed it.
