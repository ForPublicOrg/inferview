## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt, as candidates report it">
"Design a retrieval-augmented generation system that answers employee questions from our internal wiki and documents. Cover ingestion, indexing, retrieval and generation, and tell me how you would know it works."
</Callout>

The company and its numbers are a scenario. Two million pages across a wiki, a document store and a ticket system; 20,000 employees; about 30 questions a second at peak. Permissions are set per space and per page, and group membership lives in the identity provider. The wiki cannot be changed, and its permission-check API allows 50 calls a second for the whole tenant. The team is four engineers with twelve weeks to a first release.

Two people disagree. The legal team requires that when someone loses access to an HR or legal page, the assistant stops using it within five minutes. The search lead wants permissions enforced only as index filters, because live checks put p95 latency over the two-second target and use up the API limit. Design the system, decide how permissions and freshness are enforced, and write the decision note you would send both of them.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask how revocations happen in practice: a page restriction, a space permission, or a person leaving a group in the identity provider.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request the measured lag between a permission change in the wiki and its change event reaching you, at p99.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what share of the questions in the pilot logs are answered from HR and legal spaces.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Two paths. Ingestion turns change events into chunks with heading paths, document version and access labels (the space, the page restriction and the groups allowed), and indexes them for BM25 and vectors; deletes and permission changes are first-class events on their own queue. The query path resolves the asker's groups from the identity provider, filters retrieval by them, fuses, reranks within a budget and generates with citations checked against what was sent. Permissions and freshness conflict because the index is only as current as its slowest event, so permission events get a separate, faster path that rewrites access labels without re-embedding, and HR and legal content either waits for phase two or gets a live check on the final results only. I would launch without HR and legal, measure the event lag, and add them with live checks once the lag is known.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>Ingestion.</b> Connectors subscribe to change events from each source and fall back to polling with content hashes. Parsing keeps headings, lists and tables; chunks follow structure and carry the document id, version, heading path and access labels. Each chunk is indexed for BM25 and as a vector, optionally with a short model-written context; Anthropic reports that contextual embeddings with contextual BM25 and reranking cut its top-20 failure rate from 5.7% to 1.9%, at a one-time $1.02 per million document tokens. A nightly reconciliation diffs source ids and access labels against the index and repairs both, because change feeds lose events.</p>
<p><b>Where permissions and freshness collide.</b> A content edit needs parsing, chunking and embedding; a permission change needs only new labels on existing chunks. On one queue, a bulk import of 50,000 pages delays every revocation behind it. So permission events go on their own queue, processed first, as metadata updates to every chunk of the page. Group membership is not copied into the index at all: chunks carry group ids, and the asker's groups are resolved at query time with a short cache, so a person removed from a group loses access when the cache expires.</p>
<p><b>The number to choose: the group cache TTL.</b> It has to fit inside the legal team's five minutes together with the page-event lag. If the measured p99 event lag is two minutes (Hint 2; a scenario figure until measured), the TTL can be at most three minutes; one minute leaves margin and costs one identity-provider call per active user per minute. A TTL of 15 minutes breaks the requirement for group removals; zero calls the identity provider on every query.</p>
<p><b>The live check: why not everywhere.</b> Checking 50 candidates per query at 30 queries a second is 1,500 calls a second against a limit of 50. Checking only the final 8 chunks, from perhaps 5 distinct pages, is still 150 a second. Checking only results from HR and legal spaces reaches the limit when a third of answers draw on them (150 / 3 = 50 a second), so it fits with headroom only well below that share, which Hint 3 measures. A live check fails closed: when the API is down, those results are dropped and the answer says it could not use them.</p>
<p><b>Query path.</b> A router sends lookups that a structured API answers ("who owns service X") away from retrieval. The question is rewritten as a standalone query, retrieved with the asker's groups as a filter (filtered traversal or partitioning by space, since filters applied after an approximate scan return too few rows when they are selective), fused with RRF, reranked within a latency budget, assembled best first within a token budget, and answered with citations that a post-check rejects if they point at anything not sent. Answer caches are keyed by the asker's permission set or not shared across users at all.</p>
<p><b>Oversharing comes with launch.</b> Retrieval makes every page a user can open findable by asking. Microsoft's Copilot documentation says Copilot only surfaces data a user has at least view permission to, and tells customers to use their permission models to make sure the right people have access. That puts the burden on the permissions you already have: before launch, run a report of pages in sensitive spaces shared with everyone and send it to the space owners.</p>
<p><b>Evaluation.</b> A labelled set of a few hundred questions from pilot logs, with gold chunks and reference answers, including restricted-content questions asked by users who may and may not see them. Per stage: recall@k after fusion, gold in prompt, faithfulness, correctness, refusal accuracy, and permission-leak tests that must be zero. A revocation test runs every hour: remove a test user from a group and from a page, ask the questions, and alert if an answer uses the content after five minutes.</p>
</Fragment>

<li><em>What would you need to see in production to drop the live check for HR and legal?</em> Ninety days of the hourly revocation test passing with p99 event lag under three minutes, and no permission event lost in the nightly reconciliation.</li>

### The decision with two defensible answers

- **Launch without HR and legal content.** Index everything else with index-time access filters and the fast permission queue; add HR and legal in phase two once the event lag is measured and the live check is built. Cost: HR and legal questions get "I can't search that content yet" for the first release, and if Hint 3 says they are a large share of questions, the launch disappoints the users who asked for it.
- **Launch with HR and legal behind a live check on final results.** Cost: four engineers build the check, the fail-closed path and the rate-limit handling inside twelve weeks, and the first incident of an API outage turns HR answers into refusals.

I would launch without HR and legal. The legal requirement is a five-minute guarantee and nobody has measured the event lag it depends on; shipping the content before the measurement exists is shipping a guarantee we cannot state.

The failure mode of that choice: "HR and legal spaces" is a list maintained by hand, and sensitive pages live outside those spaces, such as a compensation spreadsheet in an engineering space. Excluding two spaces is not a classification. Where the source has sensitivity labels, exclude by label, and run the oversharing report on every space, not only the two.

### The decision note

> **Decision.** Release 1 answers from the wiki, document store and tickets with access enforced as index filters on page and space labels, and group membership resolved from the identity provider at query time with a one-minute cache. HR and legal spaces, and anything labelled confidential, are not indexed in release 1.
> **The legal requirement.** Revocation within five minutes is met for group removals by the cache TTL. For page restrictions it depends on event lag, which we will measure for four weeks before HR and legal content is indexed. Release 2 adds that content with a live permission check on final results, failing closed.
> **What we give up.** HR and legal questions are unanswered in release 1. Users who ask them get a message naming the spaces we do not search.
> **Rollout.** Pilot with 500 users in two departments for two weeks, then the company. The hourly revocation test and the permission-leak tests run from the first day of the pilot.
> **Rollback triggers.** Any permission-leak test failing; any revocation test using content after five minutes; p99 permission-event lag above three minutes for an hour. Rollback means the assistant stops answering from the affected source until the cause is fixed, switched by configuration.
> **Owners.** Legal signs the five-minute requirement and the release 2 entry criteria; the search lead owns latency and the event-lag dashboard; space owners own the oversharing report.

## Follow-ups

- *A VP says the bot "makes things up" in week two. How do you respond?* Pull the examples, triage each to a stage with the labelled set's metrics, and show the per-stage numbers before changing anything.
- *The identity provider has an outage. What does the assistant do?* Serve askers whose groups are cached until the TTL expires, then answer only from content open to everyone, and say so.
- *You have sixty days before the embedding model you index with is retired. How do you re-embed two million pages without breaking the five-minute revocation path?* A parallel index fed by the same permission queue, compared on the labelled set, then a switch of reads.

## Common mistakes

- Enforcing access in the prompt or after generation.
- Copying group membership into every chunk, so a person leaving a group means rewriting millions of rows.
- Putting permission changes on the same queue as content edits.
- Proposing live permission checks without computing the call rate against the API limit.
- Sharing an answer cache across users with different permissions.
- Treating "exclude the HR space" as data classification.
