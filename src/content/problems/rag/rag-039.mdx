## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Explain the nlist versus nprobe trade-off in IVF. Our index got slow as it grew; someone wants to multiply nlist by sixteen. And when would you add product quantization?"
</Callout>

The system is a scenario: a Faiss IVF-Flat index over 384-dimension float32 embeddings on one node with 128 GB of RAM. It was trained at 10 million vectors with nlist 4,096 and serves at nprobe 32 with recall@10 of 0.93 against exact search. It now holds 60 million vectors, nothing was retrained, and p95 latency has gone up about six times. The proposal on the table: retrain with nlist 65,536 and keep nprobe 32.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask for the distribution of list sizes today, not the mean.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how the centroids are searched: every centroid on every query, or through an index of their own.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask for the RAM the vectors take now and the growth expected this year.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>nlist sets how finely the space is cut; nprobe sets how many cells a query reads. A query pays nlist distances to pick cells and about nprobe x N / nlist to scan them. At 10 million vectors that was 4,096 + 32 x 10,000,000 / 4,096 = 82,000 distances; at 60 million, without retraining, it is 4,096 + 468,750 = 473,000, about 5.8 times as many, which matches the slowdown. Multiplying nlist by sixteen with nprobe left at 32 cuts the scan to about 29,000 distances, but each probe now covers a sixteenth of the space it did, so recall falls; nprobe has to grow with nlist, and the right pair comes from a sweep, not from the formula. I would retrain at nlist 32,768 to 65,536 with an HNSW coarse quantizer and sweep nprobe from 32 to 256 for the smallest value that restores 0.93. Product quantization comes in when memory, not latency, is the constraint: 60 million float32 vectors are 92 GB of a 128 GB node, so the next doubling forces it, and with it a re-rank step on full vectors.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The cost model, computed.</strong> The Faiss paper writes the distances per query as K + P x N / K for K lists and P probes, minimised at K = sqrt(P x N). With P = 32 at 60 million, that is sqrt(1.92 billion), about 43,800 lists. The formula assumes equal list sizes and an exhaustive pass over the centroids, and the paper names three ways reality departs from it: nprobe must grow with K to keep accuracy, lists are unbalanced, and the coarse quantizer is often not exhaustive. Its experiments put the best K at about 15 to 20 times sqrt(N), which at 60 million is 116,000 to 155,000; the Faiss wiki's rule of 4 to 16 times sqrt(N) gives 31,000 to 124,000. These are starting points for a sweep.</p>
<p><strong>Why the tail can get worse than the mean.</strong> Six times the vectors in the same 4,096 lists explains 5.8 times the distances on average. Untrained growth adds to the tail: new data lands in whichever centroid is nearest, and if this year's documents differ from the training set, a few lists swell. The paper calls the relative variance of list sizes the imbalance factor and shows it multiplies the expected cost. That is why the hint asks for the distribution: a p95 driven by a few huge lists is fixed by retraining, not by any nprobe.</p>
<p><strong>Two defensible answers.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Retrain at a moderate nlist, 16,384 to 32,768, exhaustive coarse quantizer.</strong> Simple, one rebuild, centroid search costs 16,000 to 33,000 distances, which the scan then dwarfs. Cost: at 60 million, sqrt(32 x N) says the optimum is already above this, and growth pushes it further, so another retrain comes sooner.</li>
<li><strong>Retrain at 65,536 with an HNSW index over the centroids.</strong> The paper notes that a fast coarse quantizer makes more lists worthwhile, because picking cells stops costing K distances. Cost: training needs 30 to 256 vectors per centroid by the wiki's rule, 2 to 17 million samples; a second index to build; and nprobe has to rise, so the latency gain is smaller than the scan arithmetic suggests.</li>
</ul>
<p>The quantity you choose is the pair (nlist, nprobe), and you defend it with a sweep: for each candidate nlist, the smallest nprobe that restores recall@10 of 0.93 on a few hundred logged queries, and its p95. The proposal as stated, nlist 65,536 with nprobe 32, is unreasonable: it will look fast and quietly lose recall.</p>
<p><strong>When product quantization earns its place.</strong> 60,000,000 x 384 x 4 = 92 GB of vectors on a 128 GB node. At the next doubling they do not fit. PQ stores M bytes per vector; at M = 48 that is 60,000,000 x 48 = 2.9 GB of codes, plus 8-byte ids, 0.5 GB. The price is accuracy: distances are approximate, so recall at any nprobe drops, and the standard repair is a shortlist re-ranked with full vectors, which the wiki lists as IVF-PQ with a refine step and a k_factor to tune. Full vectors can then live on SSD and be read only for the shortlist. Before PQ, try int8 scalar quantization: four times smaller, 23 GB here, and Hugging Face's benchmarks kept about 99.3% of retrieval performance. PQ is for when int8 is still too big, or when scan bandwidth, not RAM, is the limit.</p>
<p><strong>The constraint conflict</strong> is latency against recall against memory. More lists cut the scan and cost recall per probe; PQ cuts memory and bandwidth and costs recall that only a re-rank buys back, at an SSD read per query.</p>
<p><strong>The failure mode of this answer.</strong> The retrain fixes today's distribution. Centroids are fixed at training time, so the next year of documents skews the lists again and p95 drifts up without anyone changing anything. Track list-size skew and recall at the production nprobe after each bulk load, and schedule retraining on a threshold of either, not on a calendar.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Recall at nlist 65,536 needs nprobe 256 and p95 is no better than nlist 16,384 at nprobe 64. Which do you ship?</em> The smaller nlist: same latency, cheaper to train and to retrain.</li>
<li><em>Why does residual encoding matter for IVF-PQ?</em> Codes for the offset from the centroid are more accurate than codes for the raw vector, most of all for short codes, and Faiss does it by default.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *The sweep says no nprobe restores 0.93 at nlist 65,536 within the latency budget. Do you accept 0.90, buy another node, or move to HNSW, and who signs the lower recall?*
- *What would you need to see in the list-size distribution to retrain monthly instead of after each bulk load?*
- *Two engineers disagree: one wants int8 scalar quantization, the other PQ with a re-rank from SSD. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Build both on a 10 million sample and compare recall and p95 at the memory each leaves free.

## Common mistakes

- Raising nlist and leaving nprobe where it was.
- Reading nprobe / nlist as the fraction of vectors searched when list sizes are skewed.
- Never retraining centroids after the corpus grows six times.
- Adding PQ for latency when the index fits in RAM and int8 would do.
- Shipping PQ without a full-precision re-rank and reporting the lower recall as the index's best.
