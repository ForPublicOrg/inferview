## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"How would you evaluate an answer's citation quality? Our current check passes an answer when every citation points to a retrieved document the judge rates relevant to the question. What does that check miss?"
</Callout>

What you learn when you ask, as a scenario:

- An answer engine over product documentation and support articles serves 50,000 answers a day. Each answer averages six cited claims.
- Retrieved passages go to the model as `search_result` blocks, one block per article, with citations enabled.
- The current document-level check passes 97% of sampled answers.
- You have two weeks and one support analyst for 15 hours. A claim-citation pair took her about a minute to judge in a trial.
- Answers stream to users. The product lead wants no added latency.

Say what you would measure, how you would label it within the budget, and what the current check misses.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask what the <code>cited_text</code> of a typical citation contains today, and how long it is.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Request ten answers where the analyst disagrees with the current check, and look at which claim each disagreement is about.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask what share of claims carry a number, a version or a date.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>A citation is correct only if the cited span supports the claim it is attached to, so the unit of evaluation is the claim, not the answer or the document. Split each answer into claims, pair each claim with the text its citations point to, and label support as supported, partial, unsupported or contradicted. Report citation precision (citations whose span supports their claim) and citation recall (claims that need support and have a supporting citation). The document-level check passes a relevant document whose cited span says something else: the 2025 row of a price table cited for a 2026 price. And with one block per article, the cited span is the whole article, so today no check can be finer than the document. Split articles into paragraph blocks first.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Make spans exist.</strong> In the search results API the text block is the smallest citable unit, and the docs say that putting a result in one block means every citation returns the full text. Today's <code>cited_text</code> is a whole article, so "the span supports the claim" and "the document supports the claim" are the same test. Split each article into paragraph-sized blocks, keeping headings and table header rows with their blocks. The API guarantees that citations are valid pointers into what you sent. It says nothing about whether the span supports the claim; that is what you are measuring.</p>
<p><strong>Labels.</strong> For each claim: the claim text, its cited spans, and a label. Partial means the span supports part of the claim (one of two facts, or the right figure with the wrong condition). Contradicted is separate from unsupported, because a citation that says the opposite is worse than one that says nothing. Claims that need no support (a greeting, a restated question) are marked and excluded from recall.</p>
<p><strong>Judge and budget.</strong> 15 hours at a minute per pair is 900 pairs: 150 answers at six claims each. An LLM judge reads claim and span and returns the label; the analyst's 900 labels are split, 300 for tuning the judge's prompt and 600 held out to measure it once. If 12% of pairs are unsupported or contradicted, the 600 contain about 72 positives, and a judge true positive rate of 0.8 is known to within about ±9 points (1.96 × √(0.8 × 0.2 / 72) ≈ 0.092). Sampling 150 answers at random is reasonable for a first number; oversampling answers with numbers, versions and dates is better for finding failures, as long as the reported rate is reweighted back to traffic. Spending the whole budget on random answers is the unreasonable choice if failures are rare: at a 3% failure rate the held-out set would hold about 18 positives, and the true positive rate interval would be wider than ±18 points.</p>
<p><strong>Offline sample or online gate.</strong> Checking every claim before it is shown adds a judge call per claim to a streaming answer. Measuring on a daily sample adds no latency and protects no one. The conflict is latency against correctness, and both are defensible: a sample of a few hundred answers a day for the metric, plus an online check only on claims that carry a number, a version or a price, held until verified, is where I would start. Its cost is a visible delay on exactly the answers users check most.</p>
</Fragment>

### What a document-level check passes

- **Right document, wrong span.** The pricing article has rows for 2025 and 2026; the answer states the 2026 price and the citation points at the 2025 row, or at the article as a whole. Relevant document, unsupported claim.
- **Two facts, one citation.** "Available on the Team plan and limited to 10 seats" cites the plan page, which supports the first half; the seat limit came from a different article or from the model.
- **Post-hoc citation.** The claim came from the model's training data and was attached to a topical document. The document is relevant; nothing in it says the claim.
- **Contradiction.** The cited paragraph says the feature is not available on Windows. A relevance judge rates the document highly, because it is about exactly this.
- **Stale version.** Two versions of an article are retrieved; the claim follows the old one. Both are relevant.

The Perplexity article describes splitting documents into spans that are retrieved and ranked individually, which is what makes span-level citations possible at all; its published evaluation reports answer accuracy on SimpleQA, FRAMES, BrowseComp and HLE, which is a different question from whether each cited span supports its claim. A system can score well on one and badly on the other.

### Where this fails

Paragraph spans lose context. "It costs $20 per seat" is supported only if "it" is the plan in the claim, and the antecedent may be in the previous block. A judge that reads only the span marks it unsupported; a judge that reads the whole article is a document-level check again. Give the judge the cited span plus the preceding block and the section heading as context, and credit support only when the cited span carries the fact. That rule will still mark some correct citations as partial, and the analyst's labels are where you find out how often.

## Follow-ups

- *Legal wants every published number to be supported by its citation. Which part of your design changes from a sample to a gate, and what does it cost the answers that carry numbers?*
- *The team proposes to stop showing citations the judge marks unsupported instead of fixing the answer. What does that do to citation recall, and to user trust when the claim stays?*
- *A second model will generate the answers next quarter. What do you re-run before trusting the old judge on its citations?*

## Common mistakes

- Scoring citation quality per answer or per document, so one supported claim hides an unsupported one.
- Treating the API's valid-pointer guarantee as a support guarantee.
- Passing whole articles as single blocks and then asking for span-level evaluation.
- Merging contradicted and unsupported into one label.
- Reporting a judge's support rate without measuring the judge against human labels on held-out pairs.
