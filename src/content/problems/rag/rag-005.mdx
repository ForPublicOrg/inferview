## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"The corpus is 50 million chunks. Does your index still fit in memory, and what happens when it doesn't?"
</Callout>

The chunks are embedded with OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small, which returns 1,536 dimensions by default (OpenAI docs, checked 2026-10-28). They sit in one HNSW index with float32 vectors. The database node has 256 GB of RAM; that figure is a scenario.

Estimate the memory for the vectors plus the graph links, in GB, leaving out chunk text and metadata. Choose the graph degree `M` yourself and say why. Then say whether it fits, which change you would make so it does, and what that change costs.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask how the index library you are using sizes its graph per element, and for which values of `M`.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how much of the node's RAM is already spoken for by the database's own buffers, the operating system and other tables.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask whether a recall target exists, measured against exact search, before you pick a compression level.</p></details>

<p>The vectors are 50 million x 1,536 x 4 bytes = 307.2 GB, and the graph adds 6.4 to 8 GB at <code>M</code> = 16, so about 315 GB against 256 GB of RAM: it does not fit, and the vectors are the dominant term, 38 to 48 times the graph. halfvec brings it to about 161 GB, which fits with less headroom than it looks; binary quantization brings the search pass to about 17 GB with float vectors on disk for rescoring. Once you quantize to binary, the graph links are almost half of what is in RAM, and <code>M</code> becomes the number that matters.</p>

<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>The vectors.</strong> 1,536 x 4 = 6,144 bytes per vector; 50,000,000 x 6,144 = 307.2 GB (286 GiB).</p>
<p><strong>The graph.</strong> hnswlib documents the graph as roughly <code>M</code> x 8 to 10 bytes per element, and that is the only published per-element figure used here; pgvector lays its index out in Postgres pages, so measure it there on a sample before trusting the multiplier. pgvector's default <code>m</code> is 16, which gives 128 to 160 bytes per element, 6.4 to 8 GB. <code>M</code> = 48, the top of hnswlib's usual range, gives 19.2 to 24 GB.</p>
<p><strong>The total</strong> is 312 to 331 GB across <code>M</code> of 12 to 48, inside the accepted band of 290 to 340 GB, which also covers the same totals in GiB. The vectors are at least 92% of it at any reasonable <code>M</code>.</p>
<p><strong>What happens when it does not fit.</strong> HNSW search is a chain of dependent reads, one neighbour list after another. In RAM each hop is cheap; once the working set spills, hops become disk reads, p95 latency climbs, and recall does not change, so the quality dashboards stay green while latency alerts fire.</p>
<p><strong>The levers, with their costs.</strong> halfvec halves the vectors to 153.6 GB, about 161 GB with the graph at <code>M</code> = 16; it fits, but leaves under 100 GB for shared buffers, the operating system and every other table, and its recall cost is yours to measure. int8 scalar quantization is 76.8 GB; Hugging Face measured about 97% of retrieval performance kept, and about 99% with rescoring, on one 1,024-dimension model. Binary quantization is 50,000,000 x 1,536 / 8 = 9.6 GB, about 17 GB with the graph; Hugging Face measured 92.53% kept without rescoring and 96.45% with it on that model, and Qdrant reports 0.9847 recall for text-embedding-3-small with 3x oversampling and rescoring, its own measurement. Shortening to 512 dimensions with the <code>dimensions</code> parameter cuts float32 to 102.4 GB at a quality cost you measure on your queries. Sharding across two nodes halves the per-node footprint and adds a fan-out and a merge to every query.</p>

### The calculation

| Format | Bytes per vector | Vectors, 50 million | With graph at `M` = 16 |
|---|---|---|---|
| float32 (`vector`) | 1,536 x 4 = 6,144 | 307.2 GB | 313.6 to 315.2 GB |
| float16 (`halfvec`) | 1,536 x 2 = 3,072 | 153.6 GB | 160.0 to 161.6 GB |
| int8 | 1,536 x 1 = 1,536 | 76.8 GB | 83.2 to 84.8 GB |
| binary (`bit`) | 1,536 / 8 = 192 | 9.6 GB | 16.0 to 17.6 GB |

**The dominant term and the assumption that flips it.** At full precision the vectors dominate and `M` barely matters: going from 16 to 48 moves the total by 4 to 5%. Under binary quantization the graph at `M` = 16 is 6.4 to 8 GB against 9.6 GB of codes, and at `M` = 48 the graph is 19.2 to 24 GB, twice the codes. The quantization decision decides which choice of `M` is affordable, so tune them together.

**What is unreasonable.** Forgetting the 4 bytes per float and answering 76.8 GB. An `M` near 100 without a recall measurement that needs it. Treating a total that fits in exactly 256 GB as fitting, when the database's own buffers and the operating system share the same RAM. Mixing GB and GiB and then arguing about a 7% gap.

**The constraint conflict** is memory against recall. Every lever that fits the index in RAM loses some recall unless a rescoring pass reads full vectors, and the rescoring pass brings back the disk reads quantization removed. Oversampling is the dial between the two.

**The failure mode of the recommended answer.** Binary quantization with float vectors on disk fits the search pass in RAM but moves the cost to rescoring reads. If oversampling is raised to recover recall, the disk reads per query rise with it, and the system that was fixed for memory becomes disk-bound at peak traffic.

## Follow-ups

- *Would you switch to text-embedding-3-large?* Its 3,072 dimensions double every row of the table, and pgvector's `vector` type indexes only up to 2,000 dimensions, so you would need `halfvec` or a shorter `dimensions` setting regardless.
- *How do you budget the graph in pgvector without a published figure?* Build the index on a 1 million row sample, read its size from the database, divide by the row count and multiply out.
- *Is 50 million the right count?* Ask how it was derived: chunk overlap, duplicate documents and boilerplate pages inflate it, and deduplication is cheaper than any quantization.

## Common mistakes

- Stopping at the raw vectors and calling it the index size.
- Applying hnswlib's per-element figure to pgvector without measuring.
- Proposing binary quantization without rescoring or a recall measurement against exact search.
- Planning to use all 256 GB for the index.
