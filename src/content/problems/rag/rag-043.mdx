## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Design a two-stage retrieval and ranking system for a short-term rental marketplace using user and listing embeddings. Tell me where the labels come from, what you do about position bias, and how you evaluate it."
</Callout>

The constraints, as a scenario given by the interviewer:

- 6 million active listings. A search has a destination, dates and a guest count, and the listing must be available for those dates.
- For the median destination and dates, about 800 listings are available. The 50 largest cities have 20,000 to 120,000.
- The second stage is an existing gradient-boosted ranker owned by another team. It scores up to 2,000 candidates within its 60 ms p95 slot, and it stays this half. Your stage has 50 ms at p95.
- Logs hold impressions with positions (list and map), clicks, wishlist saves, booking requests, host rejections, bookings and cancellations.
- The marketplace team wants new listings to get exposure. The finance team judges every launch by bookings.
- Six engineers, one half-year.

Write the design: candidate generation, the embeddings, the training labels, position bias, evaluation, and a rollout plan with rollback triggers.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Availability for the dates and the destination are hard filters, and for most searches they leave fewer listings than the ranker can score, so in those markets the first stage is the filter and the embeddings are features. Only in large markets do I need a learned first stage: a query tower (destination, dates, guests, the user's recent interactions) against listing embeddings, scored over the available listings, keeping the top 2,000 for the ranker. Train on booking-oriented labels with in-search negatives and host rejections as negatives, handle position bias with position as a training feature that is dropped at scoring, and evaluate offline with NDCG of the booked listing plus retrieval recall against exhaustive scoring, then online on bookings with guardrails for cancellations, host rejections and new-listing exposure.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Candidate generation.</strong> At the median, 800 available listings fit inside the ranker's 2,000, so retrieval adds nothing but risk; filter and send them all. Above 2,000, the first stage must choose. Scoring exhaustively is cheaper than it sounds: with 64-dimensional embeddings, the largest market is 120,000 × 64 ≈ 7.7 million multiply-adds per search, about 31 MB of float32 vectors read. Measure that on the serving hardware before reaching for an index. An ANN index per market is the alternative, and availability is what makes it awkward: post-filtering after an approximate scan starves on peak dates when few listings are free (pgvector's own arithmetic is 4 of 40 candidates at a 10% match rate), so it needs availability as a pre-filter or an iterative scan.</p>
<p><strong>Listing embeddings.</strong> Trained from search sessions: listings clicked or saved in the same session are close, the booked listing is a positive for every listing in its session, and negatives come from the same market so the model learns more than geography. A new listing gets the average embedding of nearby listings with the same type, capacity and price band until it has interactions.</p>
<p><strong>User embeddings.</strong> Two parts: a short-term vector from this session's clicks and saves, and a long-term vector from past bookings and wishlists. The Airbnb paper treats users as being in continuous cold start for practical purposes, so the query tower must work with both parts empty and the evaluation must be split by history.</p>
<p><strong>Labels.</strong> Bookings are the target finance measures and the sparsest signal. Clicks are plentiful and reward photos and low prices. I train the retrieval tower on bookings and booking requests as positives, impressed-but-not-booked listings from the same search as negatives, and host rejections as negatives, because a listing a host rejects for these dates should not be retrieved for them. Clicks and saves enter as auxiliary positives with a lower weight, which you choose and tune on offline NDCG.</p>
<p><strong>Position bias.</strong> Every logged label was produced by the old ranker's ordering. The Airbnb paper adds position as a feature, regularised with dropout (0.15, chosen by a sweep), and sets it to 0 at scoring; the paper reports +0.7% bookings and +1.8% revenue online. The alternative is propensity weighting in the style of Joachims et al., with propensities estimated from a small randomised swap of adjacent positions.</p>
</Fragment>

### The decisions and what each costs

**Exhaustive scoring or ANN in large markets.** Exhaustive scoring over the available set is exact, has no index to rebuild when availability changes every minute, and costs CPU that grows with the market. A per-market ANN index costs less per query and brings back the filter problem and an index lifecycle. I would score exhaustively and set the trigger for ANN as a measured p95 above 30 ms of the 50 ms slot in the largest market; the remaining 20 ms covers fetching features. Choosing ANN everywhere for uniformity is unreasonable here: every search below 2,000 available listings, which includes the median one, would pay its recall loss to save time it already has.

**Position as a feature or propensity weighting.** Position-as-feature needs no randomisation and no propensity model. Its cost is an assumption the paper states: the position effect is taken to be independent of the listing, and the dropout rate trades accurate bias estimation against the model leaning on position. Propensity weighting rests on fewer assumptions about the model, costs bookings while the randomisation runs, and has high-variance weights at low positions. I would start with position-as-feature because it can ship without a randomisation budget, and run a small swap experiment in parallel to check the bias estimate.

**The quantity you choose: how many candidates.** The ranker takes 2,000. Sending fewer saves the ranker's time and loses recall; the right number is where retrieval recall of the eventually booked listing, measured against exhaustive scoring on a sample of large-market searches, stops rising. Sending 200 is unreasonable: the ranker was trained on candidate sets ten times larger, and its features (price percentile within the result set, for example) shift.

### Evaluation

- **Retrieval stage:** recall@2,000 of the booked listing against exhaustive scoring over the same available set, split by market size and by user history (none, short-term only, long-term).
- **End to end, offline:** NDCG of the booked listing on held-out searches. The Airbnb paper calls it their most reliable gauge and reports significant booking differences from NDCG differences as small as 0.7%, which is the resolution the offline set needs: size it so a 0.7% difference is outside the interval.
- **Offline blind spot:** logged searches only show listings the old system ranked. A listing the new retrieval surfaces that was never shown has no label; count those per search, the same way an IR pool counts unjudged documents.
- **Online:** A/B on bookings, with guardrails on cancellations, host rejection rate and new listings' share of first-page impressions. The paper reports a 6% NDCG gap for new listings, so the guardrail is not hypothetical.

### What you give up, and the rollout

Given up: perfect consistency between markets (two candidate paths), some short-term bookings in exchange for new-listing exposure, and a learned first stage in small markets.

Rollout: weeks 1 to 6, embeddings and offline evaluation; weeks 7 to 10, shadow scoring in the 50 largest cities with recall and latency logged against the current candidate set; then an A/B in ten large cities. Rollback triggers: bookings down beyond the experiment's preregistered threshold for seven days, host rejection rate up by more than its weekly variation, the retrieval stage's p95 above 50 ms for an hour, or new listings' first-page share below the current level.

### Where this fails

Peak dates are the failure case. When a large city is nearly sold out, the available set shrinks to a few hundred listings and everything works; when it is 20% available, most of the embedding neighbourhood the user would like is booked, and the system falls back to whatever is free. That is correct behaviour and it looks like a relevance regression on the dashboard, so the evaluation must be split by availability. The second failure is the bias treatment's assumption: if the position effect depends on the listing (a striking photo draws clicks lower in the list), setting position to 0 does not remove all of it, and the model keeps learning the old ranker's preferences.

## Follow-ups

- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants clicks as the main label for volume, the other bookings for alignment. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Train both, compare NDCG of the booked listing on the same held-out searches and the offline recall split by user history, then run the winner online.
- *A host complains her listing never appears for her own city. How do you debug which stage dropped it?*
- *You have 60 days before the ranker team retires its model for a new one trained on your candidates' logs. What changes in your evaluation, and who signs off on the switch?*

## Common mistakes

- Building ANN retrieval for every search when filters already leave fewer candidates than the ranker scores.
- Training on clicks as if position did not produce them.
- Evaluating offline only on listings the old system showed, and calling the result unbiased.
- Ignoring host rejections, so retrieval keeps surfacing listings that will not accept the booking.
- Averaging metrics over all users when most have little or no history.
