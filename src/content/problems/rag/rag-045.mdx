## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"Build a candidate search system. Recruiters type what the client needs, and we return the people to contact. Some filters are hard: if a candidate must be excluded, they can never appear. The structured data comes from resumes and is noisy."
</Callout>

The constraints, as a scenario:

- 3 million candidate profiles: resume text parsed from uploads, fields extracted by a parser or self-reported at signup (country, work authorisation per country, years per skill, hourly rate, availability), and platform records (opted out of contact, blocked by a client, already submitted to a client in the last 90 days).
- An audit of 500 random profiles found work authorisation missing on 31%, years of experience off by more than two years on 18%, and availability not updated in 60 days on 45%.
- Must exclude, every time: candidates who opted out, candidates a client blocked, candidates already submitted to the same client within 90 days, and, when the client requires it, candidates not authorised to work in the role's country.
- A query mixes text and constraints: "oncologist for evaluation of medical answers, licensed in the US, 20 hours a week, under $150 an hour".
- Postgres 17 with pgvector 0.8.7 is the only datastore this quarter. Four engineers. Search page p95 of 800 ms.
- Legal wants every exclusion explainable and treats the tool as one used in hiring decisions. The client success lead wants no query to return zero results.

Design the filters, retrieval and ranking, the evaluation, and say what you give up.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Split the filters by where the truth lives. Opt-outs, client blocks and recent submissions come from systems of record; they are applied exactly, at query time, fail closed, and checked again at submission. Work authorisation comes from a parser and self-reports, so it is tri-state (verified, self-reported, unknown) and the query decides what each state means. Years, rate and availability are too noisy to filter on and become ranking features. Retrieval is hybrid, BM25 for licences, titles and named skills and dense retrieval for experience descriptions, both restricted to eligible profiles before scoring, then a reranker over the top candidates that also writes the reason each one matched. Evaluation is two separate things: filter correctness as an invariant with zero tolerance, and relevance as graded recruiter judgments on pooled results.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Compliance exclusions.</strong> Opt-outs and blocks change outside the search index, and a copy in the index is stale by design. The query joins against the live tables (an anti-join on candidate id) inside the candidate-generation queries, so excluded people never enter the pool, and the response path asserts the same condition on the final list before returning it. Submission re-checks it, because a recruiter can hold a result page for an hour. If the system of record is unreachable, search returns an error, not unfiltered results.</p>
<p><strong>Eligibility from noisy fields.</strong> Work authorisation missing on 31% of profiles is about 930,000 people. Excluding unknowns protects the client and removes them from every role that requires authorisation; including them risks submitting people who cannot legally take the job. My choice: exclude profiles verified or self-reported as not authorised, show unknowns in a separate, labelled section that cannot be submitted until the candidate confirms, and send them a verification request. Licences work the same way, with the licensing board's lookup as the verified state where one exists.</p>
<p><strong>Preferences as features.</strong> With 18% of experience values off by more than two years, a hard filter of "at least five years" excludes qualified people because of the parser. Rate, availability and years become features with their freshness: an availability value older than 60 days counts for less, and the result card shows its age.</p>
<p><strong>Retrieval.</strong> Two legs over eligible profiles: BM25 over titles, licences, certifications and skills, where exact terms decide relevance, and dense retrieval over experience narratives. pgvector filters after an approximate scan, and a narrow combination such as one specialty in one country matches a small share of 3 million rows, which starves the scan; for those, the filtered set goes to exact distance computation, and wider queries use an iterative scan. The legs are merged by rank fusion into the top 100.</p>
<p><strong>Reranking and reasons.</strong> An LLM reranker reads the query and a structured summary of each of the 100 profiles (about 400 tokens each, so about 40,000 input tokens per search), returns a grade and a one-sentence reason grounded in profile fields. The reasons are what a recruiter reads, and what legal reviews for explainability.</p>
</Fragment>

### The numbers you choose

**How many profiles to rerank.** More candidates raise recall and cost tokens and latency linearly. 100 profiles at 400 tokens is 40,000 input tokens per search; send them as parallel batches and measure p95 against the 800 ms budget. Choose the number where recall of the recruiters' eventually shortlisted candidates, measured on past requisitions, stops rising. Reranking 1,000 is unreasonable at this budget; reranking 10 makes the reranker a reorder of the fusion list.

**When to switch to exact distance.** At 1,024 dimensions, a vector is 4 × 1,024 + 8 bytes, so 20,000 profiles are about 82 MB of vectors per query. Measure exact-scan p95 at a few filtered-set sizes and set the cut-over where it stays inside its share of the budget.

**How stale availability may be before it stops counting.** 60 days matches the audit's definition; a shorter window drops most of the 45% of profiles that have not updated, which is defensible for urgent roles and wrong for the default.

### Evaluation

- **Filter correctness, zero tolerance.** A property test on the serving path: random queries against a fixture with planted opted-out, blocked and recently submitted candidates, asserting none appears. In production, a daily join of every submission against the systems of record; one violation is an incident.
- **Relevance.** 200 recent requisitions, the top 20 from each candidate configuration pooled and graded 0 to 3 by two recruiters per vertical, reported as nDCG@20 by vertical and by query narrowness.
- **Outcomes.** Interviews and hires per shortlisted candidate, read with care: recruiters choose whom to contact from the order shown, so outcome labels carry the system's own position bias.
- **Audit.** Annex III of the EU AI Act lists systems that filter applications and evaluate candidates as high-risk, and New York City requires a bias audit within a year before use of an automated employment decision tool, a public summary and candidate notices. Selection rates by group, where the data may lawfully be used, are part of the release gate, and the exclusion reasons are logged per query.

### What you give up

The 930,000 profiles with unknown authorisation leave the main list for roles that require it, and some of them are qualified. Recruiters lose the ability to submit an unknown candidate quickly. Queries that combine several strict constraints sometimes return a short list or none, and the client success lead's "no zero results" is refused in writing: an empty list is shown with the constraint that emptied it, never relaxed silently. The live anti-join adds a dependency on the systems of record to every search.

**Rollout.** Shadow mode for three weeks next to the current search, comparing lists and logging every candidate the new system excludes that the old one showed, with the reason. Rollback triggers: any compliance exclusion found in a shown list or a submission, a relevance drop beyond the pooled evaluation's interval in any vertical, or a selection-rate ratio between groups outside the threshold legal sets before launch.

### Where this fails

The tri-state model is only as good as the verification loop. If candidates do not answer, the unknown section grows, recruiters learn to ignore it, and the system quietly becomes "exclude unknowns" with none of the explanation. Watch the verification response rate and the share of shortlists drawn from the unknown section. The second failure is disparate impact: if missing authorisation is not spread evenly across nationalities, a rule that is neutral on its face excludes one group far more than another. That is what the selection-rate check is for, and it can force a different treatment of unknowns than the one chosen here.

## Follow-ups

- *A client blocks a candidate while a recruiter has the results page open, and the recruiter submits ten minutes later. Walk through which check stops it.*
- *Legal asks for the reason a specific candidate did not appear for a search last month. What do you need to have logged to answer?*
- *The parser vendor ships a new model that fills work authorisation on 20% more profiles. How do you decide whether those values count as self-reported, verified, or neither?*

## Common mistakes

- Copying opt-outs and blocks into the search index and filtering on the copy.
- Treating a missing parsed value as false, or as true, without deciding which, per filter, and writing it down.
- Turning noisy preferences such as years of experience into hard filters.
- Post-filtering an approximate scan on a narrow combination and returning a short list as if it were complete.
- Relaxing constraints silently to avoid an empty result.
