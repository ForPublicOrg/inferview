## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The prompt">
"We have no labelled data and no annotators this quarter. How would you measure faithfulness on our RAG assistant every day, and what would you not trust about the judge you pick?"
</Callout>

The numbers are a scenario. The assistant answers about 40,000 questions a day. A prompt averages 3,000 tokens of context and an answer 200 tokens. The evaluation budget is a fixed amount per day that the platform team will not raise this quarter. Pick the judge, the daily sample size, and say what you would build first.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether the logs keep the context that was sent to the model, after reranking and truncation, or only the retrieved ids.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask how large a daily change in the score the team needs to see, and how fast.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask whether anyone, an engineer or a support lead, can label 200 claims once, even if nobody can label every week.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Score a daily random sample of logged answers with claim-level faithfulness: split each answer into claims, check each claim against the exact context the model was sent, report supported claims over total claims. The judge is either an LLM doing extraction and verification, as Ragas does, or a small classifier such as HHEM-2.1-Open for verification. "No labels" holds for the daily run but not for trusting the judge: label about 200 claims once and measure the judge's agreement before reading any trend. Never trust it to measure correctness, to be stable across judge versions, or to mean the same thing when the claim extractor changes.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><b>The measurement.</b> For each sampled answer, extract claims, verify each against the context that was in the prompt, and compute supported over total, as both the Ragas paper and its docs define it. Report the per-answer mean, the share of answers with any unsupported claim, and the refusal rate separately, since a refusal has no claims and its faithfulness is undefined. The judge has to see the context actually sent (Hint 1); judging against the retrieved list credits the model with chunks that were cut before the prompt.</p>
<p><b>The judge: two defensible choices.</b> An LLM judge for both extraction and verification handles paraphrase, numbers in different units and multi-sentence claims, at the cost of two model calls per answer and a judge whose behaviour moves with its version. At this scenario's sizes that is about 2 x 3,500 = 7,000 input tokens per answer, so 500 answers a day is 3.5 million judge tokens. A classifier for verification, with an LLM only for extraction, halves the calls and runs on CPU (the Ragas docs load HHEM-2.1-Open on CPU with a batch size of 10 by default), and gives the same verdict every run; its accuracy on your documents, long contexts and tables is unknown until you measure it. I would start with the LLM judge on a small sample and move verification to the classifier only if the 200-claim check shows it agrees as well.</p>
<p><b>The sample size is yours to choose.</b> Daily scores exist to catch a change. To see a move from 0.90 to 0.85 in the share of supported claims, a 95% margin of about 3 points needs n = 1.96^2 x 0.9 x 0.1 / 0.03^2, about 384 claims. Claims inside one answer are correlated, so count answers, not claims: with about four claims per answer, 300 to 500 answers a day is a reasonable range. Fifty answers a day only sees collapses; 5,000 spends the budget on precision nobody acts on. If the budget allows fewer, pool by week and say so.</p>
<p><b>The constraint conflict</b> is labelling budget against trust. The brief says no labels, and a judge with unknown agreement makes every trend uninterpretable. One labelling pass of about 200 claims, a few hours of one person, is the cheapest way to know whether a three-point drop is real. The Ragas paper shows why it matters: on its WikiEval set, three judging procedures agreed with humans at 0.95, 0.72 and 0.54 on the same data, and that set is 50 Wikipedia pages, not your policies.</p>
</Fragment>

<ul>
<li><em>The judge vendor ships a new version and the score rises four points overnight. What do you do before telling anyone?</em> Rerun yesterday's sample with the new judge; if the gap is there on the same answers, it is the judge.</li>
<li><em>Your judge budget halves next month. Do you cut the sample or move to the classifier, and what risk do you write down?</em></li>
<li><em>What would make you stop reporting faithfulness daily?</em> Weekly numbers that never move outside their margin for a quarter, in which case the daily run is a cost with no decision attached.</li>
</ul>

### What I would not trust about the judge

1. **Faithful is not correct.** The score measures agreement with the context. A faithful answer from last year's policy is faithfully wrong. Track correctness separately once there is a labelled set.
2. **The extractor sets the denominator.** The Ragas docs split "Einstein was born in Germany on 20th March 1879" into two statements, one supported, for 0.5. Split it into three (Germany; 20 March; 1879) and the same answer scores 2/3. Comparing two pipelines scored by two extractor versions compares the extractors. Pin the extraction prompt and model.
3. **The judge knows things.** A verifier can mark a claim supported because it is true, not because the context says it. Plant a few claims that are true in the world and absent from the context, and check they come back unsupported.
4. **The wrong context.** If the logs only keep retrieved ids, the judge sees chunks the model never saw, and faithfulness rises for exactly the answers that went wrong at context assembly.
5. **Versions move.** A judge model update or a library change moves the score with no change in the system. The Ragas docs mark the legacy metrics API for deprecation in 0.4 and removal in 1.0, so pin both and record them with every score.
6. **The judge disagrees with itself.** Run the same 100 answers twice and report self-agreement; a change smaller than the judge's own disagreement is not a change.

The failure mode of this design: the daily number becomes the quality metric. A model update that refuses more often raises faithfulness, because refusals drop out and the answers that remain are the easy ones. Refusal rate and faithfulness are read together or not at all.

## Follow-ups

- *Support says users still get wrong answers while faithfulness is 0.96. Where do you look first?* The documents the answers cited: stale or wrong content gives faithful wrong answers, and Missing Content gives faithful partial ones.
- *Could you skip extraction and ask the judge one question per answer, "is this answer supported?" What do you lose?* The denominator: one unsupported sentence in a long answer and a wholly invented answer get the same verdict.
- *Your colleague wants to use the production model as the judge to save cost. What would you need to see to agree?* Agreement on the 200 labelled claims at least as high as the alternative, and self-agreement measured, since the same model judging its own outputs is the case you have least evidence about.

## Common mistakes

- Judging against retrieved ids instead of the context sent.
- Reporting a trend from a judge whose agreement with humans was never measured.
- Averaging refusals in as 0 or 1.
- Comparing scores across judge or extractor versions.
- Treating faithfulness as correctness.
