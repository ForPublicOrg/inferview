checklist:
  - Builds the query set from the design partner's tasks first and pilot logs later, stratified by query type, with a written need description per query and a size defended against the labelling budget
  - Pools every candidate system to a stated depth and measures a new system's hole rate before comparing it on existing labels
  - Defines a graded scale with written guidelines and examples from the customer's own content, including superseded versions and near-duplicates
  - Handles permissions in the pool, so a human assessor only sees documents they may open and the LLM assessor's access is approved by the customer's security team
  - Measures agreement between the two experts on a double-labelled slice before treating either as the reference
  - Chooses the LLM prompt on one split of the human labels and measures it once on a held-out split
  - Accepts the LLM assessor on agreement in system rankings and on the launch decision, not only on per-label kappa, with thresholds chosen and defended
  - Checks whether LLM and human labels disagree more on documents surfaced only by the system with the LLM reranker
  - Computes the human budget, how it is spent, and the smallest NDCG difference the human slice can confirm
  - Keeps a weekly human audit of LLM labels with recalibration triggers (assessor model or prompt change, new content source, a shift in disagreement)
  - Chooses between human-only and LLM-primary labelling, with the cost of each and what is given up
  - Writes a decision memo the compliance lead can sign, with the conditions under which LLM labels stop being used

## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="The round">
"We launch enterprise search for our first customers in eight weeks. We have three retrieval stacks and no relevance labels. Design the judgment workflow that tells us which stack to launch, and keeps telling us whether search got better or worse after launch."
</Callout>

The constraints, as a scenario:

- The design partner has 4 million documents across a wiki, a ticket system and a shared drive, with per-document permissions.
- Candidates: BM25, a dense retriever, and hybrid retrieval followed by an LLM reranker.
- A pilot with 400 of the partner's employees starts in week 2 and produces the first query logs. Before that there are no queries, only interviews with the partner's teams.
- The contract forbids sending documents to crowd workers. Two of the partner's domain experts can label 6 hours a week each for six weeks. In a one-hour trial they averaged 45 seconds per query-document judgment.
- An LLM assessor is allowed through the company's approved model provider. Scenario price: $0.004 per judgment.
- In the trial, pooling the three stacks to depth 10 gave 21 unique documents per query on average, and depth 20 gave 38.
- The search lead wants LLM labels only, to move fast. The partner's compliance lead will not sign a quality claim that no human checked.

Design the workflow: queries, pools, grades, assessors, calibration, and what runs after launch.

## Answer

### The budget first

Human judgments available: 2 experts × 6 hours × 6 weeks = 72 hours; 72 × 3,600 / 45 = 5,760 judgments. Every design below is a way of spending those.

### The steps

1. **Queries.** Weeks 1 and 2: the experts write 100 queries from interviews with the partner's teams (finding a known document, a policy lookup, a ticket by error code, an exploratory question, finding a person or owner), each with two sentences describing the need. From week 2: sample pilot queries stratified by type and frequency, and the experts add the need description. Thomas et al. found the narrative alone added 0.06 kappa and asking for aspects added 0.21 on TREC-Robust, and they warn that queries sampled from a running system without descriptions would be labelled less accurately; the description is the cheapest accuracy there is.
2. **Pools.** Union of the top 20 from each stack, per query. Any later stack is compared only after its hole rate is measured: the share of its top 10 that was never judged. BEIR's TREC-COVID pool left 31.8% of TAS-B's top 10 unjudged against 6.4% for BM25, and judging those holes moved ANCE from just below BM25 to 6.7 points above it. Unjudged counted as irrelevant is a vote for whichever system built the pool.
3. **Grades.** 0 to 3, the TREC Deep Learning scale UMBRELA uses (irrelevant, related, highly relevant, perfectly relevant), with guidelines written on the partner's content: a superseded policy version is at most 1, a near-duplicate takes the grade of its original, a ticket that answers the question only in a comment is 2.
4. **Permissions.** Each pooled document is routed to an expert who can open it; a document neither can open is marked unjudgeable, not irrelevant, and counted. The LLM assessor runs under a service account the partner's security team approves, with the provider's data handling in the contract.
5. **Human agreement.** 200 query-document pairs labelled by both experts (400 judgments), disagreements discussed, guidelines revised. The resulting kappa is the ceiling the LLM is measured against, not a fixed number from a paper.
6. **LLM calibration.** 1,200 human judgments split 600 for prompt selection and 600 held out. Prompt variants and paraphrases are tried on the first 600 only; Thomas et al. saw kappa from 0.50 to 0.72 across 42 paraphrases of one prompt, so picking the best on the same labels you report on inflates the number. The held-out 600 are scored once.
7. **Labelling.** The LLM labels the full pools for 1,000 queries at depth 20: 38,000 judgments, about $152 at the scenario price.
8. **The launch slice.** The remaining human judgments label a separate set of queries for the decision between the two best stacks.
9. **After launch.** Weekly, 150 LLM labels drawn stratified by query type and by which stack surfaced the document are relabelled by an expert, and the disagreement rate and its pattern are tracked, as Bing describes doing.

### Two defensible designs

| | Human labels only | LLM-primary, human-calibrated |
|---|---|---|
| Human spend | 400 for agreement, 5,360 on pools | 400 agreement, 1,200 calibration, 900 audit over six weeks, 3,260 launch slice |
| Queries judged | 5,360 / 21 ≈ 255 at depth 10 | 1,000 at depth 20 by the LLM; 3,260 / 21 ≈ 155 by humans |
| What it buys | Every label is a person's; the compliance lead can sign it as is | Coverage of the tail and of query types; any new stack is labelled the same day |
| What it costs | About 50 queries per query type; a new stack after week 6 has no budget for its holes | Per-label accuracy is modest: UMBRELA's four-scale kappa is 0.31 to 0.37 and it gets "highly relevant" right about 30% of the time; the claim rests on rankings agreeing, not labels |

The conflict is labelling budget against coverage, and the deadline against the compliance lead's requirement. I would run the second design, because the team needs to compare dozens of configurations before launch and 255 queries spent once cannot do that, and keep a human-only slice for the claim the compliance lead signs.

### What the LLM assessor has to pass

Per-label kappa is the wrong gate on its own: UMBRELA's labels agree only fairly with TREC assessors and still order TREC systems with Kendall tau between 0.87 and 0.94. What the team needs is the same decisions. On the held-out split and the launch slice:

- Binary kappa within 0.1 of the experts' own kappa. Thomas et al. computed 0.52 between two groups of trained TREC assessors; a ceiling measured here could be lower, and the LLM is held to this team's ceiling.
- Kendall tau of at least 0.8 between the orderings of every configuration evaluated in weeks 3 to 6 under human and under LLM labels, and the same winner between the two finalists. A tau threshold of 0.95 is unreasonable: two human groups do not reach it either.
- No system-dependent disagreement. Thomas et al. flag that LLM labels may favour rankers that use LLMs, and the hybrid stack has an LLM reranker. Compare the disagreement rate on documents surfaced only by that stack with the rate on the rest; use a different model family for the assessor than for the reranker.

### The smallest difference the launch slice confirms

The trial gave a standard deviation of 0.15 for per-query NDCG@10 differences (scenario number; measure it again on pilot queries). With 155 paired queries: 1.96 × 0.15 / √155 ≈ 0.024. The human slice can confirm a gap of about 2.4 NDCG points between finalists; a smaller gap is a tie, and the memo says so. With all 5,760 judgments on humans the figure is 1.96 × 0.15 / √255 ≈ 0.018: buying 0.6 points of resolution costs the whole development loop.

### Where this fails

The calibration holds for the stacks and content that existed in week 6. After months of tuning against LLM labels, the team is optimising toward the assessor's habits, which Thomas et al. name as a risk; and a weekly sample of 150 detects only large shifts. At a 20% baseline disagreement rate, one week's 95% interval is ±1.96 × √(0.2 × 0.8 / 150) ≈ ±6.4 points, so a drift of 3 points takes several weeks of pooled audits to see. The audit is stratified by stack for that reason, and every assessor model or prompt change triggers a full re-run of the held-out 600 before its labels are used. The second failure is the permission routing: if experts can open mostly widely shared documents, the human slice under-represents restricted content, and the calibration says nothing about it. Count unjudgeable documents per stack and report them.

### The memo for the compliance lead

Decision: the launch stack is chosen on human labels for 155 queries; LLM labels are used for development and monitoring only. What we give up: differences under about 2.4 NDCG points are reported as ties; per-query diagnosis from LLM labels is unreliable for the middle grades. Conditions that stop the use of LLM labels: held-out kappa more than 0.1 below the experts' own, tau below 0.8, a stack-dependent disagreement gap on the audit, or an assessor model change that has not been re-validated.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the compliance lead says 155 queries is too few and wants all 5,760 judgments on the human slice. What do you cut from development, and what risk do you accept in writing?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in three months of weekly audits to stop using the LLM assessor for development decisions?*
- *A second customer in a regulated industry forbids sending documents to any model provider. Which steps survive, and what does the launch slice cost then?*
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants 1,000 queries at depth 20, the other 400 at depth 50. How do you decide without deciding by rank?*

## Common mistakes

- Treating unjudged documents as irrelevant when comparing a stack that did not contribute to the pool.
- Tuning the assessor prompt on the same human labels that are then reported as its agreement.
- Gating the LLM assessor on a kappa number from a paper instead of this team's own human agreement and the decision it has to make.
- Sampling pilot queries without the need behind them, then asking anyone, human or model, to judge relevance.
- Routing documents to assessors without checking that they may open them.
