## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Estimate, 30 minutes">
"Design a RAG system for a customer support chatbot: 5 million documents, 300 QPS, p95 latency under 1.2 seconds, budget under $0.002 per query." The interviewer narrows it: "First tell me how you would measure the reranker's latency. Then here is what the team measured. How many candidates can you rerank with Rerank 5 Pro, and would you use Pro at all?"
</Callout>

What the team measured (scenario), all at a steady 300 QPS with reranking off unless stated:

| Measurement | Value |
|---|---|
| Retrieval p95 (query embedding, hybrid search, fusion) | 140 ms |
| Generation p95 | 760 ms |
| End-to-end p95 | 830 ms |
| Retrieval cost per query | $0.0001 |
| Generation cost per query | $0.0008 |
| Tokens per candidate | 40 query + 400 chunk |
| First-stage recall of the labelled answer at 20, 40, 60, 80, 100 | 0.81, 0.88, 0.91, 0.93, 0.94 |

Reranker p95 under the same load (scenario):

| Candidates | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rerank 5 Pro | 135 ms | 223 ms | 311 ms | 399 ms | not measured |
| Rerank 5 Fast | 70 ms | 102 ms | 134 ms | 166 ms | 198 ms |

Prices from Cohere's launch post (vendor, 2026-10-21): Pro $0.05 and Fast $0.02 per million tokens, query and document tokens both billed.

Choose how you combine the stage latencies and how much of the SLO you hold back as headroom. Give the largest number of candidates for Pro, then decide between Pro and Fast.

## Hints

<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 1</summary><p>Ask whether the load generator kept sending at 300 QPS when the reranker slowed down, or waited for responses before sending more.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 2</summary><p>Ask what the end-to-end p95 was at the candidate count you pick, not only each stage's p95 measured alone.</p></details>
<details class="hint"><summary>Hint 3</summary><p>Ask which billing basis the contract uses for Rerank 5: tokens, or search units.</p></details>

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>With Pro, about 43 candidates. I give the reranker the SLO minus the sum of the other stages' p95s, 1,200 − 900 = 300 ms, and hold back 60 ms of headroom; the measured line is 47 ms plus 4.4 ms per candidate, so (240 − 47) / 4.4 ≈ 43. The money allows 50: $0.0011 left per query over $0.000022 per 440-token candidate. Latency binds, and the dominant term is the 4.4 ms slope. But I would ship Fast at 100: it fits in 198 ms and $0.00088, buys first-stage recall from 0.88 to 0.94, and costs less per month. Pro wins only if an eval on our queries shows its per-candidate precision beats six points of recall.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>How I would measure the latency.</strong> An open-loop load generator at the target 300 QPS, so a slow reranker builds a queue instead of quietly lowering the offered load. Production-shaped inputs: real queries and real chunk lengths, not a fixed 440 tokens, because the vendor counts the query and document together against the per-document limit and splits anything longer. The production network path from the serving region. A sweep of candidate counts across the range I might choose, several minutes per point after warm-up, at more than one time of day for a shared hosted API. Record p95 and p99 per count, fit p95 = a + bN only inside the measured range, and read the vendor's rate limits before the results. Last, an end-to-end run at the chosen N, because that is the number the SLO is about.</p>
<p><strong>The latency line.</strong> From the Pro rows: (399 − 135) / (80 − 20) = 4.4 ms per candidate, and 135 − 20 × 4.4 = 47 ms of fixed cost. For Fast: (198 − 70) / 80 = 1.6 ms and 38 ms.</p>
<p><strong>The two choices I make, and the alternatives.</strong> Composition: adding the stage p95s (900 ms) is conservative, because the slow tails of retrieval and generation rarely hit the same request; the measured end-to-end p95 of 830 ms shows by how much. Using 830 gives the reranker 370 ms instead of 300. Headroom: I hold back 60 ms, 5 percent of the SLO, for retries, GC pauses and the reranker's own tail getting worse at peak.</p>

<p>The cost limit is the same in every row: $0.002 − $0.0001 − $0.0008 = $0.0011 left, and a candidate bills 440 tokens × $0.05 per million = $0.000022, so 0.0011 / 0.000022 = 50. Reasonable answers run from 30 to 50; the assumption that flips which constraint binds is the headroom.</p>
<p><strong>The decision, two defensible answers.</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Pro at 43.</strong> 47 + 4.4 × 43 ≈ 236 ms. First-stage recall at 40 is 0.88. Monthly: 300 × 86,400 × 30 = 777.6 million queries × 43 × $0.000022 ≈ $736,000. It buys the vendor's better model per candidate; Cohere's own figures put Pro 1.4 points ahead of Fast on its parsed-PDF benchmark.</li>
<li><strong>Fast at 100.</strong> 38 + 1.6 × 100 = 198 ms, inside the 240 ms budget I chose but not the 180 ms one that 120 ms of headroom leaves. Cost 100 × 440 × $0.02 per million = $0.00088 per query, inside the $0.0011. Recall at 100 is 0.94, six points above 0.88. Monthly ≈ $684,000. It buys more answers in the window and less precision per candidate.</li>
</ul>
<p>This is the constraint conflict: latency and price cap the list, and the list caps recall. The tie-breaker is an eval on our own labelled queries, answer accuracy for Pro at 43 against Fast at 100, because a vendor benchmark on parsed PDFs says nothing about support tickets. Until that eval exists I would ship Fast at 100: the recall gain is measured on our data, the precision gap is not.</p>
<p><strong>Where the recommendation fails.</strong> The load test ran at the average 300 QPS. At a peak of, say, 450 QPS a hosted reranker's tail can move without warning, and the request then has to choose between blowing the SLO and skipping the reranker. So the reranker call gets a timeout at its budget and a fallback to the fused order, and the fallback rate becomes a monitored number: a reranker that silently falls back on 10 percent of peak traffic has lowered answer quality without any latency alert firing.</p>

<li><em>What if the contract bills Rerank 5 in search units?</em> A search unit is one query with up to 100 documents, and documents over 500 tokens including the query are split and counted separately; at 440 tokens per candidate the cost line is flat up to 100 and only latency limits the list.</li>
<li><em>What changes if chunks are cut to 250 tokens?</em> 290 tokens per candidate raises Pro's cost limit to 0.0011 / 0.0000145 ≈ 75, and latency is then the only limit; the slope should also be re-measured, since it was measured at 440 tokens.</li>

### Choices that are not reasonable

- Extending Fast's line to 160 or more candidates from a 300 ms budget. The sweep stopped at 100, and a hosted service's curve can bend past the measured range; measure before committing.
- Using mean latency instead of p95. The SLO is a p95, and per-candidate cost shows up in the tail first.
- Billing only the 400 document tokens. That gives 0.0011 / 0.00002 = 55 for Pro and overstates the budget by 10 percent.
- Leaving out the retrieval cost, which raises Pro's cost limit to 0.0012 / 0.000022 ≈ 54.

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: finance says the reranker at $684,000 to $736,000 a month costs more than generation. Which do you cut, the candidate count or the reranker, and what quality loss do you write down?*
- *Reversal: you shipped Fast at 100. What result from the Pro-at-43 eval, and of what size, would make you switch, given the switch costs six points of first-stage recall?*
- *Operating: the vendor announces that Rerank 5 Fast's price doubles in sixty days. Self-hosting a 150M cross-encoder takes a quarter. What do you run in between, and who signs off on the quality change?*

## Common mistakes

- Taking a vendor's or a blog's latency figure instead of a load test on your traffic and network path.
- Measuring with a closed-loop generator that slows down with the service, which hides the queue.
- Sizing from the latency budget alone and finding the bill afterwards, or the reverse.
- Giving the reranker no timeout and no fallback, so a slow vendor day turns into an SLO breach.
