## Question

<Callout kind="interview" label="Design round, 45 minutes">
"Design question answering over the company's documents: wikis, PDFs, contracts, incident logs, crawled vendor pages. Some documents are longer than the context window, and some users paste a question that is longer than the window too. Answers must cite what they used."
</Callout>

What you learn by asking (scenario):

- 1.2 million documents, about 9 billion tokens. The median document is about 3,000 tokens; about 2 percent are over 100,000 (contracts with schedules, log archives).
- Search today is a keyword index with document-level ACLs, owned by the search team. You may add fields and a vector index beside it; you may not rewrite it this half. ACL changes reach the keyword index within 10 minutes.
- About 3 percent of questions arrive with pasted text. The 95th percentile paste is about 300,000 tokens, mostly logs.
- Model spend must average $0.03 per question. The models on offer are Claude Sonnet 5.5 ($2 in, $10 out per million tokens) and Claude Haiku 5.5 ($0.10 in, $0.50 out per million for prompts up to 100,000 tokens; $0.50 and $2.50 above). Both have a 1M-token window. Prices checked 2026-10-28.
- Five engineers, fourteen weeks to general availability.

Cover the architecture, how long documents and long queries are handled, how you would know chunking is causing misses, questions that need many documents, permissions in retrieval and in every cache, and what changes if the task is to summarize one 200-page document. State what you give up, and write the rollout plan with its rollback triggers.

## Answer

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Parse documents with their structure, index passages of a few hundred tokens with a pointer to their parent section, and store a section summary and a document summary for anything long. Retrieve with the existing keyword index plus a new vector index, both filtered by the same ACLs, fuse and rerank, then expand winning passages to their sections inside an evidence budget the cost target sets at about 10,000 tokens. Pasted text is reduced before it is searched: keep the user's question verbatim, collapse repeated log lines, extract in windows under Haiku's 100,000-token price step, and search with the extracted signatures. Every cache is keyed or checked so that nothing derived from a document reaches a user who cannot open it. What I give up: answers that read whole long documents at question time, and cache hits across permission boundaries.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Ingestion.</strong> Parse with structure: headings, pages, tables, log line boundaries. Each document becomes passages of about 500 tokens, each carrying its section, page span, document version and ACL ids. Sections are the parents. Documents over a threshold (I would pick 20,000 tokens) also get a summary per section and one per document, written by Haiku 5.5 and stored as their own retrievable nodes, each with the source's ACL and version. A full summarization pass over the whole corpus would be 9 billion × $0.10 / 1M = $900 of input; over only the long documents it is a fraction of that, and it is redone per changed version, not per question.</p>
<p><strong>Retrieval.</strong> The keyword index stays the lexical half; it already handles part numbers and error codes. A vector index beside it holds the same passage ids, ACL ids and versions, fed from the same change stream so its ACLs lag by no more than the keyword index's 10 minutes. Both are queried with the user's groups as a filter, results fused by rank, a reranker picks the top passages, and winners expand to their parent section while the evidence budget lasts.</p>
<p><strong>The evidence budget.</strong> The quantity to choose. An answer call on Sonnet 5.5 with 24,000 tokens of evidence costs 24,000 × $2 / 1M = $0.048 before output, over the $0.03 average on its own. With 10,000 tokens and 500 tokens of output it is $0.020 + $0.005 = $0.025, leaving room for reranking and query handling. Anything from 8,000 to 12,000 is defensible; 30,000 is not, unless the answer model changes. This is the conflict: recall against cost, settled by a number you can defend in the cost review.</p>
<p><strong>Long queries.</strong> A 300,000-token paste is not a query; it is data with a question attached. Keep the user's own sentences verbatim and put them last in the final prompt. Reduce the paste deterministically first: strip timestamps and request ids, collapse lines that differ only in those, keep warning and error lines with a few lines of context. If what is left is still long, extract in windows of at most 90,000 tokens with Haiku 5.5, so each call stays under the 100,000-token step: 300,000 tokens cost 300,000 × $0.10 / 1M = $0.03 in four windows, against 300,000 × $0.50 / 1M = $0.15 in one call. The extraction output is a structured list: error signatures, components, versions, the first failing timestamp. Each item becomes a sub-query; results are fused. At 3 percent of traffic, these questions can cost several times the average and still fit it.</p>
<p><strong>Knowing whether chunking causes misses.</strong> Label a set of questions with gold evidence spans, from accepted citations and a labelled sample. Measure span recall at the retrieval stage, separately from answer accuracy, and sort every miss into three bins: the span crosses a passage boundary (chunking), the span sits inside one passage that ranked below the cut (ranking), or the span is in no passage at all (parsing: scanned pages, tables). Only the first bin is fixed by chunk size or overlap; rerun it with parent expansion off and on to see how much expansion recovers.</p>
<p><strong>Questions that need many documents.</strong> "Which vendor contracts let them terminate on 30 days' notice?" is not a similarity search; no passage resembles the set. Route such questions by detecting a quantifier over a document type, filter by metadata (document type, vendor, date) rather than ranking, and map over the matching documents' section summaries with Haiku, then reduce on Sonnet. Li et al. report that summarization-based retrieval performs comparably to long context while chunk-based retrieval lags; this is where that matters. Cap the map at a document count and say in the answer when it was hit.</p>
<p><strong>Permissions, in retrieval.</strong> Filter both indexes by ACL. Then check the final evidence set against the ACL service before the answer call, because the index's copy of an ACL can be up to 10 minutes stale and the check makes staleness cost recall instead of a leak.</p>
<p><strong>Permissions, in caches.</strong> Three caches, three answers.</p>
<ul>
<li><em>The provider's prompt cache.</em> A hit needs an identical prefix, so a request only hits on content it already sent; it cannot hand one user another user's document. Caches are isolated by organization and, on the Claude API, by workspace. The residual risk is timing: Gu et al. detected cache sharing across users at seven providers, which lets an attacker test whether a guessed prefix was recently sent. Cache only shared instructions and tools across users; per-user evidence goes after the last breakpoint.</li>
<li><em>An answer cache.</em> An answer is derived from documents, so it carries the union of their ACLs. Store the evidence document ids and versions with each entry and serve it only to a user who can read all of them, checked at read time; invalidate on any version change.</li>
<li><em>The summary store.</em> A section or document summary inherits its source's ACL and version. A cross-document summary inherits the intersection of readers, which usually means it is computed per request and not cached.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Summarizing one 200-page document.</strong> This is no longer search. Two defensible answers. Read it whole: 200 pages at about 600 tokens a page is 120,000 tokens, $0.24 of input and about $0.02 of output on Sonnet 5.5, $0.26, with cross-references between sections intact, and the accuracy risk that Chroma measured as length grows. Or map-reduce: ten section summaries on Haiku 5.5 at 120,000 × $0.10 / 1M = $0.012 plus about $0.004 of output, then a Sonnet reduce over 8,000 tokens, about $0.05 in total, at the cost of anything that only appears when two sections are read together. The stored per-section summaries from ingestion make the second nearly free when the document is already indexed. I would offer map-reduce by default and the whole read for contracts, where the cross-references are the point.</p>
<p><strong>What you give up.</strong> Whole-document reading at question time for ordinary questions; answer-cache hits across permission groups; summaries that are fresh within minutes of an edit (they regenerate per version, asynchronously); and a dense-only index, because the keyword index stays.</p>
<p><strong>Rollout plan.</strong> Weeks 1 to 6: ingestion with structure, the vector index beside the keyword index, the labelled evidence set. Weeks 7 to 10: long-query reduction and the many-document route. Weeks 11 to 14: internal users at 5 percent, then 50, then all. Rollback triggers, each disabling the feature that caused it: any answer citing a document the asker cannot open, found by the audit that replays a daily sample of answers as their askers; span recall on the labelled set below the keyword-only baseline; mean model spend over $0.03 per question for three consecutive days; the ACL service's error rate making the final check fail closed on more than a set share of questions, chosen with support before launch.</p>
<p><strong>Where the design fails.</strong> The log reduction is lossy by design. Collapsing lines that differ only in ids also collapses the one line whose id is the answer ("which request failed first?"), and windowed extraction can miss a pattern that only shows across two windows. The answer must say the paste was reduced and how, and the user needs a way to ask about the raw text, which costs the $0.15 path.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Why not give the answer model the top five whole documents, since the window is 1M tokens?</em> At the median 3,000 tokens that is 15,000 tokens and over the evidence budget already; with one long contract among them it is $0.20 or more for one answer, and the measured accuracy at that length is the open question.</li>
<li><em>Why keep the keyword index at all?</em> It already enforces ACLs, it matches exact identifiers, and replacing it is not allowed this half; the vector index adds what it misses.</li>
</ul>

## Follow-ups

- *Pushback: the final ACL check adds a network call to every answer and the product lead wants it removed because the indexes are already filtered. What do you cut instead, and what risk do you accept in writing if you lose the argument?*
- *Reversal: what would you need to see in production to drop the stored section summaries and read long documents whole at question time?* Answer-model input prices falling far enough that the evidence budget could hold whole long documents within $0.03, and an eval showing accuracy holding at that length on our own questions.
- *Two senior engineers disagree: one wants the many-document route through map-reduce over summaries, the other through an agentic loop that searches repeatedly. How do you decide without deciding by rank?* Label twenty set-style questions with their full answer sets and compare recall of the set, cost and latency for both.
- *Legal asks whether a revoked user can still get an answer from a document in the 10 minutes after revocation. What is the true answer under your design, and what would make it "no"?*

## Common mistakes

- Embedding a 300,000-token paste as one query, or truncating it to the window and losing the user's question at the end.
- Measuring only answer accuracy and blaming chunk size for misses that are ranking or parsing failures.
- Caching answers by query text alone, so a user receives an answer built from documents they cannot open.
- Treating the provider's prompt cache as the permission risk and missing the answer cache and the summary store.
- Choosing an evidence budget from the window size instead of from the cost per question.
