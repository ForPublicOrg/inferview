## The problem the paper answers

Fix the work per token, then ask how many experts to spread it over. That is the question Kimi K2's architecture section answers with an experiment, and it is the one most MoE configurations answer by copying a predecessor. The report defines sparsity as total experts divided by activated experts and fits a sparsity scaling law with the number of activated parameters held constant, so every point on the curve costs the same FLOPs per token and differs only in how many parameters sit idle for each token.

Three published designs make the spread concrete. DeepSeek-V3 activates 8 of 256 routed experts, sparsity 32, with 1 shared expert, 128 attention heads, 3 dense layers, 671B total and 37B activated parameters. Kimi K2 keeps DeepSeek-V3's 61 layers, hidden size 7168 and expert size 2048, and changes four things: 384 experts with 8 active (sparsity 48), 64 attention heads, 1 dense layer, and no expert grouping, for 1.04T total and 32.6B activated. Qwen3-235B-A22B activates 8 of 128 (sparsity 16), with no shared expert, 64 query heads and 4 KV heads.

## The mechanism in engineering terms

Sparsity buys quality per FLOP with memory. Each additional expert adds parameters that every token could use and that only a few do, so loss at a fixed FLOP budget falls, and the bill moves to wherever parameters live: optimizer state in training, HBM and interconnect at serving.

The key number in the paper is the one it reports at a fixed validation loss of 1.5: sparsity 48 reaches it with 1.69 times fewer FLOPs than sparsity 8, 1.39 times fewer than sparsity 16 and 1.15 times fewer than sparsity 32. Read as ratios between neighbours, going from 8 to 16 is worth 1.69 / 1.39 = 1.22 times, from 16 to 32 is worth 1.39 / 1.15 = 1.21 times, and from 32 to 48 is worth 1.15 times. Each step returns a little less, and each step costs more parameters.

The report states the trade in one sentence: more sparsity performs better but brings more infrastructure complexity, and K2 chose 48 to balance performance against cost. It does not quantify that complexity. You can: 1.5 times the expert memory per layer means either 1.5 times the expert-parallel ranks for the same memory per GPU, or 1.5 times the experts on each GPU. K2 kept expert parallelism at 16, which its report calls the smallest feasible, and reports about 30 GB of state per GPU.

## The second knob: attention heads

K2 halves DeepSeek-V3's attention heads from 128 to 64. The report measured both settings with 384 experts: 128 heads improved validation loss by about 0.5% to 1.2% across compute budgets, and at a 128k sequence length increased inference FLOPs by 83%. It calls that overhead a major limitation for agentic applications, where long contexts are the normal case, and keeps 64.

This decision is the mirror of the sparsity one. Sparsity is a knob whose cost lands in memory and whose benefit arrives at every context length. Attention heads are a knob whose cost scales with context length, because the attention score computation grows with the number of heads times the square of the sequence, while the expert FFNs do not depend on context at all. A model that will mostly serve short prompts could take the 0.5% to 1.2%; a model built for long agent sessions pays the 83% on every long request.

## Training cost against serving cost

With the standard 6 x activated parameters x tokens approximation (it ignores attention FLOPs, which matter more at long context), K2's pretraining is about 6 x 32.6e9 x 15.5e12 = 3.03e24 FLOPs and DeepSeek-V3's is about 6 x 37e9 x 14.8e12 = 3.29e24. Similar budgets, so the question is what each design costs to serve.

Weights are the first serving cost. At one byte per parameter, K2's weights are about 1.04 TB against 671 GB for DeepSeek-V3, before any KV cache. The second is what a decode step reads. Activated parameters set the FLOPs per token, but a batch of tokens touches many experts at once, and each touched expert's weights are read from HBM.

<p>The chance a given expert is missed by one token is 376/384; by all 64 tokens it is (376/384)^64 = 0.26. So 384 x (1 - 0.26) = <b>about 284 experts</b>, 74% of the layer. For DeepSeek-V3's 256 experts the same batch touches about 222, 87% of the layer. At serving batch sizes, decode reads most of the total parameters per step, not the activated ones, so the memory-bandwidth cost of a decode step tracks total size much more closely than the activated count suggests.</p>

Real routing is not uniform, which helps a little (fewer distinct experts) and hurts more (hot experts on one GPU set the step time). The conclusion holds: higher sparsity is cheaper to train to a given loss and more expensive to hold and stream at serving time.

## What changed in practice

The paper moved the default from "copy the predecessor's expert count" to "fit sparsity at fixed activated parameters and stop where infrastructure cost overtakes the FLOP saving". It also made attention width a serving decision rather than a quality decision: the head count was chosen by inference FLOPs at the target context length.

Two other choices travel with the configuration and are easy to miss. The run used the MuonClip optimizer, with QK-Clip at a threshold of 100, after a mid-scale vanilla Muon run with 9B activated and 53B total parameters saw maximum attention logits above 1000; the report shows 15.5T tokens with no loss spike. And K2 dropped DeepSeek-V3's expert grouping, which DeepSeek-V3 uses alongside its limit of 4 nodes per token; the report lists the difference without arguing it.

## What to ask before copying the configuration

Which loss did the law use, and at what scale? The FLOP ratios are reported at a validation loss of 1.5 with activated parameters fixed; the paper's figure is the evidence, not a guarantee at your loss target or tokenizer.

What is your expert-parallel domain? 384 experts at EP16 assumes enough memory per GPU for 24 experts per layer plus their optimizer state, and an interconnect that tolerates the exchange.

What do your sessions look like? The 64-head choice rests on 128k contexts and agentic use. A short-context product changes the inference side of that trade.

Which optimizer and balancing scheme? The run used MuonClip and reports no loss spikes; the parts of the report read for this lesson do not describe K2's load-balancing mechanism, so a copy has to choose its own.

## When this is wrong

The uniform-routing estimate of touched experts is an upper bound on distinct experts and a lower bound on imbalance. Measure real routing on your traffic before sizing serving replicas from it.

The 6ND approximation understates training cost at long context, where attention FLOPs grow with sequence length and do not shrink with sparsity.

And the sparsity law is one lab's fit on one recipe. Its direction (more experts at fixed activated parameters lowers loss) agrees with DeepSeekMoE's fine-grained segmentation result; its magnitude at your scale is a measurement you have not made yet.
