## The decision

A team has a pretrained base and a large corpus the base has seen little of: a programming language, a regulated domain's documents, another natural language. Re-training from scratch on the union of old and new data is the clean baseline and the expensive one. Continuing to pretrain the existing checkpoint on the new data is cheaper and has a known failure: the model forgets what the old distribution taught it, or, if the learning rate is kept low to prevent that, it barely adapts. The design question is how to set the schedule and the data mix so the cheap path lands where the expensive one would, and how to know during the run that it is.

## What re-warming does, measured

Most pretrained checkpoints end at the bottom of a decayed learning-rate schedule. Ibrahim et al. trained GPT-NeoX models at 405M parameters with a cosine schedule (max 3e-4, warmup over 1% of iterations, minimum 0.1 x max) and continued them on new data under three treatments.

Training on at the final, small learning rate adapted poorly to the strong shift (English to German). Re-warming the rate to a new maximum and decaying it again was necessary for adaptation, and it caused forgetting. The cleanest result in the paper is the control: re-warming on the same data, Pile to Pile, still raised loss on the Pile whenever the rate climbed from its minimum. Part of what looks like forgetting from new data is the schedule itself. A higher re-warmed maximum adapted more and forgot more; the warmup length (0% to 2% of iterations) did not change forgetting noticeably but prevented loss spikes at the start.

Replay is the third control: a fixed share of every batch drawn from the original distribution. Without replay, loss on the Pile rose by 0.27 nats under the weak shift and 1.39 nats under the strong one. A 25% replay share removed 1.23 nats of the strong-shift increase. The paper settled on 5% replay for the weak shift and 25% for the strong one, as the shares that reached similar final loss while leaving the most budget for new tokens.

Combined, the three matched re-training on the union of both datasets. Average loss across the two distributions:

| Setup | Continued, with replay | Re-trained on the union |
|---|---|---|
| 405M, weak shift, 5% replay | 2.37 | 2.35 |
| 405M, strong shift, 25% replay | 1.75 | 1.75 |
| 10B, weak shift, 5% replay | 1.89 | 1.87 |

Benchmarks followed loss on average, with one exception the paper reports: the 5% replay model trails the union model on MMLU. The abstract states the saving as "a fraction of the compute". For the weak shift the arithmetic is direct: the continued run trains on the 300B new tokens on top of a checkpoint that already exists, while a union run trains on both 300B-token datasets from scratch, so the continued run costs half of the union run if the existing checkpoint is treated as free.

## LoRA forgets less and learns less

LoRA restricts the update to low rank, so it looks like a forgetting control in its own right. Biderman et al. tested that on Llama-2-7B with up to 20B tokens of continued pretraining on Python code and on mathematical web text:

- Code: after 20B tokens, full fine-tuning reached 0.263 on HumanEval and LoRA at rank 256 reached 0.224, about where full fine-tuning was after 4B tokens (0.218). On the forgetting average of HellaSwag, WinoGrande and ARC-Challenge, full fine-tuning fell to 0.545 and LoRA held 0.617.
- Maths: full fine-tuning reached 0.293 on GSM8K, LoRA peaked at 0.203. Forgetting was about equal: 0.616 for LoRA, 0.613 for full fine-tuning.

So on code LoRA needed 20B tokens to reach where full fine-tuning was at 4B, in exchange for less forgetting, and on maths it gave up the learning without the protection. Their spectral analysis found that full fine-tuning's weight changes have rank 10 to 100 times higher than typical LoRA configurations, consistent with the capacity argument from the LoRA lesson: billions of tokens of a new domain carry more information than a low-rank update holds. Their recommendation is LoRA for instruction fine-tuning, not continued pretraining. Replay addresses forgetting without capping what the model can learn.

## Designing the run

A worked case (scenario): a 7B base, a 15B-token domain corpus of internal engineering documents and code, and access to a general web corpus as a replay source because the base's original pretraining data is not available.

**Replay share.** The shift is somewhere between the paper's weak and strong cases: same natural language, very different content. Choose 25% on first principles and test 5% and 25% in short pilots, which is the paper's own advice (test replay shares early). At 25% and 15B domain tokens, the run is 20B tokens in total.

**Replay source.** The paper replayed the actual previous dataset. A proxy corpus replays something near the original distribution, and the forgetting checks are what tell whether it is near enough.

**Schedule.** Re-warm over about 1% of steps to a maximum at or below the original pre-training maximum, then decay. Lower maxima forget less and adapt less; the pilots pick the point. Reset or re-initialise optimizer state at the transition, as the paper did.

**Shape of the decay.** A cosine schedule fixes its decay to a token budget chosen before the run. If the forgetting checks force a stop at 12B tokens, the model stops mid-decay at a high learning rate, which is worse than a model decayed to completion on 12B. The paper proposes schedules that hold a constant rate and are not tied to a token budget; the property that matters for this run is a decay phase that can be started when the stop rule fires.

## Forgetting checks and the stop rule

Three measurements, evaluated at fixed token intervals:

1. **Held-out loss on the replay distribution.** The paper's primary signal; it moves first and is cheap. It measures forgetting of the replay source, which is the original distribution only if replay is the original data.
2. **Held-out loss on the domain.** Adaptation. The curve's slope per billion tokens is what the stop rule reads.
3. **A small fixed benchmark set covering what the base did well.** Loss averages hide specific losses; the paper's own MMLU gap is one. Include whatever downstream behaviour the product depends on.

A stop rule written before the run: stop and start the decay when the domain loss improvement per billion tokens falls below a threshold chosen from the pilots, or when general held-out loss rises more than an agreed budget over its value after re-warming. A breach of the general-loss budget early in the run means the re-warmed maximum is too high or the replay share too low, and the fix is a restart with the change, not continued training.

The conflict inside the rule: a tight forgetting budget stops the run before the domain gain arrives, and a loose one ships a model that lost capabilities nobody measured. The written artefact is the budget, signed by whoever owns the downstream behaviour the benchmarks stand in for.

## When this is wrong

Ibrahim et al. measured at 405M and 10B parameters, on English-to-English and English-to-German shifts, with loss and a benchmark average as the yardsticks; a domain whose value shows in narrow downstream tasks may need more replay or a lower maximum than loss suggests, and the MMLU gap at 5% replay is a reminder that averages hide specific losses. Their replay was the true previous dataset; with a proxy, the matching result is untested. The checkpoints they continued were pretrained bases, so continuing an instruction-tuned or RL-trained checkpoint is outside the evidence, and that post-training usually has to be redone after the run. Biderman et al. used Llama-2-7B and ranks up to 256; higher ranks or different targets narrow the gap, and the question becomes whether the memory saving still matters at that rank. The GPU-hour figures above rest on a scenario throughput; the measured number from the pilots replaces it.
