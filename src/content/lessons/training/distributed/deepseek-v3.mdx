## What it is and who runs it

DeepSeek-V3 is DeepSeek-AI's mixture-of-experts language model: 671B parameters in total, 37B activated per token, pre-trained on 14.8T tokens. The technical report describes the model and, unusually, the training system in enough detail to reconstruct its parallel layout, its pipeline schedule, its communication kernels and its FP8 recipe. This teardown covers the training system only, from that report. Inference deployment is covered in the expert-parallelism lesson of the mixture-of-experts module.

The headline layout is the reason to study it. On 2,048 H800 GPUs, 8 per node on NVLink and NVSwitch with InfiniBand between nodes, it trained with 16-way pipeline parallelism, 64-way expert parallelism spanning 8 nodes, and ZeRO-1 data parallelism, and no tensor parallelism. Every dense-model playbook puts TP 8 inside the node. DeepSeek-V3 spent its engineering on making TP unnecessary.

**MoE with shared and routed experts.** 61 layers with hidden size 7168; the first three use dense feed-forward layers, the rest are MoE layers with 1 shared expert, which every token passes through, and 256 routed experts of which each token uses 8. The shared expert carries common computation without routing, so the routed experts can specialise. Attention is Multi-head Latent Attention with 128 heads.

**Auxiliary-loss-free balancing.** Instead of a balance loss that fights the language-modelling loss, each expert has a bias added to its routing score for top-k selection, nudged up when the expert is under-loaded and down when over-loaded. The report gives the bias update speed as 0.001, set to 0.0 for the final 500B tokens. Balance matters to the system as much as to the model: an all-to-all finishes when its most loaded receiver finishes.

**Multi-token prediction.** One extra module, depth 1, predicts the token after next from the main model's representation, with its own Transformer block and a projection, and shares the embedding and output head with the main model. Its loss is weighted 0.3 for the first 10T tokens and 0.1 for the remaining 4.8T. At inference it can be dropped or used for speculative decoding. For the system, the MTP module is extra compute and memory on the last pipeline stage, which already holds the output head.

**FP8 training with fine-grained scaling.** Most GEMMs run in FP8. Scaling factors are per 1x128 tile for activations and per 128x128 block for weights, so one outlier only affects its own tile. Because FP8 tensor-core accumulation loses precision over long reductions, partial sums are promoted to FP32 on CUDA cores every 128 elements. The report puts the relative loss error against BF16 below 0.25%. MoE dispatch is sent in FP8; the combine stays in BF16.

**DualPipe.** The pipeline schedule feeds micro-batches from both ends of the 16 stages, so each GPU holds the parameters of two stages, one per direction. Each chunk is split into attention, all-to-all dispatch, MLP and all-to-all combine, and the backward chunks are further split into input-gradient and weight-gradient parts. A forward chunk of one micro-batch is paired with a backward chunk of another, so each one's communication runs during the other's computation. The report's Table 2 gives the bubble as (PP/2 - 1)(F&B + B - 3W) against (PP - 1)(F + B) for 1F1B, at twice the parameters and PP + 1 micro-batches of activations.

**PP 16, EP 64, ZeRO-1, no TP.** With EP 64, each GPU holds 256 / 64 = 4 routed experts per MoE layer, and with PP 16 over 61 layers, about 4 layers per stage. That leaves little parameter memory per GPU, which the report gives as the reason DualPipe's two parameter copies do not significantly raise memory. The Playbook notes that PP combines easily with ZeRO-1, while PP with ZeRO-3 is rarely used.

The step shows where the hard constraint is. The report states that cross-node expert parallelism gives a computation-to-communication ratio of about 1:1, so without overlap the step would spend about as long waiting as computing. The schedule's job is to make one micro-batch's exchange coincide with another micro-batch's math, and its constraints follow: an even number of stages and micro-batches, and a bubble and activation footprint that do not grow with the micro-batch count.

## Context strategy

Pre-training ran at 4K tokens with a batch ramped from 3,072 to 15,360 sequences over the first 469B tokens, then held. The learning rate warmed up over 2K steps to 2.2e-4, stayed there to 10T tokens, then followed a cosine decay to 2.2e-5 over 4.3T tokens, with two further constant phases in the final 500B tokens. Long context came afterwards in two extension phases, 4K to 32K and then 32K to 128K, which the cost table counts at 119K GPU hours. Keeping pre-training at 4K is what lets the layout avoid context parallelism entirely.

## Memory and communication budget

The report lists the memory measures that, together with the large EP degree, let it skip tensor parallelism: recomputing RMSNorm and the MLA up-projections in the backward pass instead of storing their outputs, keeping the exponential moving average of the parameters in CPU memory, and sharing the embedding and output head between the MTP module and the main model on the same pipeline stage.

The communication design follows the hardware. NVLink is about 160 GB/s per GPU and InfiniBand 50 GB/s, a ratio of 3.2. Node-limited routing sends each token to at most 4 nodes. A token's hidden state crosses InfiniBand once per target node, to the GPU with the same local index, and is forwarded on NVLink to the GPUs holding its experts. Since a token selects 3.2 experts per node on average, the report notes the same InfiniBand traffic could serve up to 13 experts. The communication kernels occupy 20 of the H800's 132 SMs, in 10 channels with warp specialisation, compute permanently given up to move bytes.

## Cost and latency

The report's cost table: 2,664K H800 GPU hours for pre-training, 119K for context extension and 5K for post-training, 2.788M in total, which it prices at $5.576M assuming $2 per GPU hour. Pre-training took about 180K GPU hours per trillion tokens; on 2,048 GPUs that is 180,000 / 2,048 = 88 hours, the report's 3.7 days. Over 14.8T tokens, 2,664K GPU hours is 1,302 hours of wall-clock time on the full cluster, about 54 days.

One derived figure is useful for comparison. With the 6 x active parameters x tokens approximation, pre-training is 6 x 37e9 x 14.8e12 = 3.29e24 FLOPs. Divided by 2,664K GPU hours, that is 3.43e14 FLOP per second per GPU, 343 TFLOP/s of model FLOPs, counting attention as zero and mixing FP8 and BF16 work. It is a lower bound on the hardware throughput achieved, not an MFU, since it ignores attention FLOPs and the peak it should be divided by depends on the precision mix.

## Failure modes

The report states that training had no irrecoverable loss spikes and needed no rollbacks. It does not publish hardware failure counts, restart counts or checkpoint intervals.

The design has failure modes its own description implies. A routing collapse onto a few experts would break the 1:1 compute-to-communication balance before it showed in the loss, because the most loaded receiver sets the all-to-all time; the bias mechanism is the guard, and the decision to freeze it for the final 500B tokens is a bet that routing has stabilised. FP8 training risks silent accuracy loss from outliers; the fine-grained tiles and periodic FP32 promotion are the guard, and the 0.25% bound is the report's validation against BF16 at the scales it tested. DualPipe's two parameter copies are cheap only while EP keeps per-GPU parameters small; at a smaller EP degree the same schedule would cost real memory.

## What is not public

The full pre-training data mixture is not public. The report describes the corpus at a high level but not the proportions that would let someone rebuild it.

The cost of prior research and ablation experiments is not public. The report says so directly: the $5.576M covers only the official DeepSeek-V3 training run, priced at an assumed $2 per H800 GPU hour, not the experiments that chose the architecture and recipe, and not the cluster's purchase price. Any comparison of "training cost" with another lab's total spend compares different quantities.
