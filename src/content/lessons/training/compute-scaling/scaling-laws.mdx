## What a scaling law is a fit of

A scaling law is a regression of final pre-training loss on parameters N, tokens D or compute C, fitted on a family of runs that share an architecture, a tokenizer, a data mixture and an optimiser recipe. Inside the range it was fitted on, it interpolates well. Outside, it is an extrapolation of a power law, and a small error in an exponent becomes a large error after three or four orders of magnitude. Everything practical about scaling laws follows from those two sentences: what they predict (loss, for this recipe, near the fitted range), what they do not (benchmarks, other data mixes, other architectures), and why the rounding of a coefficient can move a 400B-parameter decision.

Given C ≈ 6ND from the previous lesson, a compute budget fixes the product ND. The scaling-law question is how to split it: the compute-optimal model size N_opt(C) and token count D_opt(C) that minimise loss for that C. Both are fitted as power laws, N_opt ∝ C^a and D_opt ∝ C^b, with a + b = 1 because their product is fixed.

## Kaplan: spend compute on parameters

Kaplan et al. (January 2020) found loss following power laws in N, D and C, with some trends spanning more than seven orders of magnitude, and found that width and depth matter little within a wide range. Their compute-efficient frontier favoured parameters: train very large models on modest data and stop significantly before convergence. The Chinchilla paper's summary of Kaplan's allocation is a = 0.73 and b = 0.27.

## Chinchilla: split it evenly

Hoffmann et al. (March 2022) trained over 400 models, from 70M to over 16B parameters on 5 to 500B tokens, and estimated the split three ways: the minimum over training curves, IsoFLOP profiles (fix C, vary N, find the loss minimum), and a parametric fit of loss as a function of N and D. The three gave a = 0.50, 0.49 and 0.46. Parameters and tokens should grow in about equal proportion.

The paper tested the claim directly. Chinchilla, 70B parameters on 1.4T tokens, used the same compute as Gopher, 280B on 300B, and outperformed it. 1.4T / 70B is where "20 tokens per parameter" comes from. The paper's Table 3 projects compute-optimal token counts from its first approach, and those stay close to the same ratio across sizes: 1B parameters on 20.2B tokens, 1T parameters on 21.2T. Check them with 6ND: 6 × 1e9 × 20.2e9 = 1.21e20, which is the FLOPs column.

So 20 is a ratio from one trained model and one of three fitting methods. The third method, the parametric fit, implied considerably more tokens per parameter, and a 2024 replication found that fit was wrong and that a corrected fit gives about 20. The next lesson walks through that. For sizing a run, the consequence is that "20" carries the error bars of a fit on runs up to 16B, extrapolated, with one method's coefficients not reproducible.

## Llama 3: fit your own, then round toward what you can run

Meta did not borrow the ratio. Llama 3's team ran IsoFLOP experiments from about 6e18 to 1e22 FLOPs on models from 40M to 16B parameters, found the loss minimum at each budget, and fitted the optimal number of training tokens as N*(C) = A C^α with (α, A) = (0.53, 0.29). Extrapolated to the flagship budget of 3.8e25 FLOPs, the fit suggested 402B parameters on 16.55T tokens. They trained 405B on 15.6T.

Three details matter more than the result. First, the extrapolation spans more than three orders of magnitude (1e22 to 3.8e25). Second, the paper observes that IsoFLOP curves get flatter around the minimum as compute grows, so being somewhat off the optimum costs little loss; they used that to justify 405B. Third, the printed coefficients are not enough to reproduce the printed answer: 0.29 × (3.8e25)^0.53 = 1.05e13 tokens, not 16.55e12. Whatever the unprinted precision or units, the extrapolation cannot be checked from the paper, and at this distance a change of 0.01 in the exponent moves the answer by a factor of 10^(0.01 × 25.6), about 1.8. A run plan rebuilt from an exponent rounded to two places inherits that error.

Llama 3 also predicted benchmark accuracy, not only loss, with a two-step method (compute to negative log-likelihood on the benchmark, then likelihood to accuracy), and reports that it only slightly underestimated the flagship's ARC Challenge score. That is a separate model stacked on the scaling law, fitted on Llama 2 checkpoints as well, and the paper's result is for that benchmark.

## Training past compute-optimal

Compute-optimal answers one question: the lowest loss for a fixed training budget. A model that will serve traffic pays 2N FLOPs for every generated or prefilled token for as long as it is deployed, and that bill does not appear in Chinchilla's objective. Sardana et al. added it: minimise training plus inference FLOPs for a target quality and an expected inference volume. With demand around a billion requests, the optimum moves to smaller models trained on more tokens.

Their worked case: a 30B model of Chinchilla quality with 1.5B inference requests is cheaper overall as 16B on 3.35T tokens, a 17% lower total cost in their accounting. That is 209 tokens per parameter.

The paper trained 47 models from 150M to 6B parameters at 10 to 10,000 tokens per parameter (10,000 only at 150M; up to 1,000 for the larger sizes) and found quality kept improving at the extreme ratios. It also found that laws fitted on typical ratios overestimate the gain from extra tokens there. Llama 3 405B sits at 15.6T / 405B = 39 tokens per parameter, close to the optimum its own fit gave for its budget; a model meant for high-volume serving would sit far to the right of that.

## What a scaling law does and does not predict

It predicts final loss for the recipe it was fitted on, near the range it was fitted on. It does not predict loss for a different data mixture, tokenizer, architecture or learning-rate schedule; each of those changes the coefficients, which is why labs refit. It does not predict benchmark accuracy without a second model, and it says nothing about capabilities that the loss does not track. It assumes data does not repeat; once a corpus runs out and tokens are repeated, the D in the fit no longer means unique tokens. And it gives a point estimate whose uncertainty is set by the number and spread of the small runs, which published papers rarely let you recompute.

## When this is wrong

A fitted exponent is a property of a recipe, and recipes change between the sweep and the run: a new data mixture, a tokenizer change, a different learning-rate schedule length. Each invalidates part of the fit. The Kaplan and Chinchilla disagreement is itself an example of a schedule detail moving the conclusion.

Loss is not the objective of most runs. Post-training, long context and tool use come later and are not in the fit. For a mixture of experts, N is ambiguous (total or activated), and a dense law does not transfer; MoE runs need their own fits, which the playbook lesson covers. And when the run is constrained by data rather than compute, the question stops being how to split C between N and D and becomes how much repetition the data tolerates, which these laws do not answer.
