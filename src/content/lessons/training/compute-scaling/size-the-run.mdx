## The decision

Given a cluster for a fixed window, choose the architecture class, the size and the token count of the run. Three options cover almost every case: a dense model near compute-optimal, a smaller dense model trained far past it, or a mixture of experts at a chosen sparsity. The decision is made once, before launch, because the learning-rate schedule, the parallelism layout and the data pipeline are all set from it; changing size mid-run means starting again.

## The inputs that change it

- **Target quality**, stated as a loss or a benchmark the team already measures, not as "frontier". The scaling fit translates it into compute for each option.
- **Expected inference volume** over the model's life, in tokens. This is the input most often missing, and it decides between the first two options.
- **GPU count, type and the window**, which with an MFU and a goodput you have measured fix the training FLOPs: C = GPUs × dense peak × MFU × goodput × seconds.
- **Unique data available**, because an option that needs more tokens than exist is really an option about repetition.
- **Serving hardware**, because total parameters must fit in memory at serving time even when only some are active.
- **The team's experience**, because a first MoE run and a fifth dense run do not have the same risk.

## The options and when each wins

**Compute-optimal dense.** Size from your own IsoFLOP fit (about 20 tokens per parameter is the published starting point). Wins when lifetime inference is small next to training: a research checkpoint, a teacher that will be distilled, a model whose job is to be the best at this budget. Costs the most per served token. Llama 3 405B sits here, and its report notes that IsoFLOP curves flatten near the minimum at large budgets, so a size within a factor of about two of the optimum costs little loss.

**Smaller and longer.** Size below the optimum and spend the compute on tokens. Wins when lifetime inference passes the break-even where training extra tokens costs less than serving extra parameters, which for a model with real traffic happens early. Sardana et al. trained models at up to 10,000 tokens per parameter and found quality kept improving, with the warning that laws fitted on typical ratios overestimate the gain at extreme ratios. Costs: more unique data, a long schedule, and a loss at this compute that is worse than the optimum by an amount only your fit can estimate.

**Mixture of experts.** Fix the activated parameters for serving FLOPs and add experts. Kimi K2 published a sparsity scaling law: at fixed activated parameters, more experts consistently lowered training and validation loss, and to reach the same validation loss of 1.5, sparsity 48 needed 1.69x fewer FLOPs than sparsity 8 in their setting. K2 itself is 1.04T total and 32B activated, 384 experts with 8 active. Wins when training compute binds and serving has memory for every expert. Costs: total parameters in memory at serving, all-to-all communication in training, and stability risk; Llama 3's report says it chose a dense model to maximise training stability.

K2's report also shows that a sizing decision includes serving-side shape: it kept 64 attention heads rather than 128 because doubling heads raised inference FLOPs by 83% at a 128K sequence length for a 0.5% to 1.2% validation-loss gain.

## A worked decision

The case is a scenario. 1,024 H100 SXM GPUs for 75 days. The team has measured 38% MFU on dense runs at this scale and plans on 90% goodput (Llama 3 reports above 90%). The model will serve about 20T tokens a month for 18 months, 3.6e14 tokens. Serving runs on 8-GPU H100 nodes, 640 GB of HBM per node. About 14T unique tokens of acceptable quality exist. The team has never trained an MoE.

Training compute: 1,024 × 989.5e12 × 0.38 × 0.90 × 75 × 86,400 = 2.25e24 FLOPs.

**Compute-optimal dense at 20 tokens per parameter.** C = 6 × N × 20N = 120N², so N = sqrt(2.25e24 / 120) = 137B on 2.7T tokens. Weights in BF16: 274 GB, which fits a node with room for KV cache.

**Smaller and longer.** A 30B model on the same compute trains on 2.25e24 / (6 × 30e9) = 12.5T tokens, 416 tokens per parameter, inside the 14T of unique data and inside the ratios Sardana et al. measured, though only on models up to 6B. Lifetime inference falls to 2 × 30e9 × 3.6e14 = 2.2e25, saving 7.7e25 FLOPs, about 34 times this run's training compute. Weights in BF16: 60 GB.

**MoE.** Keep about 30B activated and add experts. In K2's sweeps, more experts at fixed activated parameters kept lowering loss, so if that transfers to this data and recipe, the MoE reaches lower loss than the 30B dense at the same serving FLOPs. Total parameters set the serving memory: DeepSeek-V3's 671B at one byte per parameter is 671 GB, more than this node holds, so the MoE would have to be smaller or span nodes.

**Decision.** Train the 30B dense on 12.5T. The inference volume makes the compute-optimal model the wrong product, the data exists, and a first MoE run on a 75-day window adds a stability risk the team cannot buy back. What is given up: the lowest loss this compute could reach, and the MoE's better loss per activated parameter. The failure mode is that the 30B misses the quality target; the fit's estimate of its loss carries the error of extrapolating to 416 tokens per parameter, where Sardana et al. warn fits overestimate gains. The memo sets the checkpoint below and names the fallback: the same data and pipeline at 45B to 50B, at fewer tokens, restarted if the checkpoint fails.

## What to measure once it runs

**Loss against the extrapolated curve.** Before launch, write down the loss the fit predicts at 10%, 30% and 60% of tokens, with the spread of the small runs around the fit. A gap larger than that spread at 10% is a reason to stop and check data and schedule; at 30% it is a decision point for the fallback. Comparing against the curve, not against yesterday's loss, is what makes a slow miss visible.

**MFU per configuration.** Measured with 6N over the dense peak, so it can be compared with the plan and with published numbers (Llama 3: 38% to 43% BF16 across its configurations). A drop after a change in scale or sequence length is a planning input, not just a systems ticket: it moves the end date.

**Effective training time.** The share of wall clock producing steps, net of failures, restarts and checkpoint writes. Llama 3 reports above 90% and 466 interruptions in a 54-day snapshot, 419 unexpected. Tokens per day is the product of MFU and goodput, and it is the number that says whether the run finishes inside the window.

**Instability.** Loss spikes and their recovery cost, in tokens. K2 reports training on 15.5T tokens with zero loss spikes, which is a property of its optimiser and recipe, not a baseline for a team on a first run.

## When this is wrong

The playbook assumes the scaling fit is good enough to compare options; with a fit on a different data mix or tokenizer, the comparison is guesswork and the first spend should be a sweep. It treats inference cost as FLOPs, while at low batch sizes serving is limited by memory bandwidth, which favours smaller and sparser models further than the FLOP count shows. It assumes the inference forecast; if traffic comes in at a tenth of the forecast, the compute-optimal model would have been the right call. And it ignores later stages: a model that will be distilled, or heavily post-trained, changes what the pre-training run is for.
