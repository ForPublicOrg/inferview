## The problem the paper takes on

Chinchilla's conclusion rests on three estimates of how to split a compute budget between parameters and tokens. The first two (the minimum over training curves, and IsoFLOP profiles) gave an exponent a for N_opt ∝ C^a of 0.50 and 0.49. The third fitted a closed-form loss surface to every final loss:

```text
L(N, D) = E + A / N^alpha + B / D^beta
```

E is the irreducible loss, the second term is what a finite model costs, the third what finite data costs. Minimising L subject to 6ND = C gives N_opt ∝ C^a with a = β / (α + β), so the exponents of the surface fix the split. The paper's surface gave a = 0.46, with a reported 80% interval of 0.454 to 0.455. The other two methods' intervals were 0.488 to 0.502 and 0.462 to 0.534.

Two things in that table should have stopped a reader. The third method disagrees with the first, and its interval is a tenth of a percent wide, from a few hundred runs. Besiroglu, Erdil, Barnett and You (April 2024) took the third method apart.

## The mechanism, in engineering terms

The training runs were not released, so the replication starts by reconstructing the data. The authors extracted about 240 points (N, D, final loss) from the paper's plots, 245 with five outliers they drop, against the "over 400" runs the paper reports. Then they refitted the same functional form with the same robust loss (Huber) and a minimiser run to convergence.

The re-fit:

| | E | A | B | α | β | a = β/(α+β) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hoffmann et al. (printed) | 1.69 | 406.4 | 410.7 | 0.34 | 0.28 | 0.45 |
| Besiroglu et al. | 1.8172 | 482.01 | 2085.43 | 0.3478 | 0.3658 | 0.51 |

Why the original fit went wrong is the part that transfers to any team fitting its own laws. The paper used L-BFGS with a Huber loss and δ = 10⁻³. According to the replication, one of the lead authors confirmed that the Huber losses were averaged over data points instead of summed. Averaging changes the scale of the objective, and the replication reports that this caused the optimiser to terminate early, in the main fit and in every bootstrap resample. That explains both the poor fit and the narrow intervals: resampled fits that all stop early stay close to where they started, and so agree with each other.

The replication also checks rounding. Using β = 0.28 instead of the 0.2849 in the paper's TeX source biases the data term by about 13% at D = 10¹¹ tokens. That does not by itself explain the mismatch, but it is the same lesson as Llama 3's printed (α, A) not reproducing its printed extrapolation: two-digit exponents are not enough to rebuild a decision.

## The number that matters

The tokens-per-parameter ratio a fit implies depends on the budget when a is not exactly 0.5, so it is worth computing rather than quoting. The closed-form optimum for C = 6ND:

```python
def optimum(E, A, B, alpha, beta, C):
    G = (alpha * A / (beta * B)) ** (1 / (alpha + beta))
    n = G * (C / 6) ** (beta / (alpha + beta))
    d = C / 6 / n
    return n, d, d / n

gopher = 5.76e23
for name, fit in {
    "printed":     (1.69, 406.4, 410.7, 0.34, 0.28),
    "tex digits":  (1.6934, 406.4, 410.7, 0.3392, 0.2849),
    "replication": (1.8172, 482.01, 2085.43, 0.3478, 0.3658),
}.items():
    n, d, r = optimum(*fit, gopher)
    print(f"{name:12} N={n:.3g} D={d:.3g} tokens/param={r:.0f}")
# printed      N=3.22e+10 D=2.98e+12 tokens/param=93
# tex digits   N=4.03e+10 D=2.38e+12 tokens/param=59
# replication  N=7.22e+10 D=1.33e+12 tokens/param=18
```

At Gopher's budget the original surface puts the optimum at 32B to 40B parameters on 2.4T to 3.0T tokens, depending only on how many digits of the exponents you use. The replication puts it at 72B on 1.3T, 18 tokens per parameter, which is almost exactly the Chinchilla model the paper actually trained (70B on 1.4T). The replication summarises the original fit as implying about 70 tokens per parameter and its own as about 20. The paper's headline model was consistent with its first two methods all along; its third method was the outlier.

The interval finding is the other number. The replication's standard error on a gives an 80% interval about 50 times wider than the published one, and it estimates that reaching the published width would take over 600,000 experiments, where the authors likely ran fewer than 500.

## What changed in practice

The 20 tokens per parameter rule came out of the replication stronger, not weaker, but with honest error bars: the re-fit's standard error on a is 0.02, and the ratio moves to 25.6 if the five outliers stay in. Anyone quoting "20" should know it is the centre of a range that a few data points can shift by a quarter.

The procedure is where the change belongs. Teams fitting their own scaling laws on small runs inherit every failure here: an optimiser that stops early because the objective was scaled down, bootstrap intervals that inherit the same early stop, coefficients rounded before extrapolation, and a single functional form trusted over methods that disagree with it. Llama 3's choice to run its own IsoFLOP sweep and to fit tokens directly against compute is one response; fitting with more than one method and reporting where they disagree is another.

There is a cost to the reconstruction, and the paper is open about it: points read off a plot carry digitisation error, and 240 points are not the runs Hoffmann et al. had. The replication shows the published fit is inconsistent with the published figure. It cannot show what the unpublished data would give.

<p><b>What the number is.</b> 1.4T / 70B. The paper's Approach 1 projections stay near it (1B on 20.2B tokens, 1T on 21.2T). Approach 3, as published, implied far more tokens per parameter with an interval no experiment count could justify; Besiroglu et al. traced that to an optimiser that stopped early because the Huber losses were averaged, and their re-fit gives a = 0.513 and about 20 tokens per parameter.</p>
<p><b>What it does not cover.</b> It is fitted on runs up to 16B on one data mix. Our tokenizer changes what a token is, our data mix changes the coefficients, and a different schedule length changes the curve (the Kaplan and Chinchilla disagreement partly came from that). It also ignores inference: Sardana et al. show that with heavy serving, smaller models trained much longer win on total cost.</p>
<p><b>What I would do with two months on the line.</b> Spend a small fraction of the budget on IsoFLOP sweeps at our recipe, fit with two methods and bootstrap with a minimiser checked for convergence, and choose the size from that and the serving plan. The failure mode is that the sweep itself is small; if the methods disagree, I would choose a size where the IsoFLOP curve is flat across both answers rather than the point estimate of either.</p>
