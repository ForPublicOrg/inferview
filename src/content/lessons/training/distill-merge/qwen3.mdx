## What it is and who runs it

Qwen3 is the open-weight model family Alibaba's Qwen team released in 2025 under Apache 2.0, with a technical report dated 14 May 2025. It has six dense models (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B and 32B) and two mixture-of-experts models: Qwen3-30B-A3B and the flagship Qwen3-235B-A22B, both with 128 experts and 8 active per token. Every model can answer in a thinking mode, with a long reasoning block, or a non-thinking mode, chosen per turn by a flag in the prompt.

The report matters for this module because it publishes the pipeline split most teams now use: expensive multi-stage post-training for the largest models, and distillation from those models for everything smaller. It also publishes the one cost comparison of distillation against RL from a common starting point that the rest of this module leans on. All results below are the Qwen team's own measurements.

**Three pretraining stages.** Pretraining covers 36T tokens in 119 languages and dialects, up from 29 languages in Qwen2.5. Stage 1 is over 30T tokens of general data at a sequence length of 4,096. Stage 2 adds about 5T higher-quality tokens at the same length, with a larger share of STEM, code, reasoning and synthetic data and a faster learning-rate decay: a reasoning-weighted anneal before any post-training. Stage 3 extends context with hundreds of billions of tokens at 32,768, three quarters of them from documents between 16,384 and 32,768 tokens long, and raises the RoPE base frequency from 10,000 to 1,000,000; YaRN and Dual Chunk Attention give a further four-fold length at inference.

**Long-CoT cold start.** A small, heavily filtered SFT set that teaches the reasoning format before RL. Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct removes queries that are hard to verify and queries it can answer without a chain of thought. QwQ-32B generates candidate responses, and responses are dropped for a wrong final answer, substantial repetition, guessing without reasoning, a summary that disagrees with the thinking, language mixing, or suspicious similarity to validation items. The report says the goal was to instil reasoning patterns with as few samples and steps as possible, leaving the gains to RL.

**Reasoning RL.** GRPO on 3,995 query-verifier pairs, chosen to be unused in the cold start, learnable by the cold-start model, as hard as possible, and broad across sub-domains. The report says large batches, many rollouts per query and off-policy training helped, and that entropy was kept stable or rising. For Qwen3-235B-A22B, AIME'24 went from 70.1 to 85.1 over 170 RL steps.

**Thinking mode fusion.** Continued SFT on a mix of thinking data (rejection-sampled from the stage 2 model on stage 1 queries) and non-thinking data across coding, maths, instruction following, multilingual tasks, writing, QA and role-play. A `/think` or `/no_think` flag in the user or system message picks the mode; non-thinking responses keep an empty thinking block, thinking is the default, and in multi-turn conversations the last flag wins. The report says the ability to stop thinking at a token budget and answer from partial reasoning emerged from this stage rather than being trained directly.

**General RL.** Over 20 tasks with their own scoring: instruction and format following (including respecting the mode flags), preference alignment, multi-turn tool use with real environment feedback, and specialised scenarios such as retrieval-augmented answering. Rewards come in three kinds: rule-based, a model (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) scoring against a reference answer, and a preference reward model where there is no reference.

**Strong-to-weak distillation.** The five smaller dense models and Qwen3-30B-A3B do not run the four stages. They are distilled from Qwen3-32B or Qwen3-235B-A22B in two phases: off-policy, on teacher outputs in both modes, then on-policy, where the student generates and its logits are aligned with the teacher's to minimise KL.

## Distillation against RL

Table 21 is the reason this teardown sits in the distillation module. From the same off-policy-distilled Qwen3-8B checkpoint:

| Path from the checkpoint | AIME'24 | AIME'24 pass@64 | GPU hours |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starting checkpoint | 55.0 | 90.0 | |
| Reinforcement learning | 67.6 | 90.0 | 17,920 |
| On-policy distillation | 74.4 | 93.3 | 1,800 |

<Predict id="trn-dm-qwen3-points-per-kgpuh" question="Using Table 21, compute AIME'24 points gained per thousand GPU hours for each path. What is on-policy distillation's rate divided by RL's?" answer={15.3} tolerance={0.5}>
RL gains 67.6 - 55.0 = 12.6 points for 17.92 thousand GPU hours, 0.70 points per thousand. On-policy distillation gains 74.4 - 55.0 = 19.4 points for 1.8 thousand, 10.8 per thousand. The ratio is 19.4 × 17,920 / (12.6 × 1,800) = 15.3. The headline "about a tenth of the GPU hours" compares hours only; per point gained the gap is wider, because distillation also reached the higher score.
</Predict>

The pass@64 column matters more than its size suggests. RL left pass@64 unchanged at 90.0, which fits the reading that RL sharpens what the model can already sample; on-policy distillation raised it, which fits the reading that a teacher can add reachable solutions. The report presents this as support for distilling small models instead of running RL on each.

## Cost and latency

The report gives GPU hours for the two distillation-versus-RL rows and nothing else: no pretraining compute, no GPU type, no cost for the flagship's four stages. What the design implies about cost is structural. The four-stage pipeline runs once per flagship, and every small model then costs one distillation run against a teacher that already exists. The teacher must be served during on-policy distillation, since every student sample needs teacher log-probabilities, so the 1,800 hours either include teacher scoring or exclude a cost a team reproducing this would pay; the report does not say which.

At inference the cost driver is the thinking mode. A thinking budget lets a caller cap reasoning tokens, and the report presents scaling curves for it without numeric values in the text, so the latency-quality trade-off has to be measured per task.

## Failure modes

The report publishes one regression of its own design. Table 22 tracks the flagship in thinking mode through stages 3 and 4: AIME'24 went from 83.8 after reasoning RL to 81.9 after mode fusion and 81.4 after general RL, and LiveCodeBench v5 from 68.4 to 67.2 and 65.7. The team states it accepted the trade-off for versatility. Anyone copying the pipeline should expect each general-purpose stage to cost some specialist performance and should gate it on the specialist benchmarks, not only on the general ones the stage targets.

Two more follow from the design. Mode fusion puts two behaviours in one set of weights, controlled by a flag in the prompt; a flag dropped by a client template or overridden by a later turn silently switches modes, and the report's own rule that the last flag wins makes that a property of the conversation, not of the request. And distillation copies the teacher: a small model inherits the flagship's verbosity, its failure patterns and any contamination in its training, and the small model's own report card cannot show which.

## What is not public

The report does not give the pretraining data mixture proportions. It says mixtures were optimised at the instance level using annotations over more than 30T tokens, but not the ratios, the sources, or how the 5T stage 2 tokens were selected.

It does not publish the RL environments in full. The 3,995 query-verifier pairs are described by their selection criteria, not released; the more than 20 general RL tasks are described by category, with no task list, reward weights or reward model details beyond the three reward types.

It gives no numeric batch size or rollout count for reasoning RL, no size for the cold-start set (only that QwQ-32B generated N candidates per query), no thresholds for the response filters, and no account of how the teacher was chosen per student or what the on-policy phase's step count was. It does not state whether the 1,800 GPU hours include teacher inference. The GPU type behind "GPU hours" is not stated. None of these should be filled in from guesses when describing the system.

<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Yes, with conditions. The month and the single RL slot rule out running the full pipeline per size, and Qwen3's Table 21 shows on-policy distillation from a shared checkpoint beat RL at about a tenth of the GPU hours on its benchmarks. The conditions are a tokenizer shared across the family, teacher serving capacity during distillation, and per-size gates on the specialist benchmarks, because distillation copies the teacher's regressions as faithfully as its skills.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The constraint conflict is calendar against coverage. Four RL runs on one slot do not fit a month; four distillation runs need the teacher served but can share it, so they fit if the scoring capacity exists. Qwen3 is evidence the split works at these sizes; it is one family on mostly maths and code benchmarks, so I would rerun the comparison once on our own evals for one small size before committing all four.</p>
<p>Before trusting each small model I check three things. Mode behaviour, if we have modes: flag handling across templates and multi-turn conversations, since the student learns it from the teacher's outputs. Specialist regressions inherited from the teacher's general stages, like Qwen3's AIME'24 drop from 83.8 to 81.4. And length: the student's output length against its latency budget, since small models are deployed where latency matters most.</p>
<p>What I give up: the small models cannot exceed the teacher on anything, and a teacher defect ships five times. The decision note records which evaluations gate each size and the condition for running RL on a small model directly, which is a task where it must beat the teacher.</p>
