## Three methods under one name

When a training plan says "distil the 70B into an 8B", it can mean three different jobs with different requirements on the teacher, different cost profiles and different ceilings.

**Logit distillation** (Hinton, Vinyals and Dean, 2015) trains the student to match the teacher's full output distribution at every position, softened by a temperature. It needs the teacher's logits over a shared vocabulary.

**SFT on teacher samples**, sometimes called sequence-level or off-policy distillation, has the teacher generate complete responses and fine-tunes the student on them with ordinary next-token loss. It needs only the teacher's text, so the tokenizers can differ and the teacher can sit behind an API that returns no log-probabilities. DeepSeek-R1's distilled models are this kind.

**On-policy distillation** has the student generate, and the teacher scores every token the student produced. The student learns from its own trajectories, as in RL, but with a per-token signal instead of one reward per episode. It needs the teacher's log-probabilities on the student's tokens, so the teacher must be served during training and must tokenize the same way.

The choice between them is mostly decided by two facts about your setup: what the teacher exposes (text, top-k log-probs, full logits), and whether the student's mistakes on its own outputs are what you need to fix.

## Soft targets, with toy numbers

A teacher that is confident and right puts almost all mass on one class. The information about how it generalises sits in the ratios between the wrong classes, which at temperature 1 are tiny and contribute almost nothing to a cross-entropy gradient. Hinton's fix is to divide the logits by a temperature `T` before the softmax, for both teacher and student during training, and to run the student at `T = 1` afterwards.

At `T = 4` the logits become 1, 0.5 and 0. The exponentials are 2.718, 1.649 and 1, which sum to 5.367, and 1.649 / 5.367 is 30.7%. The ranking is unchanged; what changes is how much gradient the non-top classes produce, which is the knowledge the student is meant to copy.
</Predict>

Two details in the paper are easy to miss. First, softening the targets shrinks the soft-target gradients by `1/T²`, so the paper multiplies them by `T²` when it also trains on hard labels; otherwise changing `T` silently reweights the two loss terms. Second, the best results used a considerably lower weight on the hard-label term than on the soft one.

The numbers that made the case are small by current standards and still instructive. On MNIST, a large regularised net made 67 test errors, a small unregularised net 146, and the same small net trained to match the large net's soft targets at `T = 20` made 74. On an 85M-parameter speech model, a 10-model ensemble raised test frame accuracy from 58.9% to 61.1%; one distilled model reached 60.8%, so more than 80% of the ensemble's gain survived compression into one model's inference cost. The most useful result for planning data budgets: trained on 3% of the speech data, the model reached 44.5% with hard labels (and needed early stopping) against 57.0% with soft targets, close to the 58.9% of the full-data baseline. Soft targets carry more bits per example than labels do.

For LLMs the same idea becomes a per-position KL between teacher and student next-token distributions. In practice teams often keep only the teacher's top-k log-probs per position to bound storage, which turns "full distribution" into "head of the distribution" and is a decision worth writing down.

## SFT on samples: R1's 800K

DeepSeek-R1's distilled models were fine-tuned on about 800K samples curated with R1, with no RL stage afterwards. The results set the reference point for this whole area. On AIME 2024 pass@1, the distilled Qwen-7B scored 55.5 and the distilled Qwen-32B 72.6. The paper's own control is the interesting row: RL run directly on Qwen-32B-Base for over 10K steps reached 47.0, below both the distilled 32B and QwQ-32B-Preview at 50.0. The paper concludes that distilling a stronger model into a smaller one beats running large-scale RL on the smaller one, and adds that advancing beyond the teacher may still need stronger base models and more RL.

The cost profile is a batch pipeline: generate once with the teacher, filter (the next lesson's subject), then train as many students as you like on the same data. The teacher's generation cost is amortised across students and across reruns. The weakness is distribution mismatch. The student learns from the teacher's prefixes and never from its own, so at inference, once it has made a mistake the teacher would not have made, it is in a state no training example covered. That compounding error is the reason on-policy methods exist.

The other cost shows up in the outputs. DeepSeek-V3's ablation of R1 distillation into DeepSeek-V2.5 raised MATH-500 pass@1 from 74.6 to 83.2, and the mean response length went from 769 to 1,510 tokens. The V3 report names overthinking, poor formatting and excessive length as problems with R1 data. A distilled student inherits the teacher's verbosity unless the filter or the training mix pushes back, and that length is paid on every inference call.

## On-policy distillation: dense reward from a teacher

On-policy distillation samples a trajectory from the student, then asks the teacher for its log-probability of each sampled token. Thinking Machines describes the loss as per-token reverse KL with a discount factor of zero: each token's advantage is the negative of `log p_student(x_t) - log p_teacher(x_t)`. Reverse KL is mode-seeking; the student is pushed toward behaviour the teacher would produce from wherever the student actually is, and is not asked to cover all of the teacher's alternatives.

```python
def per_token_advantages(student_logprobs, teacher_logprobs):
    """One sampled trajectory; both lists hold log-probs of the tokens the student emitted."""
    # A single-sample estimate of reverse KL at each position; the policy-gradient step then
    # raises tokens the teacher likes more than the student does and lowers the others.
    return [t - s for s, t in zip(student_logprobs, teacher_logprobs)]


adv = per_token_advantages([-0.1, -2.3, -0.4], [-0.2, -0.3, -0.5])
# [-0.1, 2.0, -0.1]: the second token is likely under the teacher and unlikely under the
# student, so it gets the only large advantage.
```

The post frames the efficiency argument as information per episode: an RL episode with one scalar reward teaches on the order of one bit, while a per-token teacher signal teaches on the order of one signal per token. The published comparison is from the Qwen3 report, Table 21. Starting from the same off-policy-distilled Qwen3-8B checkpoint at 55.0 on AIME'24, RL reached 67.6 for 17,920 GPU hours and on-policy distillation reached 74.4 for 1,800, against a Qwen3-32B or Qwen3-235B-A22B teacher. That is 17,920 / 1,800 = 10.0 times fewer GPU hours for a higher score. Thinking Machines reproduced the shape at a smaller scale: from an SFT checkpoint at 60%, on-policy distillation reached 70% in about 150 steps, and the post puts the saving at 9 to 30 times in FLOPs against extending SFT, 9 times when the SFT data already exists.

Read the 10x with its conditions. Both runs start from a checkpoint that already absorbed a strong teacher's samples, and the teacher already exists; the GPU hours of building the teacher are not in either column. The Qwen3 table does not say whether teacher scoring is inside the 1,800. Problem trn-059 asks you to redo the comparison for a team without those conditions.

The same mechanism has a second use. Thinking Machines mid-trained Qwen3-8B on internal documents and lost chat behaviour: IF-eval fell from 85% to 45% with documents only, and to 79% with a 70/30 mix of documents and chat. On-policy distillation from the earlier version of the model restored IF-eval to 83% while internal QA stayed at 41% against the original model's 18%. Distillation from your own earlier checkpoint is a repair tool for behaviour lost during continued training.

## Distillation versus RL is not only a cost question

Yue et al. measured pass@k for RL-trained models against their base models at k up to 1024. RL models win at small k and the base models win at large k: RL makes the model more likely to sample a correct path it could already sample, but rarely creates paths the base could not reach. On MATH500 at k = 128, 1.0% of problems were solved only by the RL model and 3.6% only by the base. Distillation behaved differently in the same study: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B's pass@k curve sat above its base model's across the tested k, which the authors read as distillation importing reasoning patterns from the teacher.

The Qwen3 table is consistent with that reading. RL left pass@64 on AIME'24 at 90.0; on-policy distillation raised it to 93.3.

The decision procedure that follows:

- If the target behaviour exists in a stronger model you can query, distil first. It is cheaper per point and can raise the ceiling.
- If you need to exceed every available teacher, or the behaviour is defined by a verifier rather than by a model (passing your test suite, your tool environment), RL is the tool, and distillation is the warm start.
- If the teacher exposes only text, SFT on samples is the only form available; on-policy distillation needs log-probs on the student's tokens.

## When this is wrong

The 10x from the Qwen3 table is one model size, one domain (competition maths and code), one starting checkpoint and one teacher. It says nothing about tasks where the teacher is barely better than the student; the per-token signal is only as good as the teacher's preferences, and a weak teacher grades confidently toward its own errors. On-policy distillation also inherits the teacher's blind spots exactly where reverse KL is mode-seeking: if the teacher assigns low probability to a valid but unusual approach, the student is pushed off it.

Yue et al.'s result is about RL with verifiable rewards as practised in 2025, on maths, code and visual reasoning, with the base model sampled up to k = 1024. Their own limitations section notes that strong RL pipelines are proprietary and moving fast; a large-k pass@k also counts lucky guesses on short-answer benchmarks as coverage, which flatters the base model. The finding is evidence about current recipes, not a theorem about RL.

Finally, every number on this page is a benchmark score, and the benchmarks in question appear widely on the web. A distilled student can inherit contamination from its teacher's training data without any of its own training data containing the test, which is one more reason the next lesson treats the contamination check as part of the filter.
