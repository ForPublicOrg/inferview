## The filter is the product

A synthetic-data pipeline has three parts: a prompt source, a generator, and a filter that decides what becomes training data. The first two are cheap to scale. The filter decides quality, and it also decides the distribution: every filter keeps some kinds of example more readily than others, and the trained model inherits that skew. Designing the pipeline means choosing the filter, measuring what it removes, and checking that nothing it keeps is a test item.

The published pipelines use four kinds of filter, often stacked.

| Filter | Used by | What it can certify | What it biases toward |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground-truth answer or verifier | Nemotron-4 (GSM8K, MATH, instruction following), Qwen3 cold start | the final answer | tasks with checkable answers; reasoning that happens to reach them |
| Executed tests or a sandbox | Llama 3 code, Kimi K2 coding | the behaviour the tests cover | easy-to-test code; whatever the tests miss |
| Reward model | Nemotron-4, Llama 3 rejection sampling | the RM's preferences | the RM's blind spots and length preference |
| LLM judge with a rubric | Nemotron-4 (early iterations), Kimi K2 agentic trajectories | rubric items the judge can see | the judge's position and verbosity biases |

## Rejection sampling, with toy numbers

The simplest filtered generator samples K responses per prompt and keeps the best one that passes. Llama 3 samples typically 10 to 30 outputs per prompt from its latest policy and lets the reward model choose. With a hard verifier, the useful question is how many prompts end up with any passing sample at all.

<Predict id="trn-dm-synth-yield" question="A generator passes the verifier on 15% of samples, independently per sample. You draw K = 10 samples per prompt. What percentage of prompts end up with at least one passing sample?" answer={80.3} tolerance={1} unit="%">
The chance that all ten fail is 0.85^10 = 0.197, so 1 - 0.197 = 80.3% of prompts yield at least one passing sample. The other 19.7% are not a random fifth: in practice the pass rate varies by prompt, and the prompts with no passing sample are concentrated among the hardest. Raising K recovers some of them at linear cost; the filter, not K, decides whether the hardest prompts are represented at all.
</Predict>

Llama 3 reports exactly this failure. Its code data passed through a model-as-judge filter that scored correctness and style and kept only perfect scores, and the report says the filter initially hurt benchmark performance because it removed many of the challenging examples. The fix was to revise the responses to the hardest problems until they passed, not to loosen the filter. For maths, Llama 3 uses code execution as a feedback signal to drop reasoning chains that are not valid. A verifier that checks only the final answer certifies the answer, not the reasoning that reached it.

## Nemotron-4: a reward model as the filter

Nemotron-4 340B is the cleanest public case of a pipeline where almost everything is generated. The report says over 98% of the data in its alignment process was synthetic; the human-annotated part was about 20K examples, 10K for SFT and the 10K-example HelpSteer2 set used for the reward model and preference tuning. Prompts came from Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct over generated topic lists, closed-QA prompts were built on C4 documents, and responses were scored by Nemotron-4-340B-Reward, with dialogues below a threshold dropped.

The filtering decisions in the report are the part worth copying:

- **Use ground truth where it exists.** For GSM8K- and MATH-style prompts and verifiable instruction-following prompts, correct responses were chosen by checking, not by the reward model.
- **Check the judge for position bias.** When an LLM judged a pair of responses, the pair was kept only if the verdict held with the responses swapped.
- **Measure the judge.** Later iterations replaced the LLM judge with the reward model as judge, which scored 0.87 against 0.54 on Chat-Hard. The reward model's own RewardBench breakdown shows where it is weak: 92.0 overall, 67.4 on the reasoning subset. A reward-model filter is a weaker filter for reasoning data than a verifier.
- **Iterate.** The first round used Mixtral-generated data to train an intermediate 340B model, which then generated the second round's data. The report says the teacher did not impose a ceiling on the student; that is its own observation on its own benchmarks.

## DeepSeek-V3 and Kimi K2: two other uses

DeepSeek-V3's 1.5M-instance SFT set mixes reasoning data generated by an internal DeepSeek-R1, through domain expert models and rejection sampling, with non-reasoning data from DeepSeek-V2.5 that human annotators verified. The report names the problem the filter had to solve: R1's data was accurate but suffered from overthinking, poor formatting and excessive length. Each reasoning instance therefore produced two kinds of sample, the problem with its original response and the problem with a reflective system prompt and R1's response, and rejection sampling picked the final data. The filter optimised for a property (concise, well-formatted correctness) that the generator did not have.

Kimi K2 uses synthetic data in pretraining instead of post-training. It rephrases high-quality knowledge text with prompts that vary style and perspective, rewrites long documents chunk by chunk, and checks each rephrasing for fidelity to its source. The report's SimpleQA ablation compares spending ten passes over the same knowledge differently: the raw text for 10 epochs scored 23.76, one rephrasing for 10 epochs 27.39, and ten different rephrasings for one epoch each 28.94. Here the filter is the fidelity check, and the risk the report names is hallucination: a rephrasing that adds a fact the source does not contain teaches it ten times over. For agentic data the same report keeps a simulated tool-use trajectory only when an LLM judge finds the task's rubric met, and uses real sandboxes with test pass rates for coding.

## The contamination check ships with the filter

A generator that has seen the internet will reproduce benchmark items, and a filter that rewards correct answers will prefer the ones the generator memorised. Contamination is therefore a property of the pipeline, not only of the corpus.

Llama 3 reports both halves. Its post-training data was decontaminated by exact match against benchmark prompts. Its pretraining analysis scored each benchmark example by 8-gram overlap with the pretraining corpus and estimated the score gain on contaminated examples: HellaSwag had 85% of examples flagged with an estimated gain of 14.8 points for the 8B model, while for HumanEval, MBPP and MMLU the report says 8-gram overlap could not produce a good estimate. Exact match is the floor, and n-gram overlap misses paraphrases, which is the very thing a rephrasing pipeline produces.

```python
def ngrams(tokens, n=8):
    return {tuple(tokens[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(tokens) - n + 1)}


def build_index(benchmark_items, n=8):
    index = set()
    for item in benchmark_items:
        index |= ngrams(item.lower().split(), n)
    return index


def overlap(sample, index, n=8):
    """Fraction of the sample's n-grams that appear in any benchmark item."""
    grams = ngrams(sample.lower().split(), n)
    return len(grams & index) / len(grams) if grams else 0.0

# Drop a sample above a threshold you calibrate on known-contaminated and known-clean pairs;
# log every drop with the benchmark it matched so the threshold can be audited.
```

Whitespace tokens and lower-casing are a first pass. A paraphrase defeats it, so the check that matters for rephrased data is semantic: nearest-neighbour search of each kept sample against the benchmark items with an embedding model, with hits reviewed by a person at a threshold set on labelled pairs. The cost conflict is real: the semantic check is expensive at millions of samples, and a team on a deadline is tempted to skip it, which is exactly when a jump on a public benchmark should be read as contamination until shown otherwise.

## When this is wrong

Every number above is the report author's measurement of its own pipeline on its own evaluations, and the reports describe filters at the level of "scored by a reward model and thresholded" without the thresholds. Nemotron-4's claim that the teacher did not cap the student, and Kimi K2's rephrasing gain, are single ablations on specific benchmarks; neither says how far either effect extends.

The framing of a filter as a gate also understates a failure none of these reports measures: when one model is trained on its own filtered outputs round after round, the kept set can narrow even though every example passes. The pipelines here run few rounds and change generators between them; a pipeline that loops one model on its own outputs needs a diversity measure on the kept set, not only a quality score.

Finally, a verifier certifies what it checks. Tests that cover the happy path, answer checkers that ignore the reasoning, and rubrics a judge cannot verify from the transcript all produce data that is correct by the filter and wrong by the user.
