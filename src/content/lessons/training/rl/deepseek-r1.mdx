## What it is and who runs it

DeepSeek-R1 is DeepSeek-AI's reasoning model, released with open weights in January 2025 together with R1-Zero and six smaller distilled models. The paper's claim is that long chain-of-thought reasoning can be produced by RL on a base model with rule-based rewards, with no supervised reasoning examples first. R1-Zero is that experiment; R1 is the production pipeline built around it to fix what R1-Zero got wrong; the distilled models test whether the reasoning transfers by SFT alone.

There are two versions of the paper with different numbers for the same model. v1 (January 2025) reports R1-Zero's AIME 2024 pass@1 rising from 15.6% to 71.0%; v2 reports 15.6% to 77.9% and adds training details v1 left out. This teardown says which version each figure comes from.

**R1-Zero: GRPO on a base model.** GRPO applied directly to DeepSeek-V3-Base, with a template that asks for reasoning inside think tags and the answer inside answer tags, and two rule-based rewards: accuracy (the answer checked against a reference, code through test cases) and format (the tags present), combined with equal weight in v2. No learned reward model, because a neural reward model is "susceptible to reward hacking" at this scale and retraining it adds cost. v2 gives the run: learning rate 3e-6, KL coefficient 0.001, 16 samples per question at temperature 1, 32 questions per step for a batch of 32 × 16 = 512 rollouts, 10,400 steps (about 1.6 epochs), and the KL reference replaced with the current policy every 400 steps. The paper's observation is that response length and reflective behaviour grew without being asked for, including a sudden rise in the word "wait" at one intermediate checkpoint.

**Cold-start data.** R1-Zero's outputs were hard to read and mixed languages. R1 starts instead from DeepSeek-V3-Base fine-tuned on thousands of long chain-of-thought examples written to be readable, a small supervised stage that sets the output format before RL.

**Reasoning RL.** The same large-scale RL as R1-Zero on the cold-started model, plus a language consistency reward: the proportion of chain-of-thought words in the target language. v1 reports that this reward slightly lowered benchmark performance and was kept for readability, a deliberate trade of score for a property users see.

**Rejection sampling and SFT.** When reasoning RL converged, the checkpoint generated many responses per prompt and only correct, readable ones were kept, giving about 600k reasoning samples; about 200k non-reasoning samples (writing, factual QA, translation and similar) came from DeepSeek-V3's own pipeline. v1 then fine-tunes DeepSeek-V3-Base, not the RL checkpoint, for two epochs on the roughly 800k samples. The RL model is used as a data generator, and the shipped model's weights descend from SFT.

**Second RL stage.** RL on the SFT model across all scenarios: rule-based rewards for reasoning prompts and reward models for general prompts, with helpfulness judged on the final summary only and harmlessness on the whole response including the reasoning. v2 gives 1,700 steps at temperature 0.7, with general data added only in the final 400 steps.

**Distillation into smaller models.** The same 800k samples fine-tune Qwen2.5 and Llama models from 1.5B to 70B, with SFT only and no RL stage. v1 reports AIME 2024 pass@1 of 28.9% (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B), 55.5% (Qwen2.5-Math-7B), 50.4% (Llama-3.1-8B), 69.7% (Qwen2.5-14B), 72.6% (Qwen2.5-32B) and 70.0% (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct).

## Length and template

The template is the only context strategy R1-Zero has: a fixed instruction to reason inside tags and then answer, deliberately minimal so that the paper can attribute the behaviour to RL rather than to prompting. The length budget is the operating constraint that matters. v2 trains R1-Zero with a 32,768-token limit and raises it to 65,536 after step 8.2k, so the run's late stage was allowed responses twice as long; at 512 rollouts per step, the worst-case tokens generated per step went from 512 × 32,768 = 16.8M to 33.6M. A pipeline built on this recipe has to plan inference capacity for the length the policy grows into, not the length it starts at.

## Reward sources and what each can see

Every reward in the pipeline is chosen for what it cannot be fooled by easily. R1-Zero and the reasoning stage use only rules: the policy can satisfy the format check without being right, which is why accuracy carries equal weight, and it cannot talk a rule into a higher score. The language reward is a word count, so it is cheap and gameable only in the direction the designers wanted. Learned reward models appear only in the second RL stage and only for general prompts, and helpfulness is judged on the summary rather than the reasoning, which keeps the reasoning out of the reward model's view, the same separation the reward-hacking lesson argues for with monitors.

## Cost and compute

The paper's steps and batch sizes let you size the R1-Zero run in rollouts: 10,400 steps × 512 = 5.3M sampled responses, each up to 32,768 or 65,536 tokens. The second RL stage adds 1,700 steps. The comparison the paper itself draws is between RL and distillation for a smaller model: Qwen2.5-32B trained with large-scale RL for over 10K steps (DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B) reaches 47.0% on AIME 2024, while the same base fine-tuned on R1's 800k samples reaches 72.6%. For a model of that size, SFT on a stronger model's outputs beat RL from scratch on that benchmark.

## Failure modes

**R1-Zero's readability and language mixing.** The rule rewards say nothing about the reasoning text, so the policy wrote whatever helped it reach the answer, in mixed languages. The fixes were a supervised cold start and a language reward that cost some score.

**Process reward model.** The paper lists three reasons the PRM failed: a fine-grained step is hard to define in general reasoning; judging whether an intermediate step is correct is hard, because automatic annotation is unreliable and manual annotation does not scale; and a model-based PRM leads to reward hacking and needs retraining. The post-training module's reward-model lesson compares this with results where PRMs rerank samples successfully.

**Monte Carlo tree search.** Token generation has a search space far larger than a board game; capping each node's extensions to make search tractable can trap the model in local optima; and the value model that guides search is hard to train at fine granularity. The paper reports that search at inference could improve performance with a pretrained value model, but iterative self-improvement through search remained a challenge.

## What is not public

The full data mixtures are not published. That covers the prompts and verifiers used in each RL stage and their domain mix, the cold-start examples beyond "thousands" and how they were written, the content and sources of the roughly 600k reasoning and 200k non-reasoning SFT samples, and the reward models used in the second RL stage and their training data. Without these, the pipeline's shape can be reproduced and its results cannot, and claims about which ingredient mattered most rest on the paper's word.

<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Why distil.</strong> The paper's numbers say SFT on a stronger model's filtered outputs beats RL from scratch at this size, at a fraction of the rollouts. Six weeks does not allow a 10K-step RL run plus iteration.</p>
<p><strong>Filtering is the verifier work.</strong> R1's rejection sampling kept correct and readable samples. I apply my maths and code verifiers to the teacher's outputs, so the SFT data contains no wrong answers the teacher wrote confidently.</p>
<p><strong>A short RL stage after.</strong> R1's distilled models had no RL; the paper presents that as the baseline, not the ceiling. A short GRPO-family stage with rule rewards, on prompts filtered to a middle solve rate, is where remaining budget goes.</p>
<p><strong>What you give up.</strong> Independence from the teacher: its licence terms, its errors and its style come with the data, and the model will not exceed the teacher on the distribution it imitates.</p>
<p><strong>Failure mode.</strong> The teacher's samples are long; a 14B model trained on traces that long may need serving capacity the product did not plan for.</p>
