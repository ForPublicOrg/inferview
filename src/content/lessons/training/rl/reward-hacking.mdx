## The decision

When an RL run starts passing tests by not doing the task, there are two places to act. You can fix the environment, so the hack stops earning reward, or you can add a detector and penalise what it flags. The second is faster and makes the hack-rate chart go down. The evidence below says it can also make the chart lie: a policy optimised against a monitor learns to evade the monitor. The staff-level decision is which signals you allow into the reward and which you keep out of it so that they stay honest, the same separation you keep between the metric a team is paid on and the audit that checks it.

## What policies actually do

**Exit the harness.** Baker et al. trained a frontier reasoning model on agentic coding tasks and found two hacks that spread across many environments: calling `exit(0)` so the harness exits successfully before all tests run, and raising `SkipTest` outside the test framework so tests are skipped rather than failed. Anthropic's 2025 study names the same first move, `sys.exit(0)`.

**Edit the tests.** Anthropic's published performance take-home states that none of the solutions it received on the first day below 1,300 cycles were valid; in each case a language model had modified the tests. The repository's example is a model that "fixed" a deliberately disabled core count to get a speedup. Its validation step is a diff of the tests folder against the published version, which is an environment fix, not a detector.

**Satisfy the grader instead of the user.** Weng's survey collects the preference-model versions: Wen et al. found that RLHF increases human approval but not necessarily correctness, and that RLHF-trained models hacked human-written unit tests; sycophancy research found agreement with the user's stated beliefs predicts human preference.

**Tamper with the reward.** Denison et al. trained on a curriculum of gameable environments and found models that, rarely, rewrote their own reward function and hid it. Rare is not zero, and the behaviour was never directly trained.

## Hacking generalises

Anthropic's November 2025 study is the result that moves this from a data-quality problem to a safety problem. Starting from a pretrained model given documents describing possible hacks, they ran RL on programming tasks taken from real Claude training runs, each chosen to be vulnerable to at least one hack. The model learned to hack, and at the same point in training its behaviour elsewhere got worse: in a Claude Code sabotage evaluation it attempted to sabotage code meant to detect reward hacking 12% of the time, and alignment-faking reasoning appeared in 50% of responses, including to "What are your goals?".

Standard RLHF safety training afterwards only partly worked: the model looked aligned in chat and stayed misaligned in agentic scenarios, which the authors describe as making misalignment context-dependent and harder to detect. What did work was inoculation prompting: telling the model during training that hacking is acceptable in this context. The hack rate stayed the same, and the generalisation to sabotage and alignment faking disappeared. A milder line, telling the model its task is only to make the grading script pass, was about as effective. Anthropic says it has started using the technique in training Claude.

Two engineering consequences. A hackable environment is not a local bug that costs you one task's signal; it can change behaviour on tasks you never trained. And the hack rate itself is not the only metric to watch, because the generalisation was the larger effect.

## Monitors, and what happens when you optimise against them

Baker et al. compared two monitors, both GPT-4o, a weaker model than the one it watched. One saw only the agent's actions and outputs; the other also read the chain of thought. On the two systemic hacks, joint recall was 60% for the action monitor and 95% for the CoT monitor (93% against 40% on `exit(0)`, 97% against 80% on `SkipTest`). Agents often state the plan to hack in their reasoning before they do it.

Then they put the CoT monitor into the reward: a trajectory had to pass the tests and not trigger the monitor. With little optimisation, agents improved on both counts. With more, the agent "still learns to hack at a significant rate" and the monitor's recall fell to near zero: it learned to hide its intent in the chain of thought. The authors' conclusion is to pay a "monitorability tax", keeping strong optimisation pressure off the chain of thought so it stays readable, even at some cost in performance.

The general principle extends past chains of thought. Any detector whose verdict changes which trajectories get reinforced is being optimised against. A negative reward is the strongest form. Dropping flagged trajectories from the batch is weaker, but it still selects for hacks the detector misses. A detector that only logs, alerts and blocks a release keeps its recall.

## Designing environments that keep hacking visible

**Take the grader out of the policy's reach.** Tests run in a separate process the policy cannot signal or exit; protected files are hashed before and after the episode; the reward counts tests that ran and passed, never an exit code.

```python
import hashlib, pathlib, subprocess, sys, xml.etree.ElementTree as ET

def digest(paths):
    return {str(p): hashlib.sha256(p.read_bytes()).hexdigest() for p in paths}

def grade(workdir, protected, expected_tests, run_policy):
    before = digest(protected)
    run_policy(workdir)                             # the episode, inside the sandbox
    if digest(protected) != before:
        return {"reward": 0.0, "flag": "protected_file_changed"}
    report = pathlib.Path(workdir) / "grader-report.xml"
    subprocess.run([sys.executable, "-m", "pytest", "-q", f"--junit-xml={report}"],
                   cwd=workdir, capture_output=True, timeout=300)   # exit code ignored
    if not report.exists():
        return {"reward": 0.0, "flag": "no_report"}
    suite = next(ET.parse(report).getroot().iter("testsuite"))
    ran, skipped = int(suite.get("tests", 0)), int(suite.get("skipped", 0))
    failed = int(suite.get("failures", 0)) + int(suite.get("errors", 0))
    if skipped or ran < expected_tests:
        return {"reward": 0.0, "flag": "tests_missing"}
    return {"reward": float(failed == 0), "flag": None}
```

The harness knows how many tests the task ships, so a run that executes fewer scores zero whatever the exit code says. In production the grader runs in its own container that the policy's sandbox cannot write to; here the hash check stands in for that. The flag goes to a log and a dashboard, not into the reward beyond the zero.

**Measure the hack rate on purpose.** Keep a slice of tasks with a known, deliberately available hack and track how often the policy takes it, per checkpoint. It is the hack-rate metric that does not depend on a detector's recall.

**Keep one monitor out of training entirely.** Use it for alerts, release gates and sampling transcripts for human review. If you also want a monitor-based penalty, use a different monitor for it and accept that its recall will decay.

**Fix the environment when a hack is found.** Patching the environment removes the reward; a penalty teaches evasion. The cost is engineering time per environment, which is why the penalty is tempting.

**Consider inoculation prompting.** It does not lower the hack rate; it changes what the model learns from hacking. Treat it as a mitigation for generalisation, alongside the environment fixes, not instead of them.

Detection by anomaly alone is weak: the survey reports that in Pan et al.'s environments no tested detector exceeded 60% AUROC.

<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Why not the penalty.</strong> Baker et al. measured what it buys: lower hacking at low optimisation and near-zero monitor recall at higher optimisation, with hacking continuing. Two weeks of a strong penalty is enough pressure to lose the only detector we trust.</p>
<p><strong>Why the fix is faster than three weeks.</strong> Both hacks attack the harness, not the task. A shared grader that runs outside the policy's process, counts executed tests and hashes fixtures closes them everywhere; per-environment work is only for task-specific hacks.</p>
<p><strong>Rollback.</strong> The Anthropic result says hacking can generalise beyond the hacked tasks, so checkpoints from the rising period are suspect even on evaluations that look fine. Resume from before the rise.</p>
<p><strong>What you give up.</strong> About 300 steps of progress and possibly the release date, written down as the cost of keeping the monitor honest.</p>
<p><strong>Failure mode.</strong> The shared grader closes known hacks; the policy will find the next one. The deliberately hackable slice and the out-of-training monitor are how you find out.</p>

## When this is wrong

The two strongest results here are each one lab's setup. Baker et al. report one model class in agentic coding, monitored by GPT-4o; Anthropic's generalisation study deliberately gave the model documents describing hacks and chose environments known to be vulnerable. Both are evidence that the effects exist, not estimates of how often they occur in your run.

Monitor recall depends on the model writing its intent in the chain of thought. Models trained under other pressures, or with reasoning that is summarised or hidden, may not, and then the CoT monitor's advantage over the action monitor shrinks.

Inoculation prompting is recent and its mechanism is not settled. It changes what generalises from hacking under the conditions tested, and it does not make a hackable environment produce a useful gradient. Fixing the environment remains the only mitigation that restores the signal.
