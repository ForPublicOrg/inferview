## The decision

Every RL system for LLMs is two programs sharing one set of weights: an inference engine that generates rollouts fast, and a trainer that computes gradients. The objective from the PPO lesson assumes the samples came from π_old, the policy being trained. In a real system they come from a different program with different kernels, possibly from weights one or more steps behind. Three decisions follow. Where the engine runs (on the training GPUs or its own). How far behind its weights may lag. And how the loss corrects for the gap, or whether you remove the gap by making the engine and trainer agree bit for bit.

## The mismatch nobody configured

TRL's documentation states it plainly: the inference engine and the training engine are "in theory" mathematically identical, and in practice produce different outputs through precision effects and hardware-specific optimisations. The consequence is that "an otherwise on policy RL problem" becomes off-policy, and the gradient is biased. Yao et al. (NeurIPS 2025 OPT workshop) measured significantly different token probabilities between vLLM rollouts and FSDP training on the same weights.

Part of the gap is ordinary floating-point non-associativity in different kernels. Thinking Machines identified a less obvious part: kernels that are not batch-invariant. A server's batch size depends on load, reductions in matmul, normalisation and attention change their split with batch size, and so one request's logits depend on what else was in the batch. Their measurement: Qwen3-235B-A22B at temperature 0, the same prompt 1,000 times, 1,000 tokens each, gave 80 distinct completions, all identical for 102 tokens and diverging at the 103rd. With batch-invariant kernels all 1,000 were identical.

The RL consequence is in the same post. On an RLVR run with Qwen2.5-VL 8B, with no off-policy correction the reward collapsed partway through, with a spike in the sampler-trainer KL near step 318. With importance weighting the run was smooth and the KL stayed around 0.001. With bitwise-identical sampler and trainer the KL was 0 and the run was smooth too. The cost of identical numerics on Qwen3-8B, 1,000 sequences: 26 seconds with vLLM's defaults, 55 seconds with unoptimised deterministic kernels, 42 seconds with an improved attention kernel.

Your first instrument is the per-token difference between the engine's log-probabilities and the trainer's recomputed ones. TRL logs it as `sampling/sampling_logp_difference/mean`; a growing value means a widening mismatch, and it is the chart to check before blaming the learning rate.

## Correcting it: truncated and masked importance sampling

If the engine sampled token t with probability q and the trainer assigns it probability p, an unbiased correction multiplies that token's loss by p / q. Unbounded, one token the engine sampled at 0.001 and the trainer scores at 0.05 gets a weight of 50 and dominates the batch. Truncated importance sampling caps the weight; masked importance sampling zeroes samples outside a range instead. TRL's GRPO trainer turns correction on by default, uses sequence-level masking by default, and for TIS clips ratios to a configured range with an upper bound of 3.0 by default (main-branch docs, checked 2026-10-28).

```python
import math

def tis_weights(logp_trainer, logp_engine, cap=3.0):
    # per-token weight p/q, truncated; multiply the (detached) weight into the token loss
    return [min(math.exp(lt - le), cap) for lt, le in zip(logp_trainer, logp_engine)]

def admit(sample_version, learner_version, max_staleness):
    # actors tag every sample with the weights version that produced it
    return learner_version - sample_version <= max_staleness

print(tis_weights([-2.20, -3.00], [-2.30, -6.91]))  # [1.105..., 3.0]: second token capped
print(admit(sample_version=41, learner_version=43, max_staleness=1))  # False: drop or reweight
```

The first token, sampled at q = 0.100 and scored at p = 0.111, gets 1.105. The second, q = 0.001 and p = 0.050, would get 50 and is capped at 3. The cap trades bias for variance: a lower cap ignores more of the mismatch, a higher one lets single tokens move the update.

Two alternatives remove the need. GSPO's sequence-level ratio is robust enough that its authors use the engine's likelihoods directly instead of recomputing them in the trainer. And batch-invariant, matched kernels make the mismatch zero at the cost Thinking Machines measured. Yao et al. went the other way and found TIS kept training stable even with quantized rollouts, which makes cheaper rollouts available once a correction is in place.

## Where the rollout engine runs

**Colocated.** Engine and trainer on the same GPUs, taking turns. TRL's default runs vLLM inside the trainer process sharing GPU memory. Kimi K2 colocates at 1.04T parameters: each engine releases GPU memory while the other runs, and a distributed checkpoint engine broadcasts the new parameters, with each inference engine pulling only the shards it needs. The report gives a full parameter update in less than 30 seconds. On their H800 cluster, concurrent host-to-device copies and broadcasts saturated PCIe, so the three-stage update pipeline fell back to two stages. No GPU sits idle waiting for the other phase, and weight transfer is a local copy, but the phases serialise and memory has to hold the larger of the two working sets.

**Separate.** Engine on its own GPUs, trainer on others. TRL's server mode talks to vLLM over HTTP and warns that sharing GPUs between them causes NCCL errors. Each side is sized and tuned for its own work, and generation and training can overlap. The cost is a cross-cluster weight transfer every update, and idle capacity on one side whenever the other is the bottleneck.

## Staleness: overlapping generation and training

Synchronous on-policy RL waits for every rollout before training and for training before the next rollout. The step takes generation time plus training time plus weight-sync time. If actors generate batch n + 1 with the weights from step n − 1 while the learner trains on batch n, the step takes the larger of the two, and every sample is one version stale.

<p>With overlap, each step costs max(300, 100) = 300 s, so 3,600 / 300 = <b>12 steps an hour</b> against 3,600 / 400 = 9. The gain is capped by the slower side: generation is 75% of the synchronous step, so overlap can save at most the 25% that training took. The published systems sit at different points. prime-rl, used for INTELLECT-3, is async-only and "always a few steps off-policy". Qwen3's reasoning RL used off-policy training to improve sample efficiency. Kimi K2 kept colocation and attacked the long tail instead: partial rollout pauses unfinished long trajectories and resumes them in the next iteration, so one slow sample does not hold the batch. With long reasoning outputs the tail sets the step time in a synchronous system, which is why partial rollout and async actors exist.

Mechanically, staleness is a bound you enforce, not a property you hope for. Actors tag each sample with the weight version that produced it; the learner admits samples within the bound and drops or down-weights older ones; the importance weight then covers both the version gap and the numerics gap, because π_old in the ratio is now the engine's log-probability under an older version. Log the staleness histogram per step with the log-probability difference.

## When this is wrong

The step-time arithmetic treats generation and training as fixed. With long reasoning outputs, generation time is set by the slowest samples, so averages mislead; measure the distribution of per-sample generation time before choosing a staleness bound.

Importance sampling corrects a distribution gap only where the engine put some probability. Tokens the engine never samples cannot be reweighted into the batch, and a large mismatch shows up as many capped or masked tokens. When the share of capped tokens grows, the correction is hiding a problem the kernels or the engine version should fix.

The published systems report designs, not ablations against each other. Kimi K2's 30-second update is for its cluster and sharding; prime-rl's "a few steps" is not a number. Choose a bound from your own log-probability gap and reward curves, not from theirs.
