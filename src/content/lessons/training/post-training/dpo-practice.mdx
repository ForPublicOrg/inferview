## What DPO removes

PPO-based RLHF trains a reward model, then runs RL: sample from the policy, score with the reward model, estimate advantages with a value model, update, repeat, with a KL penalty keeping the policy near a reference. DPO keeps the objective and removes the loop. It needs a frozen reference model and a fixed dataset of preference pairs. It needs no separate reward model, no value model and no sampling during training. The training step is a forward pass of the policy and the reference over the chosen and rejected responses, and a classification-style loss.

The derivation is short enough to carry into a design review. The RLHF objective is to maximise expected reward minus β times the KL divergence from the reference. Its optimum has a closed form: π*(y|x) ∝ π_ref(y|x) · exp(r(x, y)/β). Solve for the reward: r(x, y) = β log(π*(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) + β log Z(x), where Z(x) is the normalising constant. Substitute that into the Bradley-Terry likelihood of a preference pair. The pair loss depends only on the reward difference, so β log Z(x) cancels, and what remains is a loss on the policy alone:

L = −log σ(β [ (log π(y_w) − log π_ref(y_w)) − (log π(y_l) − log π_ref(y_l)) ])

with y_w the chosen and y_l the rejected response. The term β log(π/π_ref) is the implicit reward. The gradient is

∇L = −β σ(r̂_l − r̂_w) [∇ log π(y_w) − ∇ log π(y_l)]

where r̂ is the implicit reward. A pair the policy already ranks correctly by a wide implicit margin gets a weight near zero; a pair it ranks wrongly gets a weight near β. The [preference-learning lab](/labs/preference-learning) has you derive this gradient for a small policy and check it against finite differences.

## What β does

β appears twice: it scales the implicit reward, and it is the strength of the KL constraint in the objective DPO was derived from. A smaller β lets the policy move further from the reference for the same margin. Label disagreement makes this concrete. Suppose a fraction p of the labels prefer A to B and the rest prefer B to A. The expected loss is p·softplus(−m) + (1 − p)·softplus(m), where m is the margin; setting its derivative to zero gives σ(m) = p, so m = log(p / (1 − p)). The policy's log-ratio difference settles at m/β. For p = 0.75 the margin is log 3 = 1.10; at β = 0.1 the policy's log-ratio difference between A and B settles at 11.0 nats, and at β = 0.5 at 2.2.

With unanimous labels (p = 1) there is no finite optimum: the loss keeps falling as the margin grows, and only the number of steps and the learning rate stop the policy. That is the practical reason to watch KL from the reference during a DPO run even though the loss has a KL term built in.

## Building the dataset

A DPO example is a prompt and two responses with a label. What to store with it:

- the prompt as the model will see it, rendered through the chat template with system prompt and prior turns, since the loss is computed on token log-probs under that rendering;
- chosen and rejected responses, and the model and sampling settings that produced each;
- the label strength and who gave it (annotator, judge model and version);
- the reference model's log-probs for both responses, computed once and cached, which removes the reference forward pass from training.

**Sources of chosen and rejected.** Llama 3 sampled two responses per prompt from two different models and had annotators rate the preference in four strengths: significantly better, better, slightly better, marginally better. Annotators could also edit the preferred response, giving a third, best response. Pairs from different models differ in style as well as quality, and DPO learns any difference that predicts the label, including the style of the model that tended to win.

**Ties and weak labels.** DPO has no tie term. A tie labelled at random contributes a pull in a random direction, and a weak preference contributes a pull as strong as a clear one. Llama 3 trained only on pairs rated significantly better or better and discarded pairs with similar responses. That discards data to buy label quality, and it is the cheaper fix compared with modelling label confidence.

**Noisy labels.** The derivation above says disagreement on the same pair caps the margin rather than corrupting the gradient direction, which is benign. Systematic noise is not: if an annotator pool prefers longer answers, DPO learns length. Measure the length difference between chosen and rejected across the set before training; if the chosen side is longer on average, either rebalance or report length-controlled results.

**Offline versus on-policy pairs.** DPO trains on whatever pairs it is given, including pairs the current policy would never generate. Llama 3 used mostly the most recent preference batches, collected from its best models, so that the data matched the policy being optimised, and repeated the cycle over six rounds. A one-shot DPO run on an old public preference set is offline in the strongest sense: the gradient pushes on responses far from what the model produces, and the implicit reward generalises poorly to its own outputs.

## Settings and the two fixes Llama 3 published

Llama 3 used DPO rather than PPO because, in its experiments, DPO needed less compute for large models and did better, especially on instruction-following benchmarks such as IFEval. The settings: learning rate 10⁻⁵ and β = 0.1. Two changes to the plain loss:

1. **Mask formatting tokens.** Header and termination tokens appear in both chosen and rejected responses, so the loss asks the model to raise and lower their probability at once. The report says that including them can lead to behaviours such as tail repetition, so they are excluded from the loss on both sides.
2. **Add an NLL term on the chosen response,** with coefficient 0.2, to keep formatting and to stop the chosen response's log-probability from falling.

The second fix answers a behaviour that surprises people the first time they plot it. DPO only constrains the difference between the chosen and rejected log-ratios. Both can fall, as long as the rejected one falls faster, and the probability mass has to go somewhere. Razin et al. call this likelihood displacement and show it can move mass to responses with the opposite meaning: in their refusal experiment, Llama-3-8B-Instruct's refusal rate fell from 74.4% to 33.4% after training on pairs meant to reinforce refusals. They trace it to pairs whose chosen and rejected responses have similar hidden representations, and filtering those pairs, by a similarity score they define, mitigated it. Step 5 of the lab builds a three-response case where plain DPO moves almost all probability to the response neither label mentions, and the NLL term reverses it.

```python
import math

def dpo_pair_loss(pc, pr, rc, rr, beta=0.1, nll_coef=0.2, n_chosen_tokens=1):
    """DPO loss for one pair from summed log-probs, plus Llama 3's NLL term on the chosen response."""
    m = beta * ((pc - rc) - (pr - rr))
    dpo = max(-m, 0.0) + math.log1p(math.exp(-abs(m)))     # softplus(-m), finite for any m
    return dpo + nll_coef * (-pc / n_chosen_tokens)

assert abs(dpo_pair_loss(-12.0, -15.0, -12.0, -15.0, nll_coef=0.0) - math.log(2)) < 1e-12
```

Llama 3 does not say in the DPO section whether its NLL term is normalised by length; the per-token form above is one choice, and the scale it sets against the DPO term is a hyperparameter to sweep, not a constant to copy.

## What to monitor during a run

Plot the chosen and rejected log-probs separately, not only the margin; the reward accuracy on held-out pairs; KL from the reference on held-out prompts, sampled from the policy; mean response length; and the rate of a behaviour the data was meant to strengthen (refusals on a safety set, schema-valid outputs). A run where margin and accuracy rise while the chosen log-prob falls is not broken by that fact alone. It is broken if the held-out behaviour moves the wrong way.

## When this is wrong

DPO's derivation assumes the Bradley-Terry model of preferences and a policy class that can represent the optimum. Neither holds exactly, and the guarantee that DPO solves the same problem as RLHF holds at the optimum, not along the path a finite run takes.

The Llama 3 settings are for its models and data; β and the NLL coefficient interact with the learning rate and the pair distribution. Copy them as a starting point and sweep.

The 74.4% to 33.4% refusal drop is from Razin et al.'s experiment designed to provoke displacement, not a measurement of production DPO runs. It shows the mechanism can be large, not how often it is.
