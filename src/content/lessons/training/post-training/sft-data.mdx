## The decision

Supervised fine-tuning sets the format, behaviours and response style that the preference and RL stages start from. The data decision has three parts: where each response comes from (people, a stronger model, or the model being trained), what is thrown away before training, and how samples are laid out in a batch. DeepSeek and Meta answer all three differently in their reports, and both treat the data pipeline as the work, with the training run as a short final step.

## Where responses come from

**Expert generators.** DeepSeek-V3 did not write its reasoning SFT data by hand. For each domain (code, maths, general reasoning) it trained an expert model with its own SFT and RL pipeline and used that expert as the data generator. Each prompt produced two kinds of sample: the problem with its original response, and the problem with a response from an internal DeepSeek-R1 model plus a system prompt asking for reflection and verification. During the expert's RL phase, high-temperature sampling let it blend the two styles, and rejection sampling chose what went into the final set. Non-reasoning data (writing, role-play, simple QA) came from DeepSeek-V2.5, with human annotators checking accuracy. The final set was 1.5M instances.

The reason for the expert step is in the same section: raw R1 output "suffers from issues such as overthinking, poor formatting, and excessive length." Table 9 of the V3 report shows the cost of distilling it directly. Adding R1 data to DeepSeek-V2.5 took MATH-500 pass@1 from 74.6 to 83.2 and average response length from 769 to 1,510 tokens: 8.6 points of accuracy for 1,510 / 769 = 1.96 times the tokens. Whether that trade is acceptable depends on what the product pays per token, which is why DeepSeek put a generator between R1 and the training set rather than copying R1's outputs.

**Rejection sampling from the model in training.** Llama 3 ran six rounds. In each round it trained a reward model on the latest preference data, sampled K responses per prompt from the best current checkpoint (K typically 10 to 30), kept the response the reward model scored highest, trained SFT on the result, and then ran DPO. The SFT data therefore drifts towards responses the reward model likes. That is the purpose, and also the risk: a reward model that likes length or hedging selects for length and hedging, round after round. The next lesson covers what reward models reward.

**Filters before the model sees anything.** DeepSeek-R1's SFT set (about 600k reasoning samples and about 200k non-reasoning samples, two epochs on DeepSeek-V3-Base) dropped chains of thought that mixed languages, ran to long paragraphs or contained code blocks. These are format filters, cheap to run and easy to audit. Llama 3's cleaning went further. It removed overused phrases such as "I'm sorry" and "I apologize", classified every example by topic with a fine-tuned Llama 3 8B, scored quality with the reward model (top quartile counted as high quality) and a Llama-based rater, scored difficulty with Instag tags from Llama 3 70B, and ran semantic deduplication: cluster with RoBERTa embeddings, sort each cluster by quality times difficulty, and keep an example only if its cosine similarity to everything already kept is below a threshold.

## The cost of rejection sampling, worked

Rejection sampling is an inference job, and it is usually the largest compute line in an SFT pipeline. As a scenario: 200,000 prompts, K = 20, and an average of 800 generated tokens per sample. That is 200,000 × 20 × 800 = 3.2 billion generated tokens to produce 200,000 kept samples of about 160 million tokens. Nineteen of every twenty generated tokens are discarded by design. Llama 3 reports that PagedAttention raised its rejection-sampling throughput by over 2 times, which is why the serving stack matters to a data pipeline. Halving K halves the cost and lowers the expected quality of the best sample; the measurement to make before the full run is the reward score of the kept sample as a function of K on a few hundred prompts, and the K where that curve flattens.

## Packing and isolation

Short samples waste a fixed-length batch if each sits in its own padded row, so trainers pack several samples into one row. Without an attention mask, the third sample in a row attends to the first two, which teaches the model to condition on unrelated text and leaks one example's answer into another's context. DeepSeek-V3 packs and uses "a sample masking strategy" so that packed examples stay "isolated and mutually invisible": the causal mask becomes block-diagonal. The loss mask is a separate decision. Prompt tokens are usually excluded from the loss so the model is trained on responses only.

## The training run is short

The published settings are small next to the data work. DeepSeek-V3: two epochs, with the learning rate on a cosine decay from 5 × 10⁻⁶ to 1 × 10⁻⁶. DeepSeek-R1: two epochs over about 800k samples. Llama 3: learning rate 10⁻⁵ for 8.5K to 9K steps on the largest models, repeated each round, with models from different data versions and hyperparameters averaged at each stage. Two epochs means every example is seen twice, so a duplicated or wrong example is seen four times or more; deduplication and verification pay off directly in what the model memorises.

## A pipeline for one capability

As an example, a team adds structured extraction from invoices to a general assistant. A pipeline in the shape of the published recipes:

```python
def build_sft(prompts, generate, score, verify, k=8, keep_top=1):
    """Rejection-sample k responses per prompt and keep the best verified ones; dedupe afterwards."""
    kept, gaps = [], []
    for p in prompts:
        cands = [generate(p, temperature=0.8) for _ in range(k)]
        ok = [c for c in cands if verify(p, c)]
        if not ok:
            gaps.append(p)          # no sample passed: send to annotators
            continue
        ok.sort(key=lambda c: score(p, c), reverse=True)
        kept.extend((p, c) for c in ok[:keep_top])
    return kept, gaps
```

The parts that carry the risk sit outside the loop. The prompt set needs the layouts and languages the product sees, sampled from logs with personal data removed. The verifier is a rule: the output parses against the schema and every extracted value appears in the source document. A rule cannot be talked into a higher score. The scorer, a reward model or a judge, ranks verified candidates for completeness and style and never overrides the verifier. The prompts in `gaps` are the examples worth paying annotators for, because they are where the current model fails. A held-out set built before any of this, from different documents, measures whether the capability improved and whether general behaviour regressed.

The mixing ratio is the decision no published recipe makes for you. Too little extraction data and the capability does not take; too much and the assistant answers ordinary questions in JSON. Train two or three ratios on a small model, measure the extraction set and a general regression set, and pick the ratio where extraction stops improving faster than the regression set degrades.

<Fragment slot="long">
<p><strong>Sources.</strong> Prompts from logs, deduplicated semantically. For each prompt, K samples from the stronger model with a system prompt asking for a minimal fix, and K from the current model. Executing the code against tests is the verifier; a judge ranks the passing candidates.</p>
<p><strong>Selection.</strong> Among passing candidates, keep the shortest one within a small score margin of the best, so that length is a tiebreak rather than a target the judge can be gamed on. Filter chains of thought with mixed languages or long prose blocks, as R1 did.</p>
<p><strong>Human budget.</strong> Prompts where nothing passes are the coverage gaps, and annotators write those. A slice goes into a held-out set nobody trains on.</p>
<p><strong>Conflict.</strong> Distillation raises pass rate and length together. Measure both on the held-out set at two mixing ratios before the full run. What you give up: some of the stronger model's accuracy on hard problems, in exchange for answers the product team will ship. The failure mode of this design is that the length tiebreak quietly favours responses that skip the explanation a user needed on hard fixes; the held-out set needs hard prompts graded by a person, not only by tests.</p>

## When this is wrong

These recipes come from labs with strong generators. If no model better than yours exists for the capability, rejection sampling from your own model selects the best of what it already does and cannot teach behaviour it never produces; human-written data or a verifier-driven RL stage is then the only source of new behaviour.

Reward-model selection makes the SFT data inherit the reward model's biases. If the reward model prefers length, six rounds of best-of-K make each round longer, and the drift does not show in a loss curve. Track length, refusal rate and phrase frequencies per round.

Packing isolation is a correctness property. A training stack that ignores the sample boundaries trains on cross-example attention without any error. Test it by packing two samples and checking that the loss on the second is unchanged when the first is replaced.

The DeepSeek and Llama numbers are the labs' own, measured on their benchmarks and settings, and neither lab publishes its full data mix.
