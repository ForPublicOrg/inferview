## What it is and who runs it

OLMo 2 and Olmo 3 are the Allen Institute for AI's open language models, and their distinguishing property is not the weights but the release around them. OLMo 2 (7B, 13B and 32B dense models; report revised October 2025) shipped the weights, the full training data, training code, recipes, logs and intermediate checkpoints. Olmo 3 (7B and 32B; report December 2025, revised April 2026) calls the same practice releasing the "model flow": every stage, checkpoint, dataset and dependency.

That makes these reports the reference for this module. The data decisions other reports summarise in a sentence (how the mid-training mix was chosen, how many anneals were averaged, what the microanneals found) are published here with tables. The results below are AI2's own measurements.

**Stability changes.** OLMo 2 is mostly a stability project. Against the previous OLMo, it replaces non-parametric layer norm with RMSNorm and applies the norm to the outputs of the attention and MLP blocks rather than their inputs, adds QK-norm (RMSNorm on queries and keys before attention), adds a z-loss penalty on the softmax normaliser, removes weight decay from embeddings, initialises every parameter from a normal distribution with standard deviation 0.02, lowers AdamW's epsilon from 10<sup>-5</sup> to 10<sup>-8</sup>, and raises the RoPE base from 10,000 to 500,000. A data change belongs here too: documents with 32 or more repeated n-grams of 1 to 13 tokens are removed.

**Pretraining mix.** OLMo 2 Mix 1124 is about 3.9T tokens, over 95% derived from the web. The 7B trains on 3.90T tokens of it, the 13B on 5T and the 32B on 6.06T, with peak learning rates of 3.0 × 10<sup>-4</sup>, 9.0 × 10<sup>-4</sup> and 6.0 × 10<sup>-4</sup>.

**Dolmino mid-training mix.** Mid-training takes 5% to 10% of training FLOPs with the learning rate decayed linearly to zero. Its pool, Dolmino Mix 1124, has about 832.6B tokens of high-quality general data (DCLM-Baseline's top 7% by fastText score, FineWeb-Edu-filtered web, decontaminated FLAN, peS2o, Wikipedia and Wikibooks, Stack Exchange) and about 10.7B tokens of math (TuluMath, Dolmino SynthMath, TinyGSM-MIND, MathCoder2 synthetic books, filtered Metamath and CodeSearchNet, and GSM8K's training split). Anneals draw 50B, 100B or 300B tokens from it. For the 7B, mid-training took GSM8K from 24.1 to 67.5 and MMLU from 59.8 to 63.7.

**Microanneals.** The proportions inside the anneal came from 19 small anneals totalling 130B tokens, mostly 50/50 mixes of a candidate math source and DCLM web text, with the proportion varied in one experiment. They showed that a math source helps at 35% of the anneal and nearly as much at 10%, that seeing a source twice beat once (GSM\* 66.0 against 63.5) and four times did not add more (65.0), and that format decides value: TinyGSM with inline code scored 25.0, below the 28.5 baseline, while a MIND rewrite of it scored 65.5. The [ablations lesson](/learn/training/pretraining-data/ablations) uses this table as its worked example.

**Souped anneals.** The base model is a weight average of several anneals on the same mix with different data orders: three 50B-token runs for the 7B, three 100B-token runs and one 300B-token run for the 13B and 32B. The paper reports the soup matching or beating the best single anneal in most of the six mixes it tried (for one, OLMES rose from 75.6 to 77.0), with one exception where averaging lost a maths skill one ordering had.

**Tulu 3 post-training and RLVR.** The instruct models follow the Tulu 3 recipe: SFT on 939,104 prompts for the 7B and 13B, DPO on about 367K prompts for the 7B with GPT-4o as the judge over responses from a pool of 20 models, then reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. RLVR uses PPO with the value function initialised from the reward model for the 7B and 13B (the 13B runs three RLVR passes, the second on GSM8K and the last on MATH) and GRPO for the 32B. The authors note OLMo 2 needed higher learning rates than the Llama 3.1 version of the recipe.

**Released model flow.** Every box above ships: data, code, configurations, logs and intermediate checkpoints, which is what lets a reader check the numbers in this teardown against the artefacts.

## Olmo 3: the same flow with more stages

Olmo 3 keeps the shape and adds a stage. The base model trains in three: pretraining on Dolma 3 Mix, 5.93T tokens drawn from a 9.31T-token pool, with Common Crawl at 76.1% of the mix and 805B tokens of science PDFs (from a 972B-token pool) converted to text with olmOCR; mid-training on a 100B-token Dolmino Mix drawn from a 2T pool of code, maths, general QA, instruction data and thinking traces; and a long-context stage, Longmino, of 50B tokens for the 7B and 100B for the 32B from a 640B pool, for contexts up to 65K tokens. Souping is used in both the mid-training and long-context stages.

The data-judging method is stated as a two-part loop: lightweight, distributed feedback on individual data sources (the microanneal idea) paired with centralised integration tests of the combined mix, scored on OlmoBaseEval, a 43-task suite with a separate 4-task held-out set. Post-training splits into three products: Think (SFT on long reasoning traces, DPO with delta learning, then RL with OlmoRL, for which the authors report a 4x training speedup), Instruct (shorter replies and function calling), and RL-Zero (RL directly from the base model, with RL data decontaminated against pretraining and mid-training data). Olmo 3.1 Think 32B, after 21 more days of RL on 224 GPUs, reports 96.2 on MATH, 80.6 on AIME 2024 and 78.1 on AIME 2025, and the authors describe it as the strongest fully open thinking model at release.

## How sources are judged

The design choice worth copying is the separation of a cheap per-source test from an expensive whole-mix test. A microanneal answers "does this source help, at what proportion, with how much repetition" for a few billion tokens of compute; the integration test answers "does the mix still work" once. It is the unit-test and integration-test split, with the difference that both tests are statistical and the unit test can pass while the integration fails, because sources interact through the shared token budget.

The second choice is to evaluate the base model on a suite fixed in advance and keep a held-out part of it. Olmo 3's 4-task held-out set is small, but its existence is the defence against the fixed-benchmark problem in the [benchmarks lesson](/learn/training/pretraining-data/benchmarks-wrong-reason).

## Cost

OLMo 2 reports compute of about 1.8, 4.6 and 13.0 × 10<sup>23</sup> FLOPs for the 7B, 13B and 32B. The 6ND rule reproduces the first: 6 × 7 × 10<sup>9</sup> × 4.05 × 10<sup>12</sup> = 1.7 × 10<sup>23</sup> with the nominal parameter count. Pretraining the 7B and 13B used 131 MWh and 257 MWh. Training ran on two clusters: Jupiter, 1,024 H100s, mainly for the 7B, and Augusta, 160 Google Cloud nodes of 8 H100s each, mainly for the 13B. The operational lessons reported are plain ones: health checks that cordon bad hosts with automatic restarts, burn-in that found problems from cabling to NCCL node ordering, and running Python garbage collection on a schedule instead of automatically, which made throughput faster and steadier.

## Failure modes

The paper's own exception on souping is the first. For one of six candidate mixes, the soup scored 43.0 on GSM\* against 60.5 for the best single anneal, while other scores improved. Averaging can erase a skill that one data order acquired; a team that soups blindly ships the regression. Scoring each ingredient before averaging catches it.

The second is benchmark proximity. The math pool contains GSM8K's training split and synthetic grade-school maths, and the headline mid-training gain is on GSM8K. The gain is real; it is also the case where a benchmark moves more than the capability it names, and the held-out evaluation is what separates the two.

The third is a closed dependency inside an open pipeline. DPO preferences are judged by GPT-4o; anyone reproducing the run with released data gets the same preferences, but regenerating them for new prompts depends on a closed model whose behaviour changes over time.

The fourth is reproducing from the text alone. Where the paper's text and tables disagree, a reimplementation from the prose trains a different model than the one released, which matters for anyone using these reports as a reference recipe.

## What is not public

Little. Data, code, configurations, logs and checkpoints are released, so most questions about internals can be answered from artefacts rather than from the papers.

The one inconsistency to state plainly: OLMo 2's section on z-loss gives the coefficient as 10<sup>-4</sup>, while Table 1 lists 10<sup>-5</sup>. The paper does not say which the released runs used; the released training configurations are the place to check, and the value should not be quoted from either location without that check.

The Olmo 3 cost figure is the authors' estimate at an assumed $2 per H100-hour, not a disclosed expense, and the papers do not total the compute spent on ablations and microanneals that preceded the final runs.

<p>Copy the stability changes, which cost nothing at run time, and the microanneal method for setting proportions. Cut the soup to the budget: one 50B anneal instead of three costs a third of OLMo 2's 7B mid-training, or three shorter anneals if a cheap per-ingredient check fits. The defence is OLMo 2's own table: souping matched or beat the best single anneal in most mixes, by 1.4 OLMES points in the example given, and once lost a skill, so it is an improvement to buy when the budget allows, not a requirement.</p>

<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The budget is the binding constraint: OLMo 2's 7B spent 150B tokens of compute on three 50B anneals, so a quarter is about 37B. That rules out the full soup. The options are one 37B anneal, or three 12B anneals souped, which averages but at a scale where each ingredient sees less data. I would run the microanneals first, cheap at a few billion tokens each, and let their noise level decide: if seeds already disagree by more than the soup's published gain, three short anneals buy more than one long one.</p>
<p>I copy the stability changes wholesale and verify the z-loss coefficient against the released configuration, because the paper's text and table disagree. I copy the Dolmino composition only after decontaminating it against our evaluations; it contains GSM8K's training split, and our launch report should not lean on a gain that came from it.</p>
<p>What I give up: the gain souping added in most of OLMo 2's mixes, and a second chance if one anneal ordering goes badly. The rollback condition is a per-ingredient regression on any held-out task beyond seed noise, checked before any average.</p>
