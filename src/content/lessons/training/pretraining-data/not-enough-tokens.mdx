## The decision

The filter stack is tuned and the arithmetic does not close: the run's token budget is larger than what survives the strict filters. A candidate report on PracHub for an NVIDIA product manager loop, posted in June 2026, lists this as the first follow-up to a data-curation question. The options are to relax the filters, repeat the high-quality data, rephrase it, synthesise new data, add sources the crawl does not reach, shrink the run, or move the problem to a teacher model by distilling. They can be combined, and the decision is the blend, with a stated cost and a stated risk for each part.

## The inputs that change it

**The gap and its shape.** A run that needs twice the high-quality tokens it has is a different problem from one that needs ten times. A 2x gap sits inside what repetition alone handles in the published evidence; a 10x gap does not.

**Which domain is short.** Knowledge-heavy text (encyclopaedic facts) responds to rephrasing; verifiable domains (maths, code) can be synthesised and checked; conversational and multilingual text is hardest to synthesise without drifting in register.

**Generation budget relative to training budget.** Rephrasing and synthesis cost inference compute, priced below.

**Teacher availability and licence.** Synthesis and distillation need a model good enough to write the data and terms that allow training on its outputs.

**Tolerance for memorisation.** Repetition raises the memorised share of outputs; for licensed or personal data that limit is set by legal, not by the benchmark.

## The options and when each wins

**Relax the filter.** Lower the classifier threshold or take a larger top fraction. FineWeb-Edu picked a threshold of 3 on its 0 to 5 scale as the best trade-off, keeping 1.3T of 15T tokens; DCLM keeps its top 10%. Relaxing is the cheapest option and wins when the ablation curve is flat near the current threshold, which only an ablation shows. The risk is quiet: the extra tokens are the ones the classifier judged worse, and their cost shows up on the benchmarks the classifier was not tuned for.

**Repeat.** Take more epochs over the high-quality set. In OLMo 2's microanneals a math source seen twice scored 66.0 on GSM\* against 63.5 once, and four times scored 65.0. Kimi K2's raw wiki text over 10 epochs scored 23.76 on SimpleQA, below both rephrasing variants. Lee et al.'s memorisation rate for deduplicated C4 rose from 0.189% after one epoch to 0.264% after two. Repetition wins for small gaps on high-value sources and is free in data engineering; its cost is memorisation and a ceiling, which OLMo 2's microanneals put at about two repetitions for one maths source.

**Rephrase.** Rewrite each document several ways and train on the rewrites. Kimi K2's knowledge pipeline prompts for varied styles and perspectives, rewrites long documents chunk by chunk, and checks each rewrite for fidelity against the source; ten rephrasings seen once scored 28.94 on SimpleQA against 27.39 for one rephrasing seen ten times. OLMo 2's microanneals show format matters as much as volume: TinyGSM with inline code scored 25.0, below the 28.5 baseline, while the MIND-rewritten version scored 65.5 and twice that volume 70.0. Rephrasing wins on knowledge and maths where fidelity can be checked; the Kimi K2 report lists its own open problems as factual accuracy across domains, hallucination, toxicity and scale.

**Synthesise.** Generate new documents from a stronger model. Qwen3 generated textbooks, question-answer pairs, instructions and code with Qwen2.5, Qwen2.5-Math and Qwen2.5-Coder. Nemotron-4 340B is the extreme case for post-training: over 98% of its alignment data was synthetic, against about 20K human-annotated examples, with its own reward model filtering low-scoring dialogues and ranking preference pairs. Synthesis wins where a verifier or a reward model can filter the output; without one, the corpus converges on the generator's habits.

**Add sources.** Reach text the crawl misses. Qwen3 recognised text in large volumes of PDF-like documents with Qwen2.5-VL; Olmo 3 converted science PDFs with olmOCR into a 972B-token pool and used 805B of it. New sources win when they exist in volume and the extraction quality is measurable; the cost is an extraction pipeline per format and a licence review.

**Distil or shrink.** Train a smaller model on fewer tokens, or train on a teacher's outputs, covered in the [distillation lesson](/learn/training/distill-merge/distillation). This changes the product, not the data, and belongs in the memo as the fallback.

## Pricing rephrasing

Generation is priced like inference. Take a rewriter with N parameters that reads each source token once (about 2N FLOPs per token for the forward pass) and writes one output token per source token (another 2N per generated token). Training a model with N parameters costs about 6N FLOPs per token.

Rephrasing: 10 × 100B tokens × (2 + 2) × 7 × 10<sup>9</sup> = 2.8 × 10<sup>22</sup> FLOPs. Training: 1 × 10<sup>12</sup> × 6 × 7 × 10<sup>9</sup> = 4.2 × 10<sup>22</sup>. The ratio is 4N / 6N = 0.67 whatever the sizes, as long as rewriter and student match. Rephrasing with an equal-sized rewriter therefore costs on the order of the training run itself. That is affordable for a targeted source (a knowledge corpus of a few hundred billion tokens) and unaffordable as a way to multiply a whole web corpus.

## A worked decision

Scenario: a 7B model planned for 4T tokens. After the strict filter there are 1.2T high-quality English tokens; relaxing to the top 20% yields another 1.0T; there is a 60B-token encyclopaedic and textbook source; maths is 40B tokens. The team has a 70B teacher with a licence that allows training on its outputs.

The arithmetic first. Filling 4T from 1.2T alone is 3.3 epochs, beyond the range the evidence covers for web text. Adding the relaxed 1.0T makes 2.2T, so about 1.8 epochs on average. The blend: take the relaxed tokens, after a 1B-scale ablation confirms the curve is flat between top 10% and top 20%; repeat the strict 1.2T twice and the relaxed 1.0T once; rephrase the 60B knowledge source ten times (600B tokens) with a 7B rewriter, which by the formula above costs 10 × 60B × 4 × 7 × 10<sup>9</sup> = 1.7 × 10<sup>22</sup> FLOPs, about a tenth of the run's 6 × 7 × 10<sup>9</sup> × 4 × 10<sup>12</sup> = 1.7 × 10<sup>23</sup>; synthesise maths with the 70B teacher, keeping only solutions a checker verifies. That is 2.4T + 1.0T + 0.6T = 4.0T before maths, so the synthetic maths displaces some relaxed tokens.

What it gives up: a share of the corpus is rewritten text in a register the rewriter chose, and rare facts in the knowledge source can be distorted in rephrasing. The memo records both, with fidelity-check pass rates as the leading indicator.

## What to measure once it runs

Fidelity: the share of rephrasings that fail the source check, by domain. Diversity: n-gram and embedding-cluster overlap between rephrasings of the same document, since ten near-identical rewrites are one epoch repeated ten times. Memorisation: verbatim continuation rates on prefixes from the repeated sources. Capability: held-out evaluations from sources not used to build the synthetic data, per domain and per language, because synthetic data written in a benchmark's style can raise that benchmark without the capability. And the trend: if the second repetition of the strict set stops moving the evaluations, stop adding epochs.

## When this is wrong

The evidence for repetition and rephrasing is narrow. Kimi K2's comparison is one table on wiki text and one benchmark, SimpleQA; OLMo 2's is on maths sources inside an anneal. Neither measures repetition of general web text during pretraining, and neither shows where returns to rephrasing stop.

The FLOP pricing assumes the rewriter reads and writes about the same number of tokens and matches the student in size. A rewriter may be larger than the student, prompts add overhead, and decode utilisation is low, so the real GPU-hour cost is higher than the ratio here; measure it on a pilot batch.

Nemotron-4's synthetic share is for alignment data, a far smaller set than a pretraining corpus, filtered by a reward model trained on human labels. It is evidence that synthetic data with a strong filter works for post-training, not that a pretraining corpus can be mostly synthetic.

And the option the playbook lists last can be the right one first. If the high-quality supply supports a smaller model at full quality, training it may beat training the planned size on stretched data, and that comparison belongs in the memo before any generation budget is spent.
