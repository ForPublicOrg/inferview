The data mixture is a schedule, not a constant. Every recent open training report splits pretraining into a long phase on a broad mix and a short phase at the end, where the learning rate decays toward zero and high-quality sources are upweighted. Llama 3 calls the second phase annealing, OLMo 2 calls it mid-training, and Qwen3 calls it stage 2. The decisions a staff engineer owns here are the shape of that schedule: what share of compute goes to the late phase, what goes into it, how its gains are measured, and how far small-model evidence about it can be trusted for the large run.

## The long phase: proportions by domain

Llama 3's final pretraining mix is roughly 50% general knowledge, 25% mathematical and reasoning tokens, 17% code and 8% multilingual. Two mechanisms produced it. A knowledge classifier labels web documents by topic and downsamples categories the web over-represents, such as arts and entertainment. Then scaling-law experiments train several small models on candidate mixes, predict each mix's performance at the large scale, and pick the mix. The proportions are the output of that procedure for Llama 3's sources and benchmarks, not a recipe.

Qwen3 takes the same idea down to the document. A multilingual annotation system labels over 30T tokens by educational value, field, domain and safety, and the mixture is optimised at the instance level with ablations on small proxy models, which the report contrasts with tuning proportions per source or domain. Neither report publishes the procedure in enough detail to reproduce; both show that the mix is chosen by measured small runs, not by argument.

A percentage only means something against source sizes. A mix sets how many tokens each domain contributes; the size of each deduplicated source sets how many times those tokens repeat.

## The late phase: annealing on high-quality data

Llama 3 reports that annealing on a small amount of high-quality code and math data improved its pre-trained 8B model on GSM8K and MATH validation by 24.0% and 6.4%, and that for the 405B model the gains were negligible. The last 40M tokens of the 405B run were annealed at 128K context with high-quality sources upsampled, and the final model is an average of checkpoints from that annealing. The paper does not say whether the 8B percentages are points or relative gains, which matters when comparing with other reports.

OLMo 2 gives the most detail of any public report. Its mid-training uses 5% to 10% of training FLOPs with the learning rate decayed linearly to zero, on the Dolmino Mix 1124: about 832.6B tokens of high-quality general data (the top 7% of DCLM-Baseline by its fastText classifier, FineWeb-Edu-filtered web, decontaminated FLAN, peS2o papers, Wikipedia and Stack Exchange) and about 10.7B tokens of math, much of it synthetic. Anneals draw 50B, 100B or 300B tokens from that pool. For the 7B model, mid-training took GSM8K from 24.1 to 67.5 and MMLU from 59.8 to 63.7.

Qwen3's stage 2 is the same move at a larger share: about 5T tokens after a first stage of over 30T, with more STEM, code, reasoning and synthetic data and an accelerated learning-rate decay, followed by a third stage of hundreds of billions of tokens at 32,768 context.

Why the late phase works is a systems question with a known shape. During the high-learning-rate phase, the most recent data has a large, transient effect on the weights; as the rate decays, updates shrink and the model settles on the distribution it sees last. A late phase is like the final pass of an optimising compiler run with profile data: cheap relative to everything before it, applied where it has the most leverage, and specific to the profile it was given.

## Souping anneals

OLMo 2 does not ship one anneal. It runs several on the same mix with different data orders and averages their weights. The 7B averages three 50B-token anneals; the 13B and 32B average three 100B-token runs and one 300B-token run. In the paper's table of six candidate mixes, the soup matched or beat the best single anneal in most: for Mix A, OLMES rose from 75.6 to 77.0 and GSM\* from 71.0 to 74.0. The exception is instructive: for Mix E the soup's GSM\* was 43.0 against 60.5 for the best single run, though its other scores improved. Averaging can wash out a skill that only one ordering acquired.

Souping costs compute that the model's token count hides. The 7B's total of about 4.05T tokens is 3.90T of pretraining plus three 50B anneals: 4.05 - 3.90 = 0.15T. The 13B's total of about 5.6T is 5T plus 3 × 100B + 300B = 0.6T. The 13B therefore spent 0.6 / 5.6 = 10.7% of its training tokens in mid-training, and every ingredient beyond the first is the price of averaging.

## Rephrasing as a mix decision

When a high-value source is small, raising its weight means repeating it. Kimi K2 reports an alternative on knowledge data: rewrite each document with an LLM in varied styles and perspectives, in chunks, with a fidelity check against the source. On SimpleQA, raw wiki text for 10 epochs scored 23.76, one rephrasing for 10 epochs 27.39, and ten different rephrasings seen once each 28.94. Kimi K2 applies the same idea to math, rewriting documents into a learning-note style and translating non-English material into English. Rephrasing turns an epoch count into a generation cost, and the playbook prices it.

## Planning a mid-training stage

A plan that survives review states five things. The budget, as a share of training FLOPs, with OLMo 2's 5% to 10% and Llama 3's 40M final tokens as the two ends of published practice. The pool and its decontamination, because every high-quality source in a late phase (FLAN, GSM8K train, Stack Exchange) sits next to a benchmark. The proportions inside the anneal, chosen by microanneals on a mid-run checkpoint, which the [ablations lesson](/learn/training/pretraining-data/ablations) covers. The number of anneals to soup and the data-order seeds. And the evaluation: target benchmarks, general benchmarks that must not regress, and held-out perplexity on domains the anneal starves, measured before and after the anneal on the same checkpoint.

## When this is wrong

The late-phase gains are measured on the benchmarks the late-phase data resembles. OLMo 2's GSM8K jump from 24.1 to 67.5 comes from a mix that contains GSM8K's training split and synthetic grade-school maths; that is a legitimate result and also exactly the case where a benchmark moves more than the capability it names. Check gains on held-out problems from a different source before reporting them.

Gains shrink with scale and the curve is not published. Llama 3 gives two points, 8B and 405B; whether a 70B sits nearer one or the other is a guess. Treat small-model anneal gains as an upper bound until measured at the target scale.

The mechanism story is a heuristic. "The model settles on what it sees last" explains why annealing helps and why it can erase skills, but it does not predict how much a given anneal moves a given benchmark, and the published reports do not separate the effect of the learning-rate decay from the effect of the data. A fair comparison runs an anneal on the unchanged mix as a control, and the reports do not publish that control next to every result.

Finally, the published mixes are for general-purpose models. A domain model, a code model or a model for one language starts from different proportions, and the reports' percentages are evidence about their procedure, not about your mix.
