## What it is and who runs it

Llama 3 405B is Meta's dense 405-billion-parameter language model, pre-trained on 15.6T text tokens with 3.8 × 10²⁵ FLOPs, on up to 16K H100 GPUs in Meta's production clusters. The figure is consistent with the usual 6 × parameters × tokens estimate for dense training: 6 × 405 × 10⁹ × 15.6 × 10¹² = 3.79 × 10²⁵. Everything below comes from Meta's paper, "The Llama 3 Herd of Models" (July 2024). It covers hardware, network, parallelism, failure data and the full training recipe of one run in a single document.

Meta moved Llama 3 from its research supercluster to production clusters, and the paper frames the move as a choice for production-grade reliability. The reliability numbers in this teardown are therefore numbers from a fleet that already had production operations around it.

**Compute and scheduler.** Grand Teton servers with eight H100s (700 W TDP, 80 GB HBM3) and two CPUs each, GPUs connected by NVLink inside the server. Jobs are scheduled by MAST, Meta's global training scheduler, which the paper says is optimized, along with the parallelism layout, to keep communication inside pods.

**4D parallelism.** GPUs are grouped in the order [TP, CP, PP, DP], innermost first. Tensor parallelism splits weight matrices and needs the most bandwidth, so it stays inside a server on NVLink. Context parallelism splits the sequence. Pipeline parallelism splits layers into stages. Data parallelism is FSDP and sits outermost because it tolerates latency: weights are prefetched and gradients reduced asynchronously. FSDP here shards optimizer state and gradients, but the paper says model shards are not resharded after the forward pass, which trades memory for avoiding an extra all-gather in the backward pass. The ordering is the same principle as placing chatty services in one rack and batch replication across regions.

**Pipeline schedule.** Existing schedules required the per-GPU batch to be divisible by the number of stages and left the first and last stages imbalanced (the first holds the embedding and warm-up micro-batches, the last computes output and loss). Meta made the number of consecutive micro-batches per stage tunable, removed one transformer layer from each of the first and last stages, used an interleaved schedule with a bubble ratio of (PP − 1)/(V × M) for V stages per rank and M micro-batches, and made point-to-point communication asynchronous.

**Collectives.** NCCLX is Meta's fork of NCCL, tuned for the tens-of-microseconds latency of the outer network layers. It chunks transfers to fit those latencies and gives small control messages priority so they are not stuck behind bulk data in the deep-buffer core switches.

**Network.** RoCE over a three-layer Clos of 24K GPUs, of which up to 16K ran this job. A rack is 16 GPUs in two servers behind one top-of-rack switch; 192 racks form a pod of 3,072 GPUs with full bisection bandwidth; eight pods form the cluster, with 1:7 oversubscription at the aggregation layer. GPUs connect at 400 Gbps. The collective library opens 16 flows between each pair of GPUs and the Enhanced-ECMP protocol hashes on extra RoCE header fields to spread them across paths. With deep-buffer spine switches and that load balancing, the paper reports running without DCQCN congestion control.

**Storage.** Tectonic, Meta's distributed file system, serves 240 PB from 7,500 SSD servers at 2 TB/s sustained and 7 TB/s peak. The paper calls checkpoint writes "highly bursty", saturating the fabric for short periods, with each GPU's checkpointed state between 1 MB and 4 GB.

**Diagnostics.** The PyTorch NCCL flight recorder records every collective into a ring buffer and dumps automatically on watchdog or heartbeat timeout, and NCCLX exposes its internal state to PyTorch so a failing collective can be snapshotted with its finished and pending transfers per rank.

## MFU by stage

| Stage | GPUs | TP | CP | PP | DP | Sequence | Batch per DP group | Tokens per batch | TFLOPs per GPU | BF16 MFU |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-training | 8,192 | 8 | 1 | 16 | 64 | 8,192 | 32 | 16M | 430 | 43% |
| Pre-training | 16,384 | 8 | 1 | 16 | 128 | 8,192 | 16 | 16M | 400 | 41% |
| Long context | 16,384 | 8 | 16 | 16 | 8 | 131,072 | 16 | 16M | 380 | 38% |

The paper explains the drop from 43% to 41%: doubling DP to 128 while keeping 16M tokens per batch halves the batch each DP group processes, which gives the pipeline fewer micro-batches to fill its bubbles. More GPUs at a fixed global batch means less work per GPU per step and a larger share of fixed costs. The long-context configuration trades data parallelism for 16-way context parallelism at 131,072 tokens and pays three more points.

Each stage changes something the infrastructure has to absorb. The batch ramp changes per-GPU work and therefore MFU, the long-context stage changes the parallel layout from DP 128 to CP 16 and DP 8, and post-training rounds turn one long job into a sequence of shorter jobs with their own checkpoints and evaluation gates. A checkpoint format that cannot reshard between layouts would force a conversion step at the long-context boundary.

## Context strategy

The paper delays long sequences to the end of pre-training because self-attention compute grows quadratically with sequence length, and extends context gradually in six stages, moving on only when short-context quality has recovered.

Context parallelism splits each sequence into 2 × CP chunks and gives rank i chunks i and 2 × CP − 1 − i, so every rank holds an early and a late chunk and causal attention work is balanced. Instead of the ring-style overlap of earlier CP implementations, Meta all-gathers keys and values and computes attention for the local queries. The paper gives two reasons: all-gather supports arbitrary masks such as the document mask, and with grouped-query attention the K and V tensors are much smaller than Q, so the exposed all-gather is small next to the attention compute. The price is that the all-gather sits on the critical path, which is acceptable only because GQA shrinks it.

## Tooling and operator control

The paper does not describe permissions in the sense an agent teardown would; the closest equivalent is who and what may intervene in a running job. Automation handled all but three of the interruptions in the 54-day window. Heavier tracing could be switched on live through online configuration changes, without a code release or job restart. Automated maintenance (firmware and kernel upgrades) was allowed to interrupt training, at least once a day. For numerics, the paper says Meta compared training loss across parallelism setups to find numerical issues and moved gradient accumulation across micro-batches, and the FSDP reduce-scatter, to FP32.

## Cost and latency

The paper gives FLOPs, not dollars or wall-clock days. A floor can be computed. At the 16,384-GPU configuration's 400 TFLOPs per GPU, 3.8 × 10²⁵ FLOPs take 3.8 × 10²⁵ / (400 × 10¹² × 3,600) = 26.4M GPU-hours, which is 67 days on 16,384 GPUs with no interruptions. At 90% effective training time it is 74.6 days. The real run mixed configurations (8,192 GPUs at 430 TFLOPs, the long-context stage at 380), so this is an order-of-magnitude check, not the run's duration, which the paper does not publish.

## Failure modes

The failure data is covered in the [failures-at-scale lesson](/learn/training/reliability/failures-at-scale): 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days, dominated by faulty GPUs and HBM3, with a percentage in Table 5 that does not match its count. The paper adds:

- NVLink failures that stall kernels without an error code, so detection relies on timeouts.
- Stragglers that slow thousands of GPUs while looking like slow communication; found by ranking suspicious communications and checking the top few.
- A diurnal 1% to 2% throughput swing attributed to mid-day temperatures affecting GPU frequency scaling.
- Power swings of tens of megawatts when every GPU idles at once, for a checkpoint, a collective, or job start and stop.
- Bursty checkpoint writes that saturate a 2 TB/s storage fabric.

## What is not public

The paper does not publish:

- **Cluster-level failure handling.** How failures are detected, how a bad host is drained and replaced, and how MAST reschedules the job are described only as automation. Restart time is not given.
- **Checkpoint interval and size breakdown.** The interval is not stated. The 1 MB to 4 GB per-GPU range is not broken down; weights plus Adam state spread evenly over 16,384 GPUs come to about 0.35 GB per GPU, and the paper does not explain the larger values.
- **Which Table 5 number is wrong.** The Faulty GPU row gives 148 interruptions and 30.1%, but 148 is 35.3% of 419. The paper's 58.7% GPU share and "about 78%" hardware share inherit the ambiguity.
- **The denominator of the failure data.** The GPU count across the 54-day snapshot is "up to 16K", and the paper does not say which 54 days of pre-training the snapshot covers.
- **Run duration and cost.** Total wall-clock time and the share of time in each Table 4 configuration are not given.

Anything said in an interview about these should be labelled as an estimate or a question, not as Meta's design.

<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Step efficiency: Meta kept 16M tokens per batch when doubling DP, so per-group batch halved and MFU fell two points; that is the paper's explanation. Failures: at the Llama 3 per-GPU rate the mean time between failures is about 371 minutes at 8,192 GPUs and 186 at 16,384. With overhead = δ/τ + (τ/2 + R)/M at τ* = √(2δM), δ = 5 seconds and R = 10 minutes (scenario), the overhead is 4.8% and 8.4%. Combined: 2 × (0.41 × 0.916) / (0.43 × 0.952) = 1.84 times the throughput, 90 / 1.84 = 49 days.</p>
<p>Two defensible responses. Take the GPUs and accept 49 days, which still beats ten weeks if nothing else slips. Or take the GPUs and raise the global batch so per-group work stays constant, which recovers the MFU point but changes training dynamics late in a run whose batch schedule was tuned; the cost is a convergence risk that only shows up in loss curves weeks later. The constraint conflict is deadline against an untested recipe change. I would keep the batch schedule, put engineering time into restart cost, which is the dominant term at 16K (R/M is 5.4 of the 8.4 points), and write down the trigger for adding the batch change: the run projected to miss the date by more than a week at the 30% mark.</p>
<p>Failure mode of the recommendation: the doubled cluster may include newer racks with a higher early failure rate than the Llama 3 average, which makes both the 91.6% and the 49 days optimistic. The first two weeks of interruption logs replace the prior.</p>
