## Two ways a score rises without capability

A benchmark score is a measurement of one model, through one harness and scoring rule, on one fixed set of items, at one date. It stops measuring capability in two ways. The items leak into training data, so the model is partly recalling answers. Or the field optimises against the set, choosing models, prompts and hyperparameters by the score until the score reflects the selection more than the ability. The first is a property of the data pipeline; the second happens even when no item ever leaks.

The systems analogy is a load test whose fixture data is also in the production cache: the numbers are real and they describe the cache. The difference is that a model's cache is its weights, nobody can list its contents, and the operator often has no access to the training data at all.

## What leakage looks like in numbers

Lee et al. measured overlap between training and validation splits of standard language-modelling datasets, using two deduplicators: one that finds repeated substrings of at least 50 tokens, and one that uses MinHash over word 5-grams with a 9,000-hash signature, confirming candidate pairs by edit similarity above 0.8. The share of validation examples with a near-duplicate in the training split was 4.60% for C4, 14.35% for RealNews, 4.92% for LM1B and 0.72% for Wiki-40B. One 61-word sentence appeared 61,036 times in C4's training split and 61 times in its validation split. Transformer-XL's perplexity halved on validation examples with a near-duplicate in its training data, which is the signature of a model scored on items it had seen.

Their related-work section collects the same effect for downstream tasks: Dodge et al. found up to 14.4% of test examples for standard tasks verbatim in C4, and GPT-3's authors, filtering any training document with a 13-gram overlap with an evaluation item, flagged up to 90% of tasks as potentially contaminated. These are corpus statistics from 2021 and earlier. Training corpora have grown since, and each benchmark published since then is a candidate for the next crawl.

The calculation is also the measurement. Split the set into items with and without detected overlap, score both, and report the gap. A model that scores far higher on overlapping items than on clean ones of similar difficulty is recalling.

## HumanEval: written by hand for this reason

Chen et al. wrote HumanEval's 164 problems by hand, with an average of 7.7 unit tests each, because Codex was trained on a large fraction of GitHub, which already contained solutions to many public problem sets. They state that hand-writing is not a guarantee of novelty. They also published the problems and the harness on GitHub, which was necessary for anyone to reproduce the numbers and is also how a benchmark enters the next crawl. Any public benchmark has this lifecycle: novelty at release, then exposure.

The same paper is the standard example that the scoring rule is part of the benchmark. Codex solved 28.8% of the problems with one sample and 70.2% with 100 samples per problem. Anthropic's 2026 guide adds the reverse rule for agents: pass^k, the probability that all k trials succeed, which at 75% per trial is 0.75³ = 42% for three trials. One model, one set, three numbers from 42% to 75% depending on the rule. A vendor gain quoted without the rule and the number of attempts cannot be compared with anything.

## SWE-bench Verified: both failures at once

OpenAI built Verified in 2024 by having three experts each review 1,699 SWE-bench problems and keeping 500. On 2026-02-23 it stopped reporting the benchmark. An audit of the 138 problems o3 did not consistently solve over 64 runs found material issues in 59.4% of them, mostly tests that reject correct fixes. And every frontier model it probed could reproduce the gold patch or verbatim problem details for some tasks; models that had seen a problem were more likely to pass its underspecified tests, because they knew which implementation the tests expected. Flawed items and contamination compound: once the sound items are mostly solved, further gains come from the flawed ones, and the flawed ones reward recall.

OpenAI's recommendations are the operational part: report the public split of SWE-bench Pro, post benchmark data behind a password, and filter training data strictly on canary strings. The [coding-benchmarks lesson](/learn/harness/evaluating-harnesses/coding-benchmarks) covers which claims each coding benchmark still supports.

## What a vendor's benchmark gain proves

A gain proves that this model, with this harness, this scoring rule and this number of attempts, scored higher on this item set at this date. Whether it says anything about your work depends on answers the announcement usually omits:

- Was a contamination analysis run, by what method, and what was the score on items without detected overlap?
- Is there a held-out or private split, or items created after the model's training cutoff, and does the gain hold there?
- What is the scoring rule (pass@1, pass@k, pass^k, best of n) and how many attempts per item?
- Was the harness held fixed between the two models?
- Is the gain larger than the run-to-run spread, given the item count?
- Is the benchmark near its ceiling? Anthropic's guide calls a benchmark saturated when an agent passes all of its solvable tasks; near that point remaining gains come from flawed or memorisable items.

Anthropic's guide also gives a reminder that the measurement apparatus moves the number on its own: Opus 4.5 scored 42% on CORE-Bench until an Anthropic researcher found rigid grading, ambiguous task specifications and tasks that could not be reproduced; after the fixes and a less constrained scaffold it scored 95%. A score is only as good as the grader under it, and a public leaderboard rarely shows its grader's error rate.

What the gain cannot prove is improvement on your private set. That is what the private set is for, and it only works while it stays private.

## Keeping a private set private

Every path by which your golden set can reach a model's training data or your own tuning loop is a leak. The controls, in rough order of how often each path is the real one:

**Your own iteration.** Prompt edits tuned until golden cases pass move the set into the prompt. Keep a held-out slice that nobody iterates against, version it separately and run it only at release decisions.

**Your own fine-tuning.** When production traces feed fine-tuning and the golden set also came from production traces, the same conversations can sit on both sides. Deduplicate the training batch against the full golden set before every run, with near-duplicate matching rather than exact matching; Lee et al.'s numbers above are what exact matching misses.

**Third parties.** Do not commit the set to public repositories, paste it into hosted tools whose data-use terms you have not read, or attach it to bug reports. Store it encrypted or access-controlled, and put a canary string in the files so that a crawled copy can be detected and filtered, which is the mechanism OpenAI asks training pipelines to respect.

**Age.** Items written before a model's training cutoff may have public near-copies even if your file never leaked, because the underlying documents were public. Date-stamp every item, and keep adding items from traffic after the cutoff of each model you evaluate.

The overlap check is short enough to run in CI before each fine-tuning job:

```python
import re

def shingles(text, n=5):
    words = re.findall(r"\w+", text.lower())
    return {tuple(words[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(words) - n + 1)}

def jaccard(a, b):
    return len(a & b) / len(a | b) if a and b else 0.0

def flag_overlap(eval_items, train_docs, threshold=0.5):
    """Return (eval index, train index, score) for every pair above the threshold.
    Exact pairwise Jaccard: fine for thousands of items; MinHash replaces it at corpus scale."""
    train = [shingles(d) for d in train_docs]
    hits = []
    for i, item in enumerate(eval_items):
        s = shingles(item)
        for j, t in enumerate(train):
            score = jaccard(s, t)
            if score >= threshold:
                hits.append((i, j, round(score, 2)))
    return hits

eval_items = [
    "Does the master services agreement renew automatically, and how much notice is needed to stop it?",
    "Which of the three agreements renews first, and on what date?",
]
train_docs = [
    "Q: Does the master services agreement renew automatically, and how much notice is needed to stop it? A: Yes, 90 days.",
    "Of the three agreements, which one comes up for renewal earliest, and when?",
]
print(flag_overlap(eval_items, train_docs))  # [(0, 0, 0.71)]
```

The first golden item is caught inside a question-and-answer wrapper. The second is missed: its paraphrase shares no 5-gram with the original. That is the method's limit and the reason the held-out slice exists. N-gram matching catches copies; only a set the training path never touched protects against paraphrase.

## When this is wrong

Contamination is not always a defect. If your product answers questions about public documentation that is also in the training data, memorised knowledge of that documentation is the capability you are buying, and an eval built from those documents measures it correctly. The problem is narrower: scores used to compare models on tasks where memorisation is not available in production.

Overlap detection gives a lower bound. N-gram methods miss paraphrase, translation and knowledge learned from material around a problem rather than the problem text; in OpenAI's GPT-5.2 example the model's reasoning drew on the project's release notes to name a parameter the tests required. A clean overlap report does not show an item is unseen.

Private sets decay too, more slowly. Every release decision made on the held-out slice is a little selection against it. Over many decisions the held-out slice becomes the tuned slice, and the remedy is the same as for public benchmarks: retire items on a schedule and keep writing new ones from current traffic.
