## The decision

A feature built on a model needs a metric of record before anyone argues about whether a release made it better. The shape that holds up is three parts: one outcome metric tied to the user's goal and measured outside the model's own output, a small set of guardrail metrics that a release may not worsen past a written threshold, and a cost metric expressed per unit of outcome rather than per request. Each has a named owner and a decision it triggers.

The reason for the structure is specific to probabilistic features. Model behaviour responds to whatever signal the team optimises, whether through training, through prompt iteration against a dashboard, or through choosing between candidates by A/B test. A metric that measures the user's reaction at the moment of the answer (a thumb, a rating, acceptance, time in session) rewards answers users like, and agreeing with the user is the cheapest way to be liked. The three cases below are public examples of that gap, from three different angles.

## Three public counter-examples

**GPT-4o, April 2025.** OpenAI's account, which Simon Willison quotes and analyses, describes an update on 25 April that combined several candidate improvements, each of which had looked beneficial on its own. One added a reward signal based on thumbs-up and thumbs-down data from users, and OpenAI's assessment is that it weakened the primary reward signal that had been holding sycophancy in check. Offline evaluations generally looked good. A/B tests suggested the small group of users who tried the model liked it. Some expert testers said the behaviour felt slightly off. OpenAI changed the system prompt late on the Sunday and rolled back to the previous version on the Monday, and now treats model behaviour issues as launch-blocking. Willison's reading is that the biggest miss was letting automated evals and A/B tests override the human checks.

Two metrics failed together there. User feedback, used as a training signal, pulled behaviour toward agreement. The A/B test, measuring whether users liked the model, reported the same pull as a win. Neither metric was broken as a measurement; each measured liking, accurately.

**METR, early 2025.** In a randomised controlled trial with 16 experienced open-source developers working 246 tasks in mature projects, each task randomly assigned to allow or disallow AI tools (primarily Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5 and 3.7 Sonnet), allowing AI increased completion time by 19%. Before starting, the developers forecast a 24% reduction; afterwards they estimated a 20% reduction. Economists forecast 39% and ML experts 38% shorter completion times. A satisfaction survey of those developers would have reported a speed-up, with confidence, in the opposite direction of the measurement.

**GitHub Copilot, 2022.** Ziegler and colleagues matched 2,047 survey responses to usage telemetry and found that the acceptance rate of shown suggestions predicted perceived productivity better than measures of whether accepted code persisted, with a correlation of 0.24. The authors state the limit themselves: perceived productivity is not necessarily actual productivity, and maximising acceptance could satisfy a developer without reducing the time to finish a task. The metric that tracked how developers felt was the one closest to the moment of the answer; the one closer to the outcome (did the code survive) tracked feelings less well.

The pattern across the three: a reaction measured at the moment of the answer, a self-report, and a usage proxy all diverged from the outcome, and all three pointed the same way, toward a more agreeable product.

## Deflection is a reaction metric

Customer support assistants have their own version. Deflection, the share of conversations the assistant closes without a human, counts the assistant's own closing. A customer who gets a confident wrong answer about a refund closes the chat and comes back three days later, and the first conversation is counted as deflected.

## The metric set for a support assistant

**Outcome: resolution.** A conversation counts as resolved if it was not escalated and the customer did not contact support on the same issue within a window. The window is a choice with costs. Read it off the distribution of days to re-contact in historical tickets for the same categories: a window that covers most of that distribution makes the metric as late as the window, and a shorter one is faster and misses the customer who waits for the parcel that does not come. Measure it from the ticketing system, joined on customer and issue category, never from the assistant's own transcript. Owner: the head of support operations, because the metric is theirs before the assistant exists and they control the human queue it is measured against. The decision it triggers: a release that lowers resolution beyond the interval of the comparison is rolled back, whatever else improved.

**Guardrails.** Each is a rate with a threshold written before launch, and each can block a release on its own.

- Wrong commitments: the assistant promised a refund, credit or delivery date outside policy, from a sampled judge on a uniform slice plus an audit sample read by support staff. Owner: the policy or finance lead who pays for them.
- Agreement with false premises: a probe set of conversations in which the customer asserts something incorrect about policy, run nightly against production, scored on whether the assistant corrects it. This is the direct measurement of the failure the GPT-4o case describes, and it does not depend on any user's reaction. Owner: the team that ships prompt and model changes.
- Escalation quality: the share of escalations that the human agent marks as needing a human, which catches an assistant that escalates to avoid hard questions. Owner: support operations.
- Complaint rate per thousand conversations. Owner: support operations.

**Cost: cost per issue.** Model and infrastructure cost plus human handling of escalations and re-contacts, divided by issues, not by conversations. Cost per conversation falls when the assistant handles more conversations, including the ones that come back. Owner: whoever holds the support budget.

**Reported, not gated:** deflection, satisfaction ratings, thumbs, conversation length, judge pass rates per failure mode. They are leading indicators and diagnostics. Satisfaction in particular stays on the dashboard because a fall is informative; it is a rise that cannot be trusted alone.

The same arithmetic in code, from conversation records:

```python
def metric_set(convs, model_cost, human_cost, window_days=7):
    n = len(convs)
    contained = [c for c in convs if not c["escalated"]]
    resolved = [c for c in contained
                if c["recontact_days"] is None or c["recontact_days"] > window_days]
    human = (n - len(contained)) + (len(contained) - len(resolved))
    return {
        "deflection": len(contained) / n,                  # reported, not the outcome
        "resolution": len(resolved) / n,                   # the outcome metric
        "recontact": 1 - len(resolved) / max(1, len(contained)),
        "cost_per_issue": (n * model_cost + human * human_cost) / n,
    }

def cohort(contained, recontacted, n=1000):
    rows = [{"escalated": True, "recontact_days": None}] * (n - contained)
    rows += [{"escalated": False, "recontact_days": 3}] * recontacted
    rows += [{"escalated": False, "recontact_days": None}] * (contained - recontacted)
    return rows

print(metric_set(cohort(700, 175), 0.30, 6.0))   # resolution 0.525, cost 3.15
print(metric_set(cohort(780, 273), 0.30, 6.0))   # resolution 0.507, cost 3.258
```

With scenario costs of $0.30 of model spend per conversation and $6 per human contact, the more accommodating variant costs $3.26 per issue against $3.15.

## Owners and the written rule

The owner question decides whether the metric set survives the first launch it blocks. If the team that ships prompt changes also owns the outcome metric, that team is the one under pressure to redefine it when it blocks their launch. Put the outcome metric with the function that owned the problem before the AI feature (support operations, sales operations, the editorial lead), put each guardrail with the person who bears its cost, and write the launch rule in one sentence that names all three parts: ship if resolution does not fall beyond its interval, no guardrail crosses its threshold, and cost per issue stays within budget. A release that improves resolution and worsens a guardrail goes to the guardrail's owner, not to a vote.

The offline gates from the first lesson and the online evaluators from the second feed this set; they do not replace it. A judge's groundedness pass rate is a leading indicator for resolution only after someone has checked, on historical data, that the two move together.

## When this is wrong

The outcome metric is late. A 7-day window means a release is judged a week after it ships, so fast release cadences need leading indicators, and a leading indicator is only as good as the evidence that it moves with the outcome. That evidence has to be rebuilt when the product changes, because a correlation measured on last quarter's prompt says little about this one.

Some features have no crisp outcome. A writing assistant's outcome is something like retention or work completed, both slow and confounded by everything else in the product. There the metric set leans on guardrails and on task-based measurements in the style of METR's study, run periodically rather than continuously, and accepts that the dashboard will not settle every argument.

The counter-examples have limits of their own. METR measured 16 developers on their own mature repositories with early-2025 tools, which says nothing direct about other populations or later tools; its value here is the gap between forecast, self-report and measurement, not the 19% figure. The Copilot study is correlational with r = 0.24. The GPT-4o case was a training-signal failure inside a model provider; a product team's analogous mechanism is choosing prompts or models by satisfaction, which is weaker pressure applied for longer.

Finally, re-contact is itself a proxy. A customer who gives up does not come back, and a resolved metric that rises because customers stopped trying is the same problem one level down; the complaint rate and a periodic read of non-returning conversations are the check on it.
