## A judge with 90% agreement and no use

Scenario, not a real company: a team builds an LLM judge to flag answers that give unsafe medication advice. They label 100 answers by hand, run the judge on the same 100, and report "90% agreement with our clinicians". The judge ships into the nightly eval. Two months later a clinician reviewing escalations finds a run of bad advice the dashboard never showed.

Here is the arithmetic that makes this possible.

## A classifier written in English

An LLM judge is a binary classifier whose source code is a prompt. You would never ship a fraud classifier on the strength of "it agrees with the analysts 90% of the time"; you would ask for precision and recall on held-out data. A judge deserves the same treatment, and the vocabulary carries over directly.

One convention first, because getting it backwards silently flips every number: in the AI Evals FAQ, **positive means an error is present**. So:

- **TPR** (true positive rate) is the share of real failures the judge catches. The FAQ's example: it flags 8 of 10 real failures, TPR 80%.
- **TNR** (true negative rate) is the share of good outputs the judge passes. Its example: 95 of 100, TNR 95%.

You need both, because a judge can buy one with the other. Saying "fail" to everything gives a perfect TPR and a TNR of zero.

Move the sliders. Start with the judge that always passes, then the useful one: it catches 8 of 10 failures and wrongly fails 6 of 90 good traces. Its raw agreement is 92%, barely above the lazy judge's 90%, but its TPR is 80%, its TNR is 93.3% and its kappa is 0.623, against 0 for the lazy judge.

Two things to notice while you play. Drag "traces that really fail" down to 2 and watch raw agreement climb towards 100% for almost any judge, because there is so little to get wrong. And compare the two pass rates under the sliders: whenever TPR and TNR are imperfect, the judge's reported pass rate differs from the truth, and in which direction depends on which kind of error the judge makes more often.

## Writing the judge

Most of a judge's quality is decided before you measure it.

**One failure mode per judge, one binary verdict.** "Is this answer good?" is not a classifier, it is a mood. "Does the answer recommend a dose without checking the patient's listed allergies?" is. The categories from error analysis become the judges' specifications, and each judge returns pass or fail, for the same reasons as in the first lesson.

**Reasoning before the verdict.** Ask for a short critique first and the verdict last, so the label is conditioned on the reasoning rather than rationalised after it. Keep the critique: when the judge disagrees with a human, the critique tells you whether the prompt or the label is wrong.

**Examples from your expert, with their reasons.** Hamel Husain's write-up of critique shadowing describes the loop: one principal domain expert labels outputs pass or fail and writes a short critique for each; those critiques become the judge's few-shot examples; you iterate on the prompt until the judge agrees with the expert. In the Honeycomb example from that write-up, the judge passed 90% agreement with the expert after three iterations. That is one example, not a target, and the agreement figure deserves the same suspicion as any other until it is split into TPR and TNR.

## Validating it properly

Treat the labelled examples like a machine-learning dataset, because that is what they are. The FAQ suggests splitting them roughly 10 to 20% for training (the pool you draw few-shot examples from), 40 to 45% for development (where you iterate on the prompt) and 40 to 45% as a test set you touch once, and aiming for 30 to 50 examples of each label in both dev and test. If you only have 3 failures in the test set, a TPR of 67% versus 100% is one example, which is noise.

Once you have test-set TPR and TNR, you can do something more useful than report them: correct the pass rate the judge reports on unlabelled production data. If p_obs is the share of production traces the judge passes, the judgy library's estimate of the true pass rate is

`true pass rate = (p_obs + fail recall - 1) / (pass recall + fail recall - 1)`

where fail recall is the judge's TPR in the FAQ's sense and pass recall is its TNR. The judgy README writes the formula with TPR and TNR and does not state which class is positive; the formula only works if pass is the positive class, so its TPR is P(judge pass | human pass), the FAQ's TNR. Check the convention before plugging in numbers. With a judge that passes 95% of good outputs and catches 60% of failures, and a production run where it passes 78.5% of traces, that is (0.785 + 0.6 - 1) / (0.95 + 0.6 - 1) = 0.385 / 0.55 = 0.70. The judge's raw number overstates quality by 8.5 points, because it lets 40% of failures through. Report it with a bootstrap interval that resamples both the labelled test set and the production sample, which judgy does for you. With a test set of a few dozen failures the interval is wide; that is information, not a flaw.

The formula has one precondition: pass recall plus fail recall must exceed 1, meaning the judge beats chance. If it does not, the correction is undefined and the judge is not measuring anything.

## Known biases and how to test for each

Each bias has a cheap test, so run the test rather than arguing about whether your judge has it.

**Position bias.** In the MT-Bench study, when the two answers were swapped, GPT-4 with the default prompt gave a consistent verdict in 65.0% of cases and favoured the first position in 30.0%; a few-shot prompt raised consistency to 77.5%. A later study of 15 judges over more than 150,000 instances found the bias varies by judge and task and is worst when the two answers are close in quality, which is exactly when you most need the judge. Test: run every pairwise comparison in both orders and count a win only if both orders agree; otherwise it is a tie. That doubles the cost and removes the effect from your numbers.

```python
def compare(x, y, judge):
    # judge(first, second) returns "A" if it prefers the first answer, "B" otherwise
    x_wins_shown_first = judge(x, y) == "A"
    x_wins_shown_second = judge(y, x) == "B"
    if x_wins_shown_first and x_wins_shown_second:
        return "x"
    if not x_wins_shown_first and not x_wins_shown_second:
        return "y"
    return "tie (order-dependent)"
```

**Verbosity bias.** Judges tend to prefer longer answers. MT-Bench's "repetitive list" attack, which padded 23 answers with rephrased repeats of their own content, fooled Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 in 91.3% of cases and GPT-4 in 8.7%. AlpacaEval's response was to regress out the length difference: the length-controlled win rate raised its Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena rankings from 0.94 to 0.98. Test: pad or trim a sample of answers without changing their content and see whether verdicts move.

**Self-preference.** A judge may favour outputs from its own model family. MT-Bench measured win-rate boosts of about 10% for GPT-4 and 25% for Claude-v1 judging themselves, but its authors say their data cannot establish the bias. The stronger evidence is Panickssery and colleagues (2024): models can recognise their own generations, and after fine-tuning, self-recognition correlates linearly with self-preference. Test: when you compare two models, judge with a third model, or check whether swapping the judge changes the ranking.

## Pairwise or pointwise?

Pairwise judging ("which of these two is better?") is the natural fit for comparing two prompt versions, and humans often find it easier than absolute grading. It is not automatically more reliable for models. Tripathi and colleagues (COLM 2025) found pairwise preferences flipped in about 35% of cases when distractor features were added, against about 9% for absolute scores. Pointwise binary judges also give you something pairwise cannot: an absolute pass rate you can gate a release on.

My default: pointwise binary judges for each failure mode, run on every candidate, and pairwise comparisons with swapped orders only when the question really is "which of these two do people prefer", such as tone or style.

## When not to use a judge

Use code when the property is mechanical: JSON shape, a date that must match a record, an identifier that must appear in the source. Use a human when the stakes are high and the volume is low, and always for the labels that validate the judge. And do not use a judge to verify facts it cannot see. A judge asked "is this answer correct?" without the reference answer or the source documents is grading plausibility, which is the thing the model being judged is already good at.

<p>Diagnose. Build the confusion matrix on a test set the prompt was never tuned on, with at least a few dozen failures. Compute TPR and TNR with failure as the positive class, and kappa. A 92% agreement with a low TPR means the judge is mostly saying pass.</p>
<p>Find the misses. Read the false negatives and the judge's critiques for them. Usually they cluster: the judge does not know a policy, the criterion is vague, or the failures are subtle variants absent from the examples.</p>
<p>Fix. Split a broad judge into one binary judge per failure mode, give each the context it needs (policy text, reference answer), and add the missed cases' expert critiques as few-shot examples drawn from the training split. Re-measure on dev while iterating and on test once at the end.</p>
<p>Report honestly. Correct the production pass rate for the judge's measured TPR and TNR, with an interval, and say what the judge cannot see. If the misses are high-severity, add a cheap code check or a human review queue for that slice instead of trusting the judge.</p>

## When this is wrong

The classifier picture assumes the human labels are ground truth. They are one expert's judgement, and a second expert may disagree with the first more often than the judge does. When you can afford it, have two people label a shared sample and measure their agreement, which sets a ceiling on what the judge can be expected to reach.

The bias correction assumes the judge's TPR and TNR on the test set hold in production. If production traffic contains a failure mode the test set does not, the judge's TPR on it is unknown and the corrected number is wrong. Re-validate after every error-analysis cycle that finds a new category, and after every change to the judge's model or prompt.

The often-quoted result that strong judges agree with human preferences over 80% of the time comes from MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, on pairwise preferences, where agreement between humans was at the same level. It is not an accuracy figure for your judge on your failure modes.

The bias numbers in this lesson come from specific models in 2023 to 2025 studies. Your judge model is probably newer. Treat the numbers as reasons to test, not as measurements of your judge.
