## The decision

Error analysis ends with a list of failure modes. Each one needs a check, and there are three kinds: code (an assertion over the output, the trace or system state), a judge (a model applying a rubric), and a person. The decision is made per failure mode, not per product, and the rule is that the cheapest check whose error profile catches the failure at the precision the failure's cost demands wins.

The two sources that write this down agree on the order. Anthropic's agent-evals guide: deterministic graders where possible, LLM graders where necessary, human graders judiciously. The LLM Evals FAQ: code when an objective rule can identify the failure, a judge when the failure requires human judgment. Both are a preference order with a condition attached, and the condition is where teams go wrong in both directions: a judge for something a regex decides, or a regex for something that needs judgement and passes every valid variation it was not written for.

## The inputs that change it

**Can a rule decide it?** The question is whether the failure is a function of data the check can read: tool-call arguments, a JSON field, a database row, the presence of a required step. If it is, code decides it every time at no marginal cost. If deciding needs reading comprehension, a rule either misses paraphrases or flags valid answers.

**What does a label cost, and how many failures exist to label?** A judge is a classifier, so it needs labelled failures before it can be trusted, and the FAQ puts the bill at 100 or more labelled examples per judge plus ongoing weekly maintenance. The binding number is usually the count of real failures, not traces: a rare failure mode may not produce enough examples to validate a judge in any useful time.

**Volume.** A human's cost scales with every item; a judge's cost is mostly fixed (labels, re-validation) plus a small per-call cost; code's cost is fixed. Volume decides between human and judge once both could do the job.

**Required precision, by error direction.** A missed failure and a false alarm cost different things. A false alarm in an offline eval costs a person's time to read it; a missed account-security failure costs an incident. The check's error profile has to match the expensive direction.

**Latency and placement.** An inline guard runs on the request path. The FAQ says a slow or non-deterministic judge is almost never a synchronous guardrail. Code can be inline; a judge usually runs after the fact on a sample.

## The options and when each wins

**Code check.** Wins when a rule decides the failure from structured data. Its error profile is near zero on what the rule covers and blind outside it, and Anthropic's guide names its weakness as brittleness to valid variations. Their own example: Opus 4.5 scored 42% on CORE-Bench until a researcher found, among other problems, grading that rejected "96.12" when it expected "96.124991"; after fixes to the grading, the task specs and the stochastic tasks, and with a less constrained scaffold, it scored 95%. A code check on free text is a judge with no validation.

**Judge.** Wins when the failure needs judgement, volume is high enough to amortise the labels, and enough failures exist to measure *s* and *f*. Its error profile is the measured pair plus the biases from the previous lesson. Give it the reference it needs: MT-Bench's judges failed 14 of 20 math gradings without a reference solution and 3 of 20 with one. Anthropic recommends one isolated judge per dimension and an explicit "Unknown" answer when the judge lacks the information.

**Human.** Wins for rare, severe failures where a judge could not collect enough labelled failures, for low volume, and always for the labels that validate the judge. Its cost is per item and its error profile includes fatigue and disagreement between reviewers.

**Combinations.** A code check that routes to a person, or a judge that sends its "Unknown" and borderline verdicts to a person, is a cascade. A cascade's recall is the product of its stages' recalls, and the first stage's misses never reach the second.

## Worked decision

A scenario, with rates invented to make the arithmetic concrete. A support bot handles refunds and account questions, 20,000 conversations a week. Its tools follow the shape of the support example in Anthropic's guide: `fetch_policy`, `verify_identity`, `process_refund` with refunds above $100 requiring approval, and `escalate_security`. Error analysis over a random sample and a month of escalations produced three failure modes.

**1. A refund over $100 is processed without approval.** Seen in about 0.5% of conversations. A rule decides it from the trace: sum `process_refund` amounts per conversation and assert an approval call exists when the sum exceeds 100. Summing per conversation matters, because two $60 refunds pass a per-call check. It runs on every conversation and, as the same predicate, inline before `process_refund` executes. The choice is code. The rule's constant is coupled to a policy number, so the policy change that moves the limit has to change the test too.

**2. The bot states a policy that the fetched policy text does not support.** Seen in about 4% of conversations. No rule decides it: the bot paraphrases, and a correct paraphrase and a wrong one share their keywords. The choice is a judge, reference-guided, given only the user's question, the `fetch_policy` result and the bot's answer, returning pass, fail or Unknown. Labelling is the cost. At 4% prevalence, a random sample needs about 2,500 conversations to hold 100 failures for dev and test, so the candidates are drawn from a signal independent of the judge, such as conversations reopened by the customer, plus a random slice. Sizing the production sample: to hold the corrected pass rate within ±1 point at 95% with *s* + *f* − 1 = 0.8 and p_obs near 0.95, the observed rate needs a half-width of 0.8 points, which takes 1.96² × 0.95 × 0.05 / 0.008² ≈ 2,850 judged conversations a week, about one in seven.

**3. An account-takeover report is handled without handing off to security.** About 0.2% of conversations are takeover reports, 40 a week. Suppose one in ten is mishandled: four failures a week. Collecting 50 labelled failures for a judge's test set would take 50 / 4 = 12.5 weeks, and the judge would then be validated on a moving target. Forty conversations a week at five minutes each is 200 minutes of a reviewer's week. The choice is a person, reviewing every takeover report, with a code check routing them: a keyword and intent rule, plus every conversation where the bot called `escalate_security`. The router's misses are invisible to the reviewer, so a random weekly sample of unrouted conversations measures the router's recall.

Before committing to the judge for mode 2, the team checks whether the labels pay for themselves against reviewing the sample by hand.

What the team gives up: mode 2 is measured on a sample with an interval, not checked on every conversation, so an individual bad answer is found only if it is sampled or reported. Mode 3 depends on a router whose recall is measured weekly rather than known. Both statements go in the eval plan.

## What to measure after deciding

**Code checks.** The false-alarm rate, from a person reading a sample of flagged traces each week; a rising rate means valid behaviour the rule did not anticipate. A test that fails when the policy constant changes without the rule.

**Judges.** *s* and *f* on fresh labels each cycle, per failure type; the corrected pass rate with its interval; the Unknown rate, which rises when the judge is missing context it needs; cost and latency per call. The FAQ's advice to phase out expensive evals more aggressively than cheap ones applies: a judge whose failure mode has not fired in months is a candidate for a cheaper check or retirement.

**People.** Queue age, agreement between two reviewers on a shared sample, and the recall of whatever routes work to them.

**Escapes.** Defects found through channels outside the checks: complaints, chargebacks, security incidents. Each escape is attributed to a failure mode. An escape in a mode with a check is a check failure and goes to that check's owner; an escape in a mode without one is new input to error analysis. The escape rate is the only measure of what all three checks miss together.

**Re-decision triggers.** A failure mode becomes decidable by a rule (the bot starts emitting a structured `policy_id`, and mode 2 partly turns into a code check); volume crosses the break-even; a judge's *f* falls below what the failure's cost tolerates.

## When this is wrong

The worked costs are scenario values. Label time differs between a tone judgement and a legal one, and judge cost per call depends on the model and the context it reads, so the break-even has to be recomputed with real numbers. In this scenario it moves with label time far more than with model price, because a judge call costs a thousandth of a hand review.

The three-way split assumes the human is the reference. Human reviewers drift and disagree, and a reviewer's error rate at 3,000 conversations a week is not the one measured at 300. Measure reviewer agreement the way you measure a judge's.

Cascades hide correlated misses. When a code check routes cases to a person, the person's apparent recall is perfect on what they see and unknown on what the router dropped; the same holds for a judge that only escalates its "Unknown" verdicts. Every cascade needs a random sample that bypasses the first stage.

A failure mode is not fixed to one check type for good. Product changes move failures between columns: a structured output field can turn a judgement failure into a rule, and a new free-text feature can turn a rule-checked one into a judgement.
