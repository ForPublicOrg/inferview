## A bias is a feature the verdict should ignore

A judge's verdict should depend on the property the rubric names. A bias is any other feature it depends on: which answer came first, how long an answer is, which model wrote it, how confident it sounds. Each one is measurable the same way as a flaky test that depends on execution order: hold the thing under test fixed, change one irrelevant input, and count how often the result changes. For a judge the fixed thing is answer quality, so every bias test is a controlled pair, two inputs a human would grade the same that differ in one feature.

The previous lesson treated the judge's error rates *s* and *f* as properties of the judge. Biases are why those rates differ across slices of traffic: a judge with a length bias has a different *f* on long answers than on short ones, and an aggregate *f* hides it. The tests below find the slices.

## Position

MT-Bench's authors ran pairwise comparisons twice with the two answers swapped. With the default prompt, GPT-4 gave the same verdict in both orders 65.0% of the time and favoured whichever answer came first in 30.0%. GPT-3.5 was consistent in 46.2% and favoured the first position in 50.0%; Claude-v1 was consistent in 23.8% and favoured the first position in 75.0%. A few-shot prompt raised GPT-4's consistency to 77.5%, and the authors add that higher consistency may not mean higher accuracy.

Shi and colleagues later measured 15 judges on 22 tasks with over 150,000 instances. The bias varied by judge and task, and the strongest factor was the quality gap between the two candidates: the closer they are, the more often the order decides. Prompt length had a weak effect. Two versions of the same prompt are close candidates by construction, so a release comparison sits where position bias is worst.

The control is the one MT-Bench used: judge both orders and count a win only when both agree, otherwise a tie. It doubles the judge calls.

## Verbosity and other distractors

MT-Bench tested length with a "repetitive list" attack: 23 answers containing a numbered list had GPT-4 rephrase the list without adding information, and the rephrased list was inserted before the original. The judge failed if it preferred the padded answer. Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 failed in 91.3% of cases, 21 of 23; GPT-4 in 8.7%, 2 of 23. Twenty-three items is a demonstration, not an estimate.

AlpacaEval's response to length bias was statistical rather than experimental. Dubois and colleagues fit a GLM that predicts the judge's preference from the length difference and other features, then predict with the length difference set to zero. Length control raised the leaderboard's Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98. The adjustment works on aggregate rankings across many comparisons; it does not tell you whether one verdict was decided by length.

Tripathi and colleagues (COLM 2025) generalised the attack. They took pairs where the judge had a preference, added a distractor to the dispreferred response (assertiveness, prolixity or sycophancy), and counted flips. Pairwise preferences flipped in about 35% of cases. When the same responses were scored on their own, about 9% of absolute scores flipped. The judges were Llama 3.2 3B, Llama 3.3 70B and Qwen2.5 3B and 72B, on IFEval-TweakSet (400 samples) and 1,689 MT-Bench responses.

The mechanism is plausible from the protocol itself. A pairwise judge answers "which is better", so any difference between the two answers is evidence, including an irrelevant one. A pointwise judge answers "does this meet the bar", and a distractor has to push one answer across a threshold. The paper's own phrasing is that binary preferences "exaggerate small gaps".

## Self-preference

A judge may favour text from its own model. MT-Bench measured GPT-4 judging with a win rate about 10% higher for its own answers and Claude-v1 about 25% higher, while GPT-3.5 did not favour itself, and the authors state that limited data and small differences mean they cannot determine whether the bias exists.

Panickssery, Bowman and Feng built the causal case. On news summaries (XSUM and CNN/DailyMail, 1,000 articles each), GPT-4 asked to choose between its own summary and another source's, with options swapped, preferred its own with a score of 0.705 on XSUM and 0.912 on CNN/DailyMail; Llama 2 7B was near 0.51 on both. Those scores alone cannot separate bias from GPT-4's summaries being better. The stronger evidence is the fine-tuning result: training models to recognise their own outputs moved self-preference along a linear trend with self-recognition, and control fine-tunes on length, vowel count and readability fell on the same line.

The FAQ's position is that using the same model as the application and the judge is usually fine, because the judge performs a different, scoped task, and that alignment with human labels is what matters. Both positions hold once the question is split. For a scoped binary check validated on human labels, self-preference shows up as a lower *f* on the judge's own family's outputs, and the confusion matrix stratified by generator measures it. For a pairwise comparison between two model families, the judge is a party to the comparison, and a judge from a third family, or the ranking under two judges, is the control.

## Grading what it cannot see

One more failure behaves like a bias in production and is not one. MT-Bench asked judges to grade answers to 10 math questions with swapped orders, 20 trials. The default prompt failed 14 of 20; chain-of-thought failed 6; reference-guided grading, with the judge given a correct solution, failed 3. A judge without the reference grades plausibility, and plausibility is what the model under test already optimises. For factual and policy checks, pass the reference: the source document, the policy text or the expected answer.

## The tests as code

Each test is a controlled pair. `compare` runs both orders; `flip_rate` measures how often a quality-preserving perturbation of the losing answer makes it win.

```python
def compare(x, y, judge):
    """judge(first, second) returns "first" or "second". Run both orders."""
    x_when_first = judge(x, y) == "first"
    x_when_second = judge(y, x) == "second"
    if x_when_first and x_when_second:
        return "x"
    if not x_when_first and not x_when_second:
        return "y"
    return "tie"

def flip_rate(pairs, judge, perturb):
    """Share of decided pairs whose loser wins after perturb(loser), a change
    meant to hold quality fixed: padding, an assertive preamble, flattery."""
    decided = flips = 0
    for x, y in pairs:
        before = compare(x, y, judge)
        if before == "tie":
            continue
        winner, loser = (x, y) if before == "x" else (y, x)
        decided += 1
        flips += compare(winner, perturb(loser), judge) == "y"
    return flips / decided if decided else float("nan")

# A toy judge that prefers the longer answer and breaks ties by position.
toy = lambda a, b: "first" if len(a) >= len(b) else "second"
pairs = [("refund issued, ref 4411", "refund issued"), ("see policy 7.2", "see policy")]
print([compare(x, y, toy) for x, y in pairs])                          # ['x', 'x']
print(flip_rate(pairs, toy, lambda t: t + ". To summarise: " + t))     # 1.0
```

The pointwise version of `flip_rate` perturbs one answer and counts verdicts that change from fail to pass. Run both on the same pairs and the comparison in Tripathi's paper becomes a measurement on your judge and your data rather than a number from someone else's.

## Pairwise or pointwise for a release question

The decision depends on what the release question asks.

**"Does the candidate meet the bar on failure mode X?"** Pointwise binary, one judge per failure mode. It gives an absolute pass rate, which is what a gate needs, and it is the form the previous lesson's correction applies to. Tripathi's result says it is the protocol less moved by distractors, and their discussion recommends absolute scoring for instruction-following and correctness. Most regression questions are this one.

**"Which of two candidates do users prefer on a subjective axis?"** Tone, helpfulness of phrasing, style. There is no bar, only a preference, and pairwise asks it directly. Run both orders, report ties, use a judge from neither candidate's family, and length-control or test with padded pairs. Expect most of the signal to vanish into ties when the candidates are close, because that is where Shi's results put position bias.

**Many candidates.** Pairwise cost grows with pairs, which MT-Bench notes grow quadratically: six candidates are 15 pairs, 30 judge calls per item with swaps, against 6 pointwise calls. Pointwise first to cut the field, pairwise on the last two.

MT-Bench records the cost on the other side: absolute scores can shift when the judge model changes, while pairwise results are more stable. A pointwise gate needs its judge model pinned and re-validated on change; a pairwise comparison is less sensitive to the judge but gives no pass rate.

## When this is wrong

The bias numbers come from specific models: GPT-4, GPT-3.5 and Claude-v1 in 2023, and open-weight judges from 3B to 72B parameters in Tripathi's 2025 study. A current judge, and especially a reasoning model, may be more or less biased than any of them. The numbers are reasons to run the tests on your judge, not estimates of its bias.

Consistency under swapping is not accuracy. A judge that always prefers the longer answer is perfectly consistent across orders. Swap tests remove position from the result; they do not show that the remaining verdicts are right, which only human labels do.

The controlled pair assumes the perturbation holds quality fixed. Padding a list holds quality fixed; adding a caveat or a confident summary may change it, and a human would grade the perturbed answer differently. Have a human check a sample of perturbed answers before counting flips as bias.

Pointwise robustness in Tripathi's study is relative. About 9% of absolute scores still flipped under a distractor, and pointwise scores carry their own calibration drift when the judge model changes.
