## The decision

After error analysis, the taxonomy names the failure modes worth checking. Every check that is not plain code needs labels: a judge needs a labelled set to be tuned on and a separate one to be measured on, and a golden set needs verdicts someone will defend. Those labels come from a few expert hours a week, and the experts' definitions move as they read. The decision this lesson prepares is a written labelling programme: who labels, how many labels per mode per week, how agreement is measured, when old labels are redone, and which modes the programme will refuse to gate because it cannot afford the labels to gate them honestly.

Labelling has the shape of a production pipeline: a fixed-capacity worker (the expert), a work queue (traces to label), a quality check (agreement) and schema migrations (rubric changes). It differs from one in that the worker's output definition is itself an output of the work.

## Who labels

The AI Evals FAQ recommends one domain expert as the "benevolent dictator" for most small and medium-sized companies, and more annotators only when the scope or the number of domains requires it. The reason is throughput of decisions, not of labels: one person's definitions do not need reconciling. The cost is that the taxonomy and the labels carry one person's view of the product, and nothing measures how idiosyncratic that view is.

A second labeller is how you find out. Anthropic's engineering post on agent evals (9 January 2026) gives the test for a well-specified task: two domain experts would independently reach the same pass or fail verdict. The same post reports the price of skipping the test: Opus 4.5 scored 42% on CORE-Bench at first and 95% after grading bugs and task specifications were fixed. Of the 58 points of measured failure (100 − 42), 53 (95 − 42) went away when the grader and the task definitions were fixed.

The usual staffing is the expert as decider and an engineer or product manager as second labeller on an overlap sample. The second labeller's hours do not come out of the expert's budget, and their disagreements are what the expert's time is spent on.

## How you know they agree

Raw agreement is the wrong number when one class is rare, which is the normal case for a failure mode. Husain's 2024 essay warns that raw agreement misleads on imbalanced data; the FAQ asks for a chance-corrected metric such as Cohen's kappa when there is more than one annotator.

<p>The expert fails 11 of 100 and the engineer 9 of 100. Chance agreement is p<sub>e</sub> = 0.11 × 0.09 + 0.89 × 0.91 = 0.0099 + 0.8099 = 0.8198. Kappa = (0.90 − 0.8198) / (1 − 0.8198) = 0.0802 / 0.1802 = <b>0.45</b>. Ninety percent agreement on a mode that fails about 10% of the time is mostly the two of them agreeing that ordinary traces pass. Of the 15 traces either labeller failed, they agree on 5.</p>
</Predict>

The last line of that reveal is the number to act on. The FAQ gives no kappa threshold, so the programme writes its own and the reason for it. The process the FAQ describes is the useful part: label independently, measure, discuss the disagreements, update the definition, relabel, and let the domain expert decide what is still disputed and record why. In the example, the ten disagreements are the agenda, and the output of discussing them is a sharper definition, such as whether "the viewing date" means the first slot or any slot.

When the labels later grade a judge, agreement is measured the same way, as the judge's true positive rate (failures it catches) and true negative rate (good outputs it passes), both computed on the labelled test split. The FAQ asks for acceptable levels set by the consequences for your application. Because both rates are conditional on the true label, they are not biased by a sample enriched with failures; a pass rate computed on that same enriched sample would be.

## Throughput: what four hours a week buys

The FAQ's minimum review is 30 minutes for 20 to 50 outputs, which is 30 / 50 = 0.6 to 30 / 20 = 1.5 minutes per output. Use the slow end for binary labels on real traces: 1.5 minutes. Four expert hours is 240 minutes, or 160 binary labels a week at that rate.

The FAQ's sizes for a judge-backed failure mode are 100 to 200 labelled examples, split 10 to 20% train (examples that may appear in the judge prompt), 40 to 45% dev for tuning and 40 to 45% test for one final measurement, with 30 to 50 failing and 30 to 50 passing examples in both dev and test where possible. The failing examples are the expensive ones. A random sample of a mode that fails 6% of the time needs 60 / 0.06 = 1,000 labels to collect 60 fails, which is 1,000 × 1.5 = 1,500 minutes, 25 hours, more than six weeks of a four-hour budget for one mode.

Change the failure rate in the pool to 30% and the same targets need 200 labels and 5 hours. That pool is built, not sampled: traces flagged by guardrails, by user complaints or by a cheap keyword filter, which is the FAQ's own list of what to prioritise. Enrichment is safe for the judge's TPR and TNR, for the reason above, and it is unsafe for anything that reads like a production rate. A programme that enriches says so next to every number it publishes.

## Drift and rotation

Definitions move while people label. Shankar and co-authors named it criteria drift: users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria. In their study, nine practitioners added criteria after seeing new kinds of bad output and reinterpreted the old ones; sessions ran 45 minutes to 1 hour 15 minutes, and more than half said they would iterate further with more time. The FAQ draws the operational conclusion: do not write a detailed rubric before reviewing examples.

Three rules follow. Every label carries the rubric version it was made under, as a column, the way a row carries its schema version. A definition change triggers relabelling of the traces where the old and new definitions can disagree, budgeted from the same hours. And the labelled sets are rotated: the FAQ's cadence is at least 100 fresh traces per review cycle, cycles of 2 to 4 weeks, 10 to 20 traces a week in between, weekly for new systems and monthly once mature. Fresh traces find the modes the current taxonomy does not have; without them the programme only ever measures last month's failures.

## A six-week programme

Scenario: a support assistant, one expert for four hours a week, an engineer as second labeller, two judge-backed modes (A and B) and one mode a code assertion can check (C). Open coding of a multi-step trace is assumed at 2.4 minutes, slower than a binary label; that figure is the scenario's, not a measurement.

| Week | Expert work | Minutes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Open-code 100 fresh traces at 2.4 minutes | 240 |
| 2 | Axial coding and written definitions (60); binary labels for A, B and C on the same 100 traces at 1.5 (150); 20 fresh outliers (30) | 240 |
| 3 | 120 labels for A from an enriched pool (180); resolve disagreements with the engineer's overlap labels (30); 20 fresh (30) | 240 |
| 4 | 120 more labels for A (180); rubric revision and relabelling (30); 20 fresh (30) | 240 |
| 5 | 140 labels for B from an enriched pool (210); 20 fresh (30) | 240 |
| 6 | Second cycle: open-code 100 fresh traces | 240 |

The total is 6 × 240 = 1,440 expert minutes, 24 hours. Mode A ends with 240 enriched labels; at a 30% failure rate in the pool that is 72 fails and 168 passes, and a 42.5% test split holds about 30 fails, the bottom of the FAQ's range. Check it in the widget: 60 fails for dev and test plus a 15% train share is 60 / 0.85 ≈ 71 fails; enter 71 failing examples at a 30% rate and it asks for 237 labels. Mode B ends with 140 labels and 42 fails, about 18 in a test split. Mode C is gated by its assertion, with the 100 week-two labels used only to check that the assertion agrees with the expert.

What the programme refuses to gate, written into the plan before week one: mode B, until its test split holds 30 fails; any mode whose definition changed in the current cycle, until relabelling is done; any mode whose second-labeller kappa stays near the 0.45 of the example above after one rubric revision, because the label itself is not yet defined. Those modes are reported with counts and sample sizes and do not block a merge.

## When this is wrong

The sizes come from one practitioner source. The FAQ's 100 to 200 labels per mode and 30 to 50 per class are working guidance, not derived from a target interval width; a mode with a severe failure and a tight acceptable rate may need a larger test split, sized from the interval width the gate has to resolve.

The minutes per label assume short traces. A 40-step agent trajectory or a long document review can take far longer than 1.5 minutes, and the programme must be planned on a timed sample of your own traces, not on the FAQ's rate.

A single expert is a bus factor of one. The benevolent-dictator model trades agreement work for dependence on a person; for regulated decisions or multiple domains, the FAQ's own exception applies and a panel with measured agreement replaces the dictator.

Enriched pools carry their filter's blind spots. A pool built from guardrail flags contains only failures the guardrail can see, so a judge tuned on it can look strong on test and miss the failures that never trip the flag. A slice of uniformly sampled traces in every test split is the check.
