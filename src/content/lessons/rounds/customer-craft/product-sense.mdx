## What a model changes in a product answer

A product-sense answer for a deterministic feature can treat correctness as a given and spend its time on users, problems, solutions and a metric. A model-backed feature cannot. The same input can produce different outputs, some outputs are wrong in ways that look right, and every request has a marginal cost that a database lookup does not. Four parts follow from that and belong in the answer before any launch is proposed: the failure the user cannot detect, a success metric with guardrails, the cost per outcome, and the eval plan that gates the launch.

Applied-LLMs, written in June 2024 by six practitioners, puts the same point as a section heading: "The model isn't the product, the system around it is." The four parts are the system around it.

The prompts fall into three shapes. Productising a capability starts from a model ability and has to find a job for it. Growing a metric with a fixed team starts from a number and has to choose what not to build. "When not to use an LLM" tests whether the candidate will recommend the cheaper, auditable option. The four parts below apply to all three.

## The failure the user cannot detect

Sort the feature's failures by who notices and when. A failure the user sees immediately, such as an image that ignores the prompt, costs a retry. A failure the user notices later, such as a wrong delivery date, costs a support contact and some trust. A failure nobody notices, such as a summary that drops a refund the company promised, becomes a liability that surfaces in a complaint or an audit. Model-backed features produce all three, and the third class decides the design.

The design levers move failures toward the detectable classes: show the source next to the claim, preview an action before executing it, keep actions reversible, and route the cases where a wrong answer is costly and invisible to a person. Garage Labs calls the last of these autonomy by reversibility: the more damage an action can do and the harder it is to undo, the more confirmation it needs. In the text-to-music prompt the undetectable class includes output that imitates a protected work closely enough to cause a takedown, which no listener flags at generation time. The answer names that class and the control for it before it names a launch market.

## When not to use an LLM

Applied-LLMs advises prioritising deterministic workflows, and notes small classifiers such as DistilBERT as a strong baseline for classification. Garage Labs reports that LinkedIn's version of the question rewards saying a rule would work better when it would.

The decision procedure is short. If the correct output is fully determined by rules that can be written down and tested, write the rules: they are cheaper per call, auditable, and their failures are reproducible. If the input is unstructured language and a wrong output is either cheap or detectable, a model fits. If the input is unstructured and a wrong output is costly and undetectable, the model needs a narrower scope, a review step, or both before it ships. A common production shape uses the model only to map free text onto a structured request and lets deterministic code decide and act; the model's failures then land in a validation layer that can reject them.

## The success metric and its guardrails

Garage Labs' metrics chain is mission, goal, North Star, guardrails. For a model-backed feature the North Star has to count outcomes, not outputs. "Images generated" rises when users regenerate because the first result was wrong; "sessions where the user kept or shared an image" counts success. Hamel Husain's field guide describes a team that raised a generic helpfulness score by 10 percent while users still struggled with basic tasks, which is the same error at the eval layer.

Guardrails are the metrics that must not move while the North Star rises: the rate of the undetectable failure, measured by sampling and review because users will not report it; policy violations; latency at the percentile the workflow cares about; and cost per outcome. In a fixed-team prompt such as the three-engineer one, the guardrails also decide what is refused: each guardrail needs an owner and a measurement, and a team of three can operate only a few.

## Cost per outcome

Token prices fall quickly. Applied-LLMs reports that the cost of davinci-level performance dropped from $20 to under 10 cents per million tokens over four years, a halving time of six months, as of June 2024. That makes token price a weak lever in a product answer and the success rate a strong one, because failures are paid for twice.

<Fragment slot="prompt">
<p>Scenario inputs, not published prices. A support assistant makes 3 model calls per conversation at $0.015 per call. It resolves 55 percent of conversations; the rest go to a human agent at $5.00 per ticket, the same cost as a ticket that never touched the assistant.</p>
</Fragment>
<p>Model cost is 3 × $0.015 = $0.045 per conversation. Escalations cost 0.45 × $5.00 = $2.25. The blend is $2.295, about $2.30, against $5.00 without the assistant. Halving the token price saves about 2 cents per ticket. Raising resolution from 55 to 65 percent saves 0.10 × $5.00 = $0.50. The dominant term is escalation, so the product lever is the resolution rate, and the eval that measures it is the cost model's most important input.</p>
</Predict>

The scenario hides two assumptions that flip the conclusion. If an escalation after a failed assistant attempt costs more than a fresh ticket, because the customer is angrier or the agent has to read the transcript, the assistant can lose money at a resolution rate that looks good. And "resolved" here means the conversation ended without escalation; if some of those were wrong answers the customer accepted, the real cost per correct outcome is higher and the difference shows up later as complaints.

```python
def cost_per_ticket(calls, price_per_call, resolve_rate, human_cost, escalation_premium=0.0):
    model = calls * price_per_call
    escalated = (1 - resolve_rate) * human_cost * (1 + escalation_premium)
    return model + escalated

print(round(cost_per_ticket(3, 0.015, 0.55, 5.00), 3))       # 2.295
print(round(cost_per_ticket(3, 0.015, 0.55, 5.00, 0.5), 3))  # 3.42: a 50% premium on escalations
```

## The eval plan before launch

The eval plan is the part of the answer that converts the other three into a launch decision. It has four pieces: a labelled set drawn from real inputs rather than invented ones; per-criterion pass or fail checks, which Applied-LLMs suggests starting as assertion-based tests built from real input and output samples; a launch threshold for the North Star and a ceiling for the undetectable failure, both fixed before the results are seen; and an online sample after launch, reviewed by a person, that measures the undetectable class the offline set may not contain.

The ceiling cannot be zero. Applied-LLMs reports, from its authors' work in 2024, hallucination at a baseline of 5 to 10 percent and difficulty getting below 2 percent even on tasks as simple as summarisation. Those figures are dated and model-dependent; the point that survives is that the plan states an acceptable non-zero rate, the sample size that can measure it, and what the product does in the cases that fail.

## Prototyping inside the round

The reported Meta format changes the evidence available. With a working prototype on the screen, the undetectable failure can be produced rather than described, and an answer that names it has a demonstration attached. By Garage Labs' account the grading is on steering and critiquing the AI tool, which rewards treating the prototype's first output as a sample to test rather than a result.

## When this is wrong

Every attribution on this page is secondhand. Question banks built from candidate reports skew toward memorable prompts and paraphrase them, and a single report can be repeated across vendors until it looks like several. The structure here is useful regardless of whether any company asks these exact prompts.

The four-part structure suits features where outputs are unstructured and failures are silent. For a model used as a classifier with labelled ground truth, the problem reduces to precision, recall and cost, and the extra parts are overhead. For a creative tool where users regenerate freely and there is no wrong answer, the undetectable class may be small, and the answer spends its time on retention and cost.

The cost model assumes linear costs. Real deployments have committed-capacity pricing, caching discounts and human teams that cannot shrink in proportion to deflected tickets, so the saving per resolved ticket is smaller than the arithmetic until staffing changes. And the token-price trend quoted is from 2024; it argues for treating price as a falling input, not for a specific future price.
