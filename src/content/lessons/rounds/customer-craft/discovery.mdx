## What the round is scoring

A discovery conversation is requirements elicitation where the stakeholder does not know which of their requirements are binding. The output is not rapport. It is a short list of facts that decide the architecture and the pilot: what the buyer will measure, what has already failed and how they found out, which constraints cannot move, and what workflow the system replaces. Anything proposed before those are known is a guess the customer has to correct, and a customer who has seen failed pilots will not bother.

The format is only partly public. Anthropic's London Forward Deployed Engineer post, published 14 September 2026, lists "strong communication skills to conduct discovery with customers" as a requirement, alongside building inside customer systems and shipping MCP servers, sub-agents and agent skills. OpenAI's Forward Deployed Software Engineer post asks for embedding with strategic customers "to understand their business challenges and technical requirements in detail", an experiment-driven approach, and scopes of work for both proofs of concept and production deployments. Those are the official texts.

The round itself is reported, not published. A vendor page from Perspective AI (29 May 2026, sourced to Glassdoor reports and candidate write-ups, not confirmed by Anthropic) describes a customer-conversation simulation in Anthropic's Applied AI Engineer loop: a stage of 60 to 90 minutes containing a 45-minute discovery call, with the interviewer playing a buyer such as a VP of Engineering at a 5,000-person fintech. It lists what a candidate should uncover: the buyer's current evaluation criteria, previous failed AI deployments, constraints that cannot change (compliance, latency, data residency) and the workflow the system would replace. techinterview.org (23 July 2026) reports the same simulation. Treat the length and weight as unconfirmed; the skill is in both job posts regardless.

## Each answer gates a design decision

A design document has a constraints section and a non-goals section because they prune the solution space before anyone argues about components. Discovery fills those sections from a person instead of a ticket. The difference is that the customer states some constraints as solutions ("keep customer data out of it") and does not know which of their preferences are hard limits until legal is asked.

The useful way to hold the topic list is by what each answer decides:

| What you elicit | What it decides | What a wrong guess costs |
|---|---|---|
| How the buyer evaluates AI tools today | The pilot's success metric and who owns it | A pilot that passes your eval and fails theirs |
| Previous deployments that failed, and how the failure was found | Whether monitoring and review are the real requirement; the size of the trust deficit | Repeating the failure with a better model |
| Data classes touched (PII, PHI, payment data, export-controlled) | Processor, retention arrangement, which API features are allowed | A rebuild when legal reviews the data flow |
| Residency | Hosting platform and region | Discovering the platform cannot meet it after the build |
| Latency the workflow tolerates | Synchronous or queued design, model tier, streaming | An agent that is correct and unusable at the counter |
| The workflow being replaced, with its volume | The baseline, the action surface, the unit of cost | Automating the step that was never the bottleneck |
| Who signs off and what evidence they accept | The form of the pilot readout | A good result nobody with authority reads |

Residency shows why the order matters. Anthropic's data-residency documentation, checked 2026-10-28, offers two values for `inference_geo`, `"us"` and `"global"`, and one workspace geo, `"us"`; on Amazon Bedrock and Google-managed Vertex AI the region is set by the endpoint or inference profile instead. A customer whose data must be processed in the EU has therefore already ruled out the first-party routing controls as documented, and the hosting question becomes which cloud platform and region, checked against that platform's list on the day. That is a fact to find in minute ten, not week three. The [FDE decomposition playbook](/learn/rounds/customer-craft/fde-decomposition) works the hosting choice in full.

## Reflecting back instead of pitching

Perspective names pitching as the failure mode and describes the interviewer inviting it, with a buyer who doubts Claude does anything ChatGPT cannot. A pitch answers no open question on the table above. The same challenge read as discovery is a statement that the buyer has, or lacks, evaluation criteria: what would a tool have to do on their own data for them to choose it, and who ran the last comparison.

Reflecting back is reading the spec back to its author. "Volume is shipment-status questions, the metric you report is first-response time, the last bot quoted wrong delivery dates for a month before complaints surfaced it, EU data stays in the EU, and the COO signs off" is a structured statement the customer can correct line by line, so the restatement is also an elicitation tool: the corrections are the new information.

## From "it feels dumb" to a hypothesis and an experiment

techinterview.org reports the question "A customer says the bot 'feels dumb.' What do you change first?" The complaint is an adjective over an unknown distribution of failures. The procedure that turns it into engineering work is error analysis, which Hamel Husain's field guide (March 2025) calls the highest-return activity in improving an AI product.

Ask for transcripts the customer considers bad and, for each, what a good answer would have been. Write a free-text note on each, group the notes into failure types, count them, and take the largest group as the hypothesis. In the NurtureBoss case in the same guide, three issues accounted for over 60 percent of the problems, and date handling went from 33 percent to 95 percent success after fixes targeted at it. The counts make the decision. The guide argues that generic scores such as "helpfulness" create the appearance of progress without it, and recommends binary pass or fail judgements per failure type because a change in the pass rate is immediately interpretable.

For a support bot the buckets are usually separable by where the error starts: wrong facts (retrieval or stale data), right facts and missed intent (instructions or context), correct but unusable (length, hedging, tone), and unable to act (missing tool or permission). Each bucket names a component, which is what makes it a hypothesis rather than a mood. The experiment is one change aimed at the largest bucket, scored pass or fail on the same transcripts before and after, read as a paired comparison, with the changed transcripts shown to the customer next to the score.

The number of transcripts to request is derivable, and it is the number to agree on in the meeting.

<Fragment slot="prompt">
<p>Among the conversations the customer calls bad, one failure type accounts for 10 percent. Each transcript you read shows that type with probability 0.1, independently.</p>
</Fragment>
<p>The chance of missing it in n transcripts is 0.9 to the power n. Setting 0.9ⁿ ≤ 0.05 gives n ≥ ln 0.05 / ln 0.9 = 2.996 / 0.1054 = 28.4, so 29. A type at 5 percent needs ln 0.05 / ln 0.95 = 58.4, so 59; a type at 20 percent needs 14. Asking for "a few examples" finds the dominant bucket and misses the ones that justify the second experiment.</p>
</Predict>

The same arithmetic as a function:

```python
import math

def transcripts_needed(prevalence, confidence=0.95):
    """Smallest n with P(at least one sighting) >= confidence, assuming independent samples."""
    return math.ceil(math.log(1 - confidence) / math.log(1 - prevalence))

print([transcripts_needed(p) for p in (0.2, 0.1, 0.05)])  # [14, 29, 59]
```

Independence is the assumption that breaks first. Transcripts the customer picks are the memorable ones, and memorable failures cluster; ask for a random sample from the logs as well as the curated set, and report which bucket counts came from which.

## Thirty minutes with a sceptical stakeholder

A stakeholder who has watched two pilots fail carries the most useful evidence in the room: how those pilots failed and how long it took to notice. That history decides whether the pilot is a model project or a monitoring project.

A plan for a 30-minute session, ordered so that constraints that rule out designs arrive before any design is discussed:

| Minutes | Elicit | Leaves the room as |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 6 | The workflow, its volume, the number reported upward today | A baseline and a unit of work |
| 6 to 14 | Data classes, residency, retention, audit, latency tolerance | A hosting shortlist and a list of forbidden features |
| 14 to 22 | The failed pilots: what went wrong, who noticed, after how long | The monitoring requirement and the pilot's guardrail metric |
| 22 to 27 | Restatement, corrected by the stakeholder | A one-paragraph scope both sides accept |
| 27 to 30 | The data needed, the owner of each open question, the sign-off path | A dated next step |

The allocation is a choice, not a measurement. It front-loads constraints because a residency or retention answer can eliminate an option outright, while the history section only changes emphasis. If the stakeholder opens with the failed pilots, the history goes first; the table is a coverage target, not a script.

## When this is wrong

The simulation round is described by a vendor and a prep site, not by Anthropic, and the vendor's page cites no documents. Its length, its weight in the loop and its pass criteria may differ by team or have changed. The job posts are the firmer evidence, and they describe discovery as part of the job rather than a round format.

Some buyers have finished discovery themselves. A procurement team comparing vendors on a written evaluation plan wants that plan answered, and first-principles questions read as not having read it. The elicitation then narrows to the gaps in their plan: missing guardrail metrics, a test set drawn from the wrong population, a latency target with no percentile.

Error analysis assumes the customer can hand over transcripts. In regulated settings the first sample may take weeks to clear, and the first experiment then runs on synthetic or redacted data whose failure distribution differs from production. The readout says which buckets the redacted set could not represent.

The person in the room is often not the person with the answer. Discovery that ends with every constraint confirmed is rare; discovery that ends with each open question assigned to a named owner and a date is the realistic outcome.
