## The decision

A forward-deployed brief arrives as a sentence: "use AI on our support tickets", "the dashboard is wrong", "we want Claude for our claims team". The decision is what the first two weeks build and how the result will be judged, and it is expressed as six artefacts: a scope with non-goals, a success metric owned by the customer, a data-access plan, a hosting choice, a list of compliance questions with owners, and a two-week plan that ends in a measurable pilot.

The round that tests this is reported, not published. techinterview.org (5 August 2026) describes a 45-minute decomposition round, citing Palantir's description of it as breaking a vague, real-world challenge into component parts, scored on structure, end-user empathy, data reasoning, scoping and response to pushback. Its example prompts carry no company attribution, among them a customer who says their dashboard is wrong and cannot say which number, and patient records spread across six systems. A vendor page on OpenAI's loop (reviewed 13 July 2026, built mainly from one first-person account) describes a 60-minute solution-design round that checks whether the candidate starts from the customer and the definition of success or from model names. A candidate report on aced.io, for an Anthropic ML Engineer, Prompt Engineer role, records the question this playbook works in full: a regulated-industry customer wants to use the Claude API; design the implementation, choose the hosting model, then say what changes for a government contractor.

## The inputs that change it

**Data class.** Personal data, protected health information, payment data, controlled unclassified information and export-controlled data each bring a different processor requirement. The data class is the input that most often eliminates options.

**Who must be the processor.** Anthropic's retention documentation, checked 2026-10-28, separates two cases. On the first-party API, Claude Platform on AWS, Claude in Microsoft Foundry and Anthropic-managed Google Cloud, Anthropic is the processor. On Amazon Bedrock and Google-managed Vertex AI, the cloud provider is. Many enterprises already have a processing agreement and an approved boundary with one cloud, and extending it is faster than adding a processor.

**Retention.** Two arrangements exist on the first-party API. Zero data retention is enabled per organisation through Anthropic's sales team. HIPAA readiness needs a signed BAA, is enforced per organisation, cannot be switched off once enabled, and makes the API return a 400 for features outside it. Neither arrangement covers batch processing (29-day retention), the Files API, code execution (container data kept up to 30 days), the MCP connector or Agent Skills; prompt caching is covered by both. Some models require 30-day retention and are unavailable under ZDR unless Anthropic authorises it. Under either arrangement, content flagged by trust and safety systems may be retained for up to 2 years. Each of these removes a feature from the design or a model from the shortlist.

**Residency.** The first-party `inference_geo` parameter takes `"us"` or `"global"` on Claude 4.6 and later models, US-only is priced at 1.1 times the standard rate, and workspace geo is `"us"` only and fixed at creation. On Bedrock and Google-managed Vertex the region comes from the endpoint.

**Freshness and write access.** techinterview.org's question about a day-stale answer against a minute-stale one decides between a nightly extract and a live integration. Reading is a data-access request; writing to a system of record is a change-control request with a different approver.

**Team and calendar.** Who on the customer side can grant access, label examples and sign off, and how many days each takes. In regulated customers the access approval, not the engineering, can set the start date.

## The options and when each wins

**First-party API with an arrangement (ZDR or HIPAA readiness).** Wins when the customer's legal team can add Anthropic as a processor, the newest models and features matter, and the data stays in the US or may be routed globally. It loses when the data class needs a processor the customer has already approved, or when residency outside the US is a hard rule.

**The customer's cloud (Bedrock, Google-managed Vertex AI, Foundry).** Wins when the customer's compliance boundary, network controls and procurement already run through that provider, and when residency requires a region the first-party controls do not offer. The cost is that model and feature availability follow the platform, pricing is the platform's own, and retention controls are the provider's, documented separately.

**A government boundary.** Anthropic's government page, checked 2026-10-28, lists Claude in Amazon Bedrock with FedRAMP High and IL5, Claude on Vertex AI with FedRAMP High and not IL5, and Claude for Government with FedRAMP High, not IL5, and classified work in pilot. It routes ITAR-controlled data to Bedrock in AWS GovCloud (US) and IL6 to Bedrock in the AWS Secret region. Wins, and is required, when the contract specifies an authorisation level. The cost is model lag: the June 2025 GovCloud approval named Claude 3.5 Sonnet v1 and Claude 3 Haiku while newer models were available commercially, with later ones "may be added in the future". The model in the boundary today has to be checked on the day, and it is the model the eval runs on.

**No model in the first step.** Wins when the brief is a data problem in disguise. "The dashboard is wrong" can be a definition, freshness or join problem, and then the first deliverable is a reconciliation, not an assistant.

## A worked decomposition: the dashboard nobody can point at

The prompt gives a symptom with no locus, which makes it a bisection problem over a data pipeline whose specification lives in the user's head. The first hour has a fixed shape. Get the screen the user looks at and the decision they make from it, because that decision names the two or three numbers that matter. Pick one entity and one day, compute those numbers by hand from the source system, and compare. Each disagreement falls into a class with a different owner: definition (the metric means something else to finance than to the dashboard), freshness (the extract is a day old and the user expects the morning's data), duplication from a join, time zone boundaries, or a filter nobody remembers adding. The scope for week one is reconciliation of the decision-driving numbers with a written definition for each, signed by the user. A model enters only if the remaining work is explaining discrepancies in language to many users, and even then the deterministic reconciliation is the ground truth it is evaluated against.

## A worked decision: regulated customer, then government contractor

Stated case, a scenario: a US health insurer wants drafts of member correspondence about claim decisions, generated from the claim record and the policy text, reviewed by a claims specialist before sending. The data is PHI.

**Scope.** Drafts only; no sending, no claim-status changes. Non-goals: appeals, anything that writes to the claims system.

**Hosting.** Two defensible options. The first-party API with HIPAA readiness gives the newest models; the cost is adding Anthropic as a processor and designing without batch, the Files API, code execution and the MCP connector, which the API will reject. The insurer's existing cloud keeps the processor list unchanged, provided that provider's own documentation lists the model service as eligible for PHI under the insurer's agreement, which is a check, not an assumption; the cost is the platform's model list and its own retention terms. With an approved AWS boundary already in place, the second option avoids a new processor review. HIPAA readiness is not available on Claude Platform on AWS, so "on AWS" means Bedrock in this case, and the distinction goes in the decision memo.

**Data access.** A read-only view with the minimum fields the draft needs, a service identity per environment, and audit logs on the insurer's side recording which claim was read for which draft.

**Success metric and guardrail.** The North Star is the share of drafts sent with no substantive edit, owned by the claims operations lead. The guardrail is drafts that state another member's data or a wrong claim decision, found by specialist review, with a ceiling of 1 percent. How many reviewed drafts that ceiling needs is a derivable number.

<Predict id="rounds-cc-zero-defect" question="How many reviewed drafts with zero errors do you need before claiming, at 95% confidence, that the error rate is below 1%?" answer={299} tolerance={3} unit="drafts">
<Fragment slot="prompt">
<p>Each draft independently contains the serious error with probability p. You review n drafts and find none. The claim holds at 95% confidence when a true rate of 1% would produce zero errors in n drafts with probability at most 0.05.</p>
</Fragment>
<p>0.99ⁿ ≤ 0.05 gives n ≥ ln 0.05 / ln 0.99 = 2.996 / 0.01005 = 298.1, so 299. The rule of three (3 / 0.01 = 300) gives the same answer. A two-week pilot with a few specialists may review a few hundred drafts, so a 1% ceiling is measurable in the pilot; a 0.1% ceiling needs about 3,000 and is not, which belongs in the readout as a limit of what the pilot can show.</p>
</Predict>

**Government contractor variant.** The same drafting system for a contractor handling CUI, some of it ITAR-controlled. Four things change. Hosting moves to an authorised boundary: Bedrock in AWS GovCloud (US) is the route the government page gives for ITAR data. The model shortlist becomes whatever is authorised there today, so the eval reruns on that model and the readout states any gap from the commercial prototype. Features follow the boundary, so the design cannot assume anything the GovCloud deployment does not offer. And the approvers change: the contractor's security officer and the contract's authorisation requirements decide, and the schedule follows their review, so the two-week plan starts with that review rather than a build.

**Two-week plan.** Eugene Yan's practice, as quoted in Hamel Husain's field guide, time-boxes two weeks for data feasibility before a month for technical feasibility, with a decision point at each. The two-week pilot here is the first box.

| Days | Work | Exit condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 2 | Access approved, data classified, hosting confirmed in writing | Read access to a de-identified sample, or a named blocker with an owner |
| 3 to 4 | Labelled set: real claims with the letter the specialist would send | A set large enough to find the common failure types (see the discovery lesson) |
| 5 to 8 | Baseline drafting pipeline on the chosen hosting | Drafts for the whole set, logged |
| 9 to 10 | Error analysis and one targeted change | Failure counts before and after |
| 11 to 13 | Specialist review of live drafts | Guardrail count against the 299-draft bound |
| 14 | Readout to the sign-off owner | Expand, change or stop, with the evidence |

The day counts are a choice for this case, not a benchmark. If access takes longer than two days, the plan does not compress the review; it moves the readout.

## What to measure after deciding

The North Star and guardrail from the pilot, sampled weekly after expansion. Specialist edit distance on the drafts that were edited, to see whether the edits are shrinking or shifting to a new failure type. The rate of API errors from features outside the retention arrangement, which should be zero and is a design regression when it is not. The model available in the boundary, checked on each provider release, since a new authorised model triggers a rerun of the eval rather than an automatic switch. And the vendor page's expansion question, "how would you know it is working well enough to expand to the next team?", answered with the same metrics on the next team's sample before rollout.

## In the room

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"The insurer pilot passed on the newest commercial model. The customer's parent company is a defence contractor and its security office now says the work must run in GovCloud, where the newest authorised model is two generations older. The pilot readout is in ten days. What do you present?"
</Callout>

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>I present the pilot result as evidence about the task, not about the deployable system, and rerun the eval on the authorised model in GovCloud before the readout. Ten days covers the rerun on the existing labelled set and a smaller specialist review. If the older model clears the guardrail but not the North Star, I recommend a narrower scope in the boundary over waiting for a newer authorisation with no date. I give up the headline number.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The constraints conflict: the deadline is fixed, the authorised model is fixed, and the evidence I have is for a model the customer cannot use. Presenting the commercial result as if it transferred would be the error that ends the engagement when production numbers arrive.</p>
<p>The labelled set and the error taxonomy transfer; the numbers do not. Days 1 to 3 go to standing up the GovCloud path with the same prompts and data access, days 4 to 6 to the full offline eval on the authorised model, days 7 to 9 to a specialist review of live drafts. The review will be smaller than 299 drafts, so the readout says the 1% ceiling is not yet demonstrated and states the count it has.</p>
<p>The recommendation has three possible shapes, decided by the numbers: the older model meets both thresholds, and we expand; it meets the guardrail and misses the North Star, and we narrow scope to the claim types where it performs; it misses the guardrail, and we stop and say what authorisation would need to change. The decision memo records which of those happened and who signed.</p>
</Fragment>

## When this is wrong

The platform facts on this page were checked on 2026-10-28 and change faster than the reasoning. Feature eligibility, model availability in government regions and residency options are versioned; the procedure is to check each on the day and record the date in the decision memo.

Retention arrangements and authorisations are necessary, not sufficient. A BAA or a FedRAMP High boundary says what the provider does; the customer's obligations for access control, audit logging and minimum necessary data remain in the integration and are reviewed there.

The interview prompts come from a candidate report, a prep site and a vendor page. The aced.io report does not record what the candidate answered or how it was graded, and the techinterview.org prompts are illustrations, not quotes from a named loop.

Not every brief decomposes into a model project. When the first hour shows a definition or freshness problem, the right two-week plan has no model in it, and a decomposition that adds one to look like AI work has the scope wrong.
