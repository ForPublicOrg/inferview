## "We want an AI agent for support"

Scenario: the interviewer leans back and becomes a customer. "We're a logistics company. We want an AI agent for support. Can you build it?" There is no code in this round. There is a customer with a vague request, a past failure they have not mentioned yet, a legal team they will mention eventually, and a decision-maker who is not in the room. Forty minutes later the interviewer knows whether you would have built the right thing.

Applied-AI and forward-deployed loops include this kind of round, or the same skills inside a system-design round. Prep vendors describe a customer-discovery simulation in Anthropic's applied-AI loop; Anthropic has not published it, so treat the format as reported. The skills it tests are written plainly into the job descriptions, which is the better reason to practise them.

## What the job posts say the work is

Anthropic's Forward Deployed Engineer post (London, posted 14 September 2026 according to a job-board mirror) describes working inside customer systems to build production applications with Claude, and names the deliverables: MCP servers, sub-agents and agent skills. It asks for at least 4 years in a technical, customer-facing role, production experience with prompt engineering, agent development and evaluation frameworks, and travel of 25 to 50 percent. The application asks a "Why Anthropic?" question and says great answers are often 200 to 400 words.

OpenAI's Forward Deployed Software Engineer post describes embedding with strategic customers, an experiment-driven and iterative approach, and preparing scopes of work and project plans for both proof-of-concept prototypes and production deployments. It asks for 7 or more years of full-stack experience and travel of up to 50 percent.

Read together, the posts describe one loop: find out what the customer needs, scope something small enough to prove, build it inside their constraints, measure it, and plan the path to production. A discovery round tests the first two steps. The rest of the loop tests the others.

## Discovery is requirements engineering with a person who does not know the requirements

If you have run an incident review, you have the instinct. You do not open a postmortem with your theory; you ask what happened, in order, and what each person saw. Discovery works the same way. The customer's words are data, and every claim you make before you have that data costs you a question you could have asked.

The difference from an incident review is that the customer often does not know what they need. "An AI agent for support" is a solution they have heard of, not a requirement. Your job is to work back from the solution to the problem: which tickets, at what volume, measured how, failing in what way today, under which constraints, decided by whom.

Try it. The simulator below is a scripted conversation with five turns and three replies at each. Some replies uncover facts on a hidden checklist; pitches and assurances uncover nothing. The checklist appears at the end.

Play it twice: once pitching at every turn, once asking. Then look at which replies worked. Open questions about the customer's work. Asking what happened with the last tool. Asking what the rules are instead of promising they are met. Reflecting back a constraint so the customer corrects or extends it. A small, concrete next step instead of a big proposal.

## Questions that earn information

One prep vendor lists the topics a strong candidate covers in this round: how the buyer evaluates AI tools today, past deployments that failed, compliance, latency, data residency, and the workflow being replaced. Turned into questions you can actually say:

- "Which part of the work takes the most time today, and roughly how much of it is there?"
- "How do you measure that work now? What number would someone above you notice moving?"
- "Have you tried a tool for this before? What happened, and how did you find out?"
- "What data would this touch, and are there rules about where it is stored, who sees it, and what gets logged?"
- "If it is slow, what is slow? A few seconds, a minute?"
- "Who else has to say yes, and what would they need to see?"

Then reflect back. "So the volume is shipment-status questions, the measure is first-response time, the last bot quoted wrong delivery dates and nobody noticed for weeks, EU data stays in the EU, and your COO signs off. Have I got that right?" A reflection does two jobs: it shows you listened, and it gives the customer a structured statement to correct, which they will do more readily than answer another question.

Pitching is the failure mode prep guides describe for this round. It is easy to see why candidates fall into it: product knowledge feels like the thing being tested. It is not. Every minute spent on features is a minute not spent finding out which features matter.

## Scoping a vague ask into a measurable change

The second skill is turning an adjective into a number. "Make it smarter", "it feels dumb", "we need it to be more accurate" all have the same treatment.

Ask for examples. Ten to twenty conversations the customer considers bad, and what a good answer would have been for each.

Categorise the failures. Wrong facts (often retrieval), right facts but missed intent (often instructions or context), correct but unhelpful (long, vague, hedged), and unable to act (no tool or no permission). The biggest bucket is the hypothesis.

Attach a metric and a set. "Resolves 6 of 20 refund questions correctly; target 15" is something both sides can check. The numbers here are an illustration of the form, not a benchmark; agree the real target with the customer.

Change one thing and measure. Show the customer the transcripts that changed, not only the score.

The problem for this module, [the bot feels dumb](/problems/rnd-014), walks through this in full, and [the sceptical CTO role-play](/problems/rnd-015) is the discovery conversation with a harder customer.

## Does this need AI at all?

A good discovery conversation sometimes ends with "not this". The question to ask is what the task needs. If the right answer is fully determined by rules you can write down (an address change allowed before dispatch, refused after), code is cheaper, faster and auditable. If the inputs are unstructured language and an occasional wrong answer is tolerable or catchable, a model may fit. If a wrong answer is costly and hard to detect, the design needs review steps or a narrower scope before a model goes near it. Anthropic lists "do the simple thing that works" among its values; in a customer round, recommending the simpler option when it fits is a strong signal, not a lost sale.

## Explaining limits to people who do not build models

An executive asks why the feature cannot be 100 percent accurate. The answer that works avoids both jargon and false comfort. "The model writes the most likely answer given what it has read and what we gave it. Usually that is right. When the information it needs is missing or ambiguous, it can still produce a fluent answer, and fluent is not the same as correct. We cannot remove that entirely, so we measure how often it happens on real cases, design so wrong answers are caught before they cause harm, and route the risky cases to a person." Then give the measured rate on their cases, if you have one, and what happens when it is wrong.

The same structure works when a product manager wants to ship with a known failure rate: state the rate with its sample size, describe the worst realistic failure in the user's terms, and propose the mitigation and the threshold at which you would stop the launch.

## In the room

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"A customer wants an AI agent for 'support'. How do you run discovery?"
</Callout>

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>I find out what "support" means for them before proposing anything: which tickets dominate and at what volume, how they measure the work today, what went wrong with anything they tried before, what data and compliance rules apply, which actions an agent would need to take and what checks a human does first, and who signs off. I reflect it back in their words, then propose one small pilot on their real tickets with a success measure they set, and ask for the sample data and the person I need to start.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Open with their work, not my product: "Which tickets take most of your team's time?" Volume and type decide whether this is a retrieval problem (where is my parcel), an action problem (change my address), or something else.</p>
<p>Baseline next: what they measure today. If they track first-response time and satisfaction, those are the numbers a pilot has to move, and I need the current values.</p>
<p>History: what they tried before and how it failed. A previous bot that gave wrong answers nobody caught tells me evaluation and monitoring are the real requirement, and that trust has to be rebuilt.</p>
<p>Constraints: data, residency, audit, latency. In a regulated or multi-region business these shape the architecture before any prompt is written.</p>
<p>Actions and risk: anything that changes state needs the same checks a human agent does, a cut-off, and an escalation path. Reading is low risk; writing is not.</p>
<p>Decision: who signs off and what evidence they need. Then I reflect everything back, propose a pilot on a sample of their real tickets scored against their own resolutions, and name what I will and will not build in it.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>The customer's data is messy and compliance-heavy. How do you proceed?</em> Start with the subset legal will approve, redact, keep it in-region, log access, and say what the pilot cannot show because of the restriction.</li>
<li><em>What if they insist on a demo first?</em> Give a short one on their own example, then return to questions; a demo on a generic example answers nothing about their problem.</li>
<li><em>How do you know discovery is done?</em> When you can write a one-paragraph scope with a measurable success criterion and the customer agrees with every sentence.</li>
</ul>

## When this is wrong

The discovery-simulation round is described by prep vendors, not by the companies. Its length, its weight in the loop and whether it is pass or fail are all unconfirmed. Practise the skill because the job descriptions ask for it, not because of a specific format.

Some customers do want the pitch. A buyer who has already run an evaluation and is choosing between vendors may be annoyed by first-principles questions. Read the room: if they answer your first question with a detailed spec, switch to clarifying that spec and to how you would prove fit.

The simulator is a script with one customer and fixed replies. Real conversations branch further, customers contradict themselves, and the person in the room is often not the person with the answers. Use the script to practise the kind of reply that earns information, and practise with a friend playing a customer who does not cooperate.

What I would say in the room: "Before I suggest anything, I want to understand the work, how you measure it, what you have tried, and what constraints we are inside. Then I will propose the smallest pilot that would prove this on your data."
