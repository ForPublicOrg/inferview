## The unit is the round

No company in this table has one AI policy. Anthropic's default is no assistance in live interviews or take-homes, and its performance-engineering take-home explicitly allows AI. Meta's AI-enabled round, according to a vendor's account, replaces one of two onsite coding rounds and the other stays algorithmic without an assistant. Sierra's onsite build allows any tooling while its debugging pilot has not settled its AI level. OpenAI's guide says only that expectations vary by interview. A loop can therefore contain a round where an assistant is expected and a round where using one ends the process, and the rule that applies is the one stated for that round.

The costs on the two sides are not symmetric. Amazon's guidelines, as reported in March 2025, say AI use without explicit permission may lead to disqualification; Anthropic lists having Claude write code when it has not been allowed as not allowed. Not using an assistant where one is expected costs score: Canva reports that candidates with little AI experience struggled to guide the tool, and Shopify's head of engineering says candidates without a copilot usually lose to those with one. Neither quantifies it. So the safe default when a round's rule is unstated is the company's published default, and where none is published, the question goes to the recruiter in writing before the loop.

## What moved between March 2025 and August 2026

Laid out by date, the rows show a direction and a mechanism.

The direction is substitution. Canva's AI-Assisted Coding round replaced its Computer Science Fundamentals screen. Meta's AI-enabled round replaced one of two coding rounds. Sierra's onsite replaced its two coding interviews and its algorithms interview, and system design replaced the coding phone screen. Google's pilot changes the existing code comprehension round. In each case an algorithms-shaped round was converted, not supplemented, so a loop that adopts an assistant round usually has one fewer place to show unassisted algorithm work, and the remaining unassisted round carries that whole signal.

The mechanism is the take-home. Anthropic's engineering post reports that by May 2025, with Claude 3.7 Sonnet, over half of candidates on its performance take-home would have scored better by delegating to Claude Code entirely, and that each new model generation forced a redesign. An unproctored take-home cannot enforce a ban, and Canva's June 2025 post says it chose transparency over detection for the same reason. The companies that publish AI-allowed formats (Sierra, Cognition, Anthropic's take-home) also publish formats where the work is judged on decisions that the model does not make for the candidate: what to build, what to cut, how to invest in tooling.

The environment differs as much as the permission. Meta allows only the built-in assistant, and the vendor account says it answers in chat without editing files. Google's pilot names Gemini. Sierra and Cognition let the candidate bring any tool, which includes agents that edit files and run commands. What can be verified in the time, and how, depends on which of these the round provides; the first and third lessons in this module cover the chat-panel and agent cases.

## Reading a row before relying on it

The label says how a row decays. An official page changes without notice, and two in this table (Meta's careers page and OpenAI's guide) carry no date, so the only date available is when it was last read. A press report is fixed at publication and describes a document the reader cannot see; the Google row is a press account of an internal document, and the spokesperson confirmed the initiative, not each detail. A vendor guide aggregates candidate accounts and can trail the company: the vendor's Meta guide lists six languages, while Meta's page lists nine, and the official page wins.

Vendor accounts are still the only public source for some round mechanics. The Meta time budget below rests on one.

## Review cadence

This page is re-checked against every source each quarter; the next check is due on 28 January 2027. A row is re-checked early when a company publishes a new careers or engineering post about interviews, when a pilot's stated start date passes (Google's second half of 2026 is the current case), or when a reported exercise contradicts a row. ## In the room

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Your team will convert one of its two onsite coding rounds to an AI-assisted round within a quarter. Half the interviewers have never run one, and the hiring plan does not move. Which round do you convert, which tools do you allow, and how do you keep the remaining round's signal?"
</Callout>

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Convert the round whose signal is already closest to day-to-day work, usually the practical implementation round, and keep the algorithmic round unassisted, which is Meta's split as the vendor reports it. Provide one built-in assistant rather than any tool, because interviewers who have never run the format need comparable sessions to calibrate on. What I give up is the signal on how a candidate drives their own setup; Sierra and Cognition get that by allowing any tool, and they pay for it in variance between sessions.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Two constraints conflict. A quarter with half the panel inexperienced argues for the most controlled format. The reason to convert at all, observing how a candidate works with the tools they will use on the job, argues for the least controlled.</p>
<p>The round to convert is the one where an assistant changes what is observed least destructively. In an algorithms round the assistant can produce the core idea, and the round stops measuring it. In a practical build round the assistant produces code, and the round shifts to measuring decomposition and verification, which is what the published formats say they score. So the build round converts and the algorithms round stays as the unassisted signal.</p>
<p>For tooling, a single built-in assistant with a fixed model menu gives every candidate the same capabilities, which lets new interviewers compare sessions. I would write that choice down as a decision record with its cost: the format does not observe agent use, and if the role is agent-heavy that gap is real.</p>
<p>Calibration comes before rollout: interviewers run the new problem with the assistant themselves, pairs shadow before running it alone, and the written rubric names verification behaviours that can be observed, such as a test written from the spec before accepting generated code. The failure mode is that the converted round drifts back into an algorithms round with a chat window attached, because the problem was not changed to make the assistant's output something that has to be checked.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>Two senior interviewers disagree: one wants any-tool, one wants the built-in assistant. How do you decide without deciding by rank?</em> Run both on the same problem with internal volunteers, compare how far apart interviewer scores land for the same session, and pick the format where they agree more; record the result with the decision.</li>
<li><em>An unassisted take-home is also in the loop. What do you do about it?</em> Anthropic's post reports models outscoring most candidates on its take-home; an unproctored ban is not enforceable, so either allow AI and redesign the problem, or move the signal into a live round.</li>
<li><em>What would make you convert the second round too?</em> Evidence from the first quarter that the unassisted round adds no signal the converted round lacks, measured on the same candidates, not interviewer preference.</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

## When this is wrong

Every row is a snapshot. Several sources are undated, four rows rest on press accounts of documents or interviews rather than on policy pages, and one rests on a vendor. A company can change a round between the check date and an interview, and pilots, by definition, change. The recruiter's written statement for the specific round overrides this table.

The table also lists only companies that have said something in public. Most employers have published nothing, and the absence of a row is not a policy. And the labels flatten detail that matters inside a round: "allowed" can mean a built-in chat panel that cannot edit files or an agent with a shell, and those are different rounds to prepare for even when the row reads the same.
