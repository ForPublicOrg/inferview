## What the round measures

A reconciliation round asks you to pair one table with another: receipts with card transactions, invoices with payments, shipments with carrier events. Writing the join takes minutes in any notebook. The work that distinguishes answers is everything around it: a written definition of a match, a matcher that respects one-to-one constraints, a measured statement of how often it is wrong, and a report of what it left unmatched and why.

The one public account of such a round in an AI-engineer loop is a candidate report on aced.io about Ramp, with the interview roughly three months before this lesson's check date. The candidate expected a CodeSignal exercise and got a Google Colab session involving data analysis. The questions listed include receipt reconciliation with AI guiding the implementation, and using transformers to embed receipts and transactions. The report gives no data, matching rule or duration, and the candidate was not hired, so nothing here reconstructs the exercise; the data and rules below are built for this lesson.

## Defining a match before writing code

The definition is a policy with three parts: which fields must agree, how closely, and whether the mapping is one-to-one. Ramp's help centre publishes the rule its product uses for automatic receipt verification, which is a useful reference point because it is a shipped rule with documented failure cases. The amount must match, and either the date or the merchant must match. A receipt date within one calendar day either side of the transaction date counts as a date match.

The same documentation lists where that rule breaks. A receipt sent before its charge appears may attach to the closest existing charge from the same vendor, which can be days or weeks old. Two adjacent same-vendor charges of the same amount can be matched either way round. Amazon order confirmations do not auto-match because the amount can change at shipment. Receipts forwarded through several hops lose the structured data the matcher uses.

Exact amount equality fails in at least three common ways, each with a different fix.

**Tips.** Visa's 2019 guidance for restaurant merchants allows merchant category codes 5812, 5813 and 5814 to add up to 20% to the authorization amount without liability for authorization-related disputes, and tells merchants not to include an estimated tip in the authorization. An itemised receipt shows the bill before the tip; the posted charge includes it. A tip band for those category codes is the fix: the charge may exceed the receipt by up to the band, and only for those codes.

**Currency.** A receipt in euros and a charge in dollars agree only through the exchange rate on the posting date, which the notebook does not have unless you fetch it. Without it, these pairs are unmatched with a reason, not guessed.

**Splits.** One order, several charges, as Ramp's Amazon note implies. This is a one-to-many match. A one-to-one matcher should not claim it; it should report the candidates.

The disjunction in "date or merchant" trades precision for recall. Amount plus date with an unrelated merchant accepts coincidences: two round-number purchases on the same day at different shops. Requiring the merchant always closes that hole and loses receipts whose merchant name differs from the card descriptor. Which side to give up is the decision the round is about, and it depends on the cost of each error, which the next sections make concrete.

## Matching is an assignment, not a filter

Filtering each receipt's candidates independently lets two receipts claim one charge. The matcher has to assign: score every candidate pair, take pairs best-first across the whole table, and skip any pair whose receipt or charge is already taken. Processing receipts in file order instead lets an early receipt take a charge that a later receipt fits better. When a receipt's two best candidates score the same, the matcher abstains, because a coin flip between two identical taxi fares is a wrong match half the time and a human can resolve it in seconds.

Candidate generation is a blocking step: amount and date windows cut the pair space before any expensive comparison runs. That is where the reported "embed receipts and transactions" fits. An embedding model is one way to score merchant similarity between "Blue Fern Cafe" and "BLUEFERN CAFE", and it belongs on the blocked candidate pairs, not on the full cross product. The matcher below uses `difflib` string similarity so it runs in the browser; swapping in an embedding comparison changes one function.

The score itself (merchant similarity, minus a penalty per day of gap, minus the relative amount difference) is a policy choice with arbitrary weights. Its only job is to order candidates, and the labelled sample is what tells you whether the ordering is good.

## Measuring on a labelled sample

On real data you cannot label every pair. Precision is estimated from a random sample of the automatic matches; recall needs labels on receipts drawn from the whole population, including those the matcher left alone, because a matcher that is never wrong about the pairs it claims can still miss half the pairs that exist.

## Reporting what remains unmatched

The output has two sides. Receipts with no charge, grouped by reason with a count and one example each: no candidate in the window, foreign currency, possible split, ambiguous tie, candidate taken by a better pair. Charges with no receipt, which in an expense system are the items someone has to chase. A reason column turns the unmatched pile from a failure rate into a work queue, and it shows where the next rule change would pay: if most unmatched receipts are foreign-currency, an exchange-rate lookup recovers them; if most are ties, the matcher needs another field such as a card's last four digits or a timestamp.

The asymmetry between the two error types sets the threshold. An unmatched receipt goes to a person, who resolves it at a known cost. A wrong match is silent: the receipt is filed against the wrong charge, and the right charge now looks covered, so a missing receipt is hidden rather than surfaced. In an expense or audit setting that usually makes precision the constraint and recall the objective, which is why the stricter rule in the code cell is the better default even though it matches one fewer receipt.

## When this is wrong

The single public report of a notebook round names receipts and transactions and nothing more. Other loops may use the notebook for exploratory analysis, metric definition or model evaluation, where the matcher framing does not apply; what carries over is writing the definition before the code and reporting what the analysis cannot see.

Ramp's rule and the Visa tip allowance are reference points, not truths about your data. The 20% figure is Visa's, for three restaurant codes, in a 2019 document. Mastercard sets its own tolerance, and Toast's support page on the Mastercard card-not-present rule (updated 20 October 2026) says 30% in its title and body and 20% in its URL and a subheading, which is a fair picture of how reliable secondary summaries of these rules are. A band copied without checking the network's own rule for the data in front of you can be wrong in both directions.

The lesson assumes labels are cheap enough to sample a few hundred pairs. When they are not, the measurement is the bottleneck, and a smaller matcher with fewer paths, each sampled properly, beats a cleverer one whose error rate nobody knows.

The one-to-one assumption fails for splits, refunds and partial shipments. Extending the assignment to one-to-many is a subset-sum search over candidate charges, which grows quickly with the window; the split check in the cell only looks at pairs, and says so.
