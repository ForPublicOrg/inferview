## What the round tests

A candidate report from a Cohere staff machine learning engineer loop, about five months old at the check date, describes a stage where the candidate presents a paper chosen from a list the company provides. The same report lists the technical rounds as decoding tokens with top-k sampling without PyTorch and designing knowledge distillation and an LLM post-training pipeline. It does not state the presentation's length or the questions that followed, so the structure below is this lesson's, sized to 20 minutes and scaled to whatever the invitation says.

The round is a design review with someone else's design. Four things are on trial: whether you can state the problem the paper solves and why earlier approaches fell short; whether you can explain the mechanism in terms an engineer could implement; whether you know what the evidence shows under its stated conditions and where it stops; and whether you can say what changed in practice because of the paper, and what it would change for the team in front of you. The difference from a summary is the third item. A summary repeats the headline number. An account at staff level says what the number assumes, what it was compared against, and which of those assumptions fails in a setting the panel cares about.

## A 20-minute structure

The split is by section of the argument, not by section of the paper.

| Minutes | Segment | What it must contain |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 2 | Problem | The task, the prior best approach and the specific way it fell short |
| 2 to 8 | Mechanism | The method as an algorithm with its inputs, outputs and the one design choice that makes it work |
| 8 to 14 | Evidence | The key table or figure, the metric's definition, the baseline, what is held fixed, the conditions |
| 14 to 17 | Limits | What the evidence does not cover, stated by the authors and found by you |
| 17 to 20 | Consequence | What changed in practice, and what you would do with it here |

The mechanism gets six minutes because it is the part an implementer needs, and the evidence gets six because it is where a reading gets tested. The limits segment is short but carries the most weight per minute: it is where you show that you checked the paper rather than trusted it.

Choosing from a provided list is a decision with three inputs. Pick a paper whose central number you can recompute from its own tables, because that is the strongest form of evidence you can bring. Prefer one whose evidence has conditions you can examine, such as a metric with a nonstandard definition, a baseline that was or was not tuned, or a cost accounting choice. And prefer one close to the work implied by the rest of the loop: in the reported Cohere loop, decoding and post-training. If no paper on the list meets all three, the first one matters most.

## Reading versus understanding, worked on one paper

Large Language Monkeys (Brown and colleagues, arXiv 2407.21787, v3 December 2024) is a good rehearsal paper because its headline is clean and its conditions are easy to miss. It is also the subject of a [walkthrough on this site](/learn/harness/verification/parallel-attempts).

**The problem.** Inference compute is usually spent on one attempt per problem. The paper asks what happens if you spend it on many independent attempts and pick among them.

**The mechanism.** Sample k candidate solutions per problem from the same prompt at a nonzero temperature, then select. The paper measures coverage: the fraction of problems solved by any of the k samples. For code it checks with the unit tests, for formal proofs with the Lean4 checker, and for GSM8K and MATH with an oracle that knows the correct final answer. It estimates coverage with the unbiased pass@k estimator.

**The headline.** The abstract reports SWE-bench Lite issues solved with DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct rising from 15.9% with one sample to 56% with 250 samples, beating a single-sample state of the art of 43%.

The questions that separate reading from understanding are about what that comparison holds fixed.

*What is the metric?* 56% is coverage: an issue counts if any of 250 attempts passes. The 43% it is compared with is a single submitted attempt. The comparison is fair only if a deployed system can tell which of its 250 attempts is right, and on SWE-bench the check is the benchmark's own tests, which a real deployment does not have. The paper is explicit that this works where answers can be verified automatically; the headline comparison reads as if it holds generally.

*What does the evidence say about selection without a verifier?* The abstract says majority voting and reward models plateau beyond several hundred samples. Section 4.1 and the caption of Figure 7 put the plateau around or before 100 samples on GSM8K and MATH. Reading the body, not just the abstract, gives you the tighter number and a question to ask about why the summaries differ.

*How good is the verifier?* The paper reports that 34 of the 300 SWE-bench Lite problems have flaky tests, 30 of them flaky even on the dataset's correct solutions, and that 35 of 122 CodeContests problems have correct solutions that fail the tests. Coverage measured by a noisy verifier is bounded by the verifier.

*What is the cost accounting?* Table 1 compares spending on five cheap attempts with one expensive attempt.

**The limits the authors state.** Section 5 lists them: attempts are independent and identical in prompt and hyperparameters, diversity comes only from temperature, CodeContests and MiniF2F use single-turn generation without execution feedback, earlier attempts are not reused, and verifying unstructured outputs is an open problem. Section 2.3 calls FLOPs a crude cost metric.

**What changed in practice.** The consequence to argue is narrower than the headline: on tasks with a trustworthy automatic check, attempts are a resource you can trade against model size, and the verifier, not the sampler, becomes the component to invest in. On tasks without one, the paper's own body says selection stops improving early.

## The estimator, because someone will ask

The pass@k estimator is the one place the paper's evidence depends on a formula, and recomputing it is the quickest way to show you read the method section. With n samples of which c are correct, the probability that a random subset of k contains at least one correct sample is one minus the probability that all k come from the n − c incorrect ones.

```python
from math import comb

def pass_at_k(n: int, c: int, k: int) -> float:
    """Unbiased estimate of P(at least one of k samples is correct), given c correct out of n."""
    if n - c < k:
        return 1.0
    return 1.0 - comb(n - c, k) / comb(n, k)

# Toy case: 10 samples, 2 correct.
print(pass_at_k(10, 2, 1))   # 0.2
print(pass_at_k(10, 2, 5))   # 1 - comb(8, 5) / comb(10, 5) = 1 - 56 / 252 = 0.778
```

Two correct samples in ten gives pass@1 of 0.2 and pass@5 of 0.778. Nothing in the formula selects the right sample; it assumes an oracle that recognises it. That is the same condition as the headline, visible in five lines.

## When this is wrong

The evidence about the round is one candidate report. It says the paper came from a provided list and gives nothing about length, audience or questions. Other companies may ask for a paper of your choice, a paper you wrote, or a reading discussion with no presentation at all; the structure here assumes a talk and a panel that has read the paper.

The 20-minute split assumes the paper has one central mechanism and one decisive table. Survey papers, benchmark papers and position papers do not; for those the evidence segment becomes a critique of the methodology and the mechanism segment shrinks.

The emphasis on recomputing numbers assumes the paper reports enough to recompute them. Many do not, and an honest "I could not reproduce this from what is published" is a finding, not a gap in preparation.

Finally, the advice to pick a paper close to the company's work assumes you can read that work from the rest of the loop. A list provided by the company may be chosen precisely to be outside it, to see how you read unfamiliar material.
