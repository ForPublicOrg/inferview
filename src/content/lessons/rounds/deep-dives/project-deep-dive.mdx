## The decision

A project deep dive asks you to choose one piece of past work and defend it under questioning. Two decisions are made before the round: which project, and how to cut the presentation so the questions land on material you can back with evidence. Both depend on the probe list, because the presentation is the shortest part of the round and the probes are where it is scored.

The probe list is consistent across the reports this lesson draws on. Hello Interview's OpenAI L5 guide, built from aggregated candidate reports, lists why the work was needed and what was hard, which decisions were yours, the alternatives you considered and why you rejected them, what went wrong during delivery, the impact including whether other teams had to adopt the work or change their plans, and what you would do differently now. A candidate report from an Anthropic machine learning engineer loop, about a year old at the check date, adds probes that matter for model work in particular: why machine learning was the right tool for the problem and how you knew, whether a business user was in the loop, whether people adopted it, how long it took to reach production, and what the budget was.

Read together, the probes ask for six things: the alternatives considered, your part as distinct from the team's, the measured result and how it was measured, what went wrong, whether anyone adopted it, and what you would undo. A project whose answers to all six are on record (a design document with rejected options, a dashboard, a postmortem, a migration that other teams ran) is the one to present, even if a larger project sounds better in the first minute.

## The inputs that change it

**The format's length.** Reported formats span an order of magnitude. Hello Interview reports a roughly 30-minute presentation followed by detailed questions at OpenAI, and one account of 15 minutes of questions inside a one-hour round. A candidate report from a senior forward deployed engineer loop at Cognition, about a month old at the check date, describes a take-home explained in a 5-minute Loom video and a separate round to present past work. A Sierra candidate report from about two years before the check date describes a 5 to 10 minute talk on a technical topic of the candidate's choice, after a walkthrough of the take-home's design choices. Sierra's April 2026 post describes the current onsite differently: the Review phase is a demo and a debate of product flows, code, the path to production and how AI was used, on work built that day. Synthesia's staff posting, published 27 August 2026, lists a 60-minute Delivery and Impact Interview as a deep dive into past projects, impact and ways of working, before any technical round.

**Live or recorded.** A recorded walkthrough is watched without you, so every claim must carry its own evidence on screen. A live presentation can leave evidence for the probes, provided you know where it is.

**Your past work or a take-home.** For a take-home walkthrough the project is chosen for you and the decision is only the cut. For past work, the choice of project is most of the outcome.

**Your ownership.** Hello Interview's guide advises a project you helped define and lead over a larger system where you owned a small piece. The "which decisions were yours" probe is answered badly from a project where the decisions were made above you.

**What you can show.** Confidentiality removes numbers and screenshots. A project whose result you can only state as "it went well" fails the measured-result probe regardless of how good it was. Decide before the round which numbers you can disclose, as ranges or relative changes if necessary, and say so when you present them.

**The level.** At senior level the probes stay inside the project. At staff level, and in Synthesia's posting where the deep dive sits under "delivery and impact", the adoption probe widens: which teams changed their plans because of your work, and what you did to make that happen.

## The options and when each wins

**The project you defined and led end to end.** Usually smaller than the largest thing you worked on. Wins in most rounds, because every probe has an answer from your own memory and records: you made the decisions, you saw the alternatives, you know what broke. It loses when it is so small that "what changed in the organisation" has no answer, which matters most in a delivery and impact round at staff level.

**The largest system you contributed to.** Wins only when your part is a separable component with its own decisions and its own measured result, such as the evaluation harness for a migration or the retrieval layer of a product. It loses when your part has to be explained through the team's decisions, because every answer to the "which decisions were yours" probe then has to separate your part from the team's after the fact.

**A project that failed or was reversed.** Wins when the reversal itself was a decision you made on measured evidence: you stopped a model rollout because the eval showed a regression, or you replaced a model with rules because the volume did not justify it. The Anthropic probe "how did you know it was the right use case" has its clearest answer in a project where you concluded it was not. It loses in a round framed around delivery, where a reversal with nothing shipped leaves the impact probe empty.

**A take-home walkthrough.** No choice of project; the decision is the cut. In a 5-minute recording the walkthrough has room for the problem in one sentence, the one design decision with the alternative you rejected, the evidence that it works (a test run or an eval result on screen), what you cut and why, and what you would do next. Everything else in the build goes unshown unless a later round asks for it.

## Cutting the talk to the format

For a 30-minute presentation, a split that maps onto the probes: 3 minutes on the problem and why it needed solving then; 5 minutes on the constraint that made it hard and the alternatives you rejected, with the reason for each; 10 minutes on the design, marking which decisions were yours; 7 minutes on the result and how it was measured; 5 minutes on what went wrong and what you would undo. The proportions are this lesson's choice. For a 10-minute talk scale each segment by a third and drop the design detail first, because it is the part the questions will reopen anyway.

The measured-result segment is where most deep dives are thinnest. A before-and-after number without its sample size and its noise is a claim, not a result, and the probe "how do you know" is the predictable next question.

## Worked decision: two candidate projects

Scenario (constructed for this lesson): a senior engineer has a 60-minute deep dive with a 30-minute presentation, and two candidate projects.

Project A is a company-wide move from keyword search to hybrid retrieval for a support assistant, run by three teams over two quarters. The engineer built the offline evaluation harness: a labelled query set, recall and answer-quality metrics, and the CI gate that blocked two releases. The headline result, a resolution-rate improvement, belongs to the programme.

Project B is a ticket-triage classifier the engineer proposed, built and launched over six weeks. It replaced keyword rules for one queue. It was adopted by two of the five support teams it was offered to; the other three kept the rules because their queues were small. The engineer has the design document with two rejected options (fine-tuning a small model, and keeping rules with a weekly review), a shadow-mode comparison, and a postmortem from a week when a taxonomy change silently broke the label mapping.

Run each against the six probes. For A, alternatives and the measured result belong mostly to the programme; the engineer's own part (the harness) has a good "which decisions were yours" answer and a strong "what went wrong" answer in the two blocked releases, but the result probe drifts to a number the engineer did not produce. For B, every probe has a recorded answer, including an unflattering one: three of five teams declined it, and the reason (queue volume below the point where a model was worth maintaining) is itself the answer to "how did you know it was the right use case".

The decision is B for a senior loop, presented with the adoption gap stated in the result segment rather than left for the questions. A is the better choice in one case: a staff loop where the round is about cross-team delivery, if the presentation is reframed around the harness as the thing three teams depended on and the two blocked releases as its measured effect.

## What to measure after deciding

Rehearse against the probe list, timed, with someone who asks the probes in a random order and pushes on each answer once. Record three things. The share of probes you answered by pointing at an artefact (a document, a chart, a commit, a postmortem) rather than from memory. The time spent on the design segment against your plan, since it is the segment that overruns. And every probe where your answer used "we" for a decision you cannot attribute; those are the answers that change the outcome of a "which decisions were yours" follow-up.

</Callout>

<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The interval included zero, so the launch rested on the cost side, not on the lift: the model was cheaper to maintain than the rules it replaced and shadow mode showed no regression on the high-severity queue. I would ship again on those grounds, but I would have stated them as the reason instead of the lift, and I would have run the experiment paired on the same tickets to get a tighter interval in the same two weeks.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Two constraints were in conflict: a fixed launch date and an experiment too small to separate a 3-point lift from noise at 1,200 tickets per arm, where the 95% half-width is about 3.9 points. Extending the experiment was not available without moving the date, which the team had committed to another group.</p>
<p>The launch was defensible on other evidence. The rules needed weekly manual review; the classifier did not. Shadow mode on the high-severity queue showed no case where the model routed a ticket the rules would have escalated. So the decision was "no worse on the measured risk, cheaper to run", and the lift was a hoped-for bonus. That is what the launch note should have said.</p>
<p>With the same two weeks, I would have scored both systems on the same tickets offline from the logged traffic, which removes ticket-to-ticket variance from the comparison, and kept the online test for the operational metrics. I would also have written a rollback trigger: a rise in reopened tickets above the pre-launch baseline for a week. What I give up by reframing is the headline number on the slide, which I could not defend anyway.</p>

## When this is wrong

The evidence base is thin. Hello Interview's guide aggregates candidate reports without dates or attribution per claim. The Cognition and Anthropic reports are single accounts on a third-party site. The Sierra candidate report describes a loop from about two years before the check date, and Sierra's own April 2026 post describes a different onsite. Only the Synthesia posting is an official description, and it names a 60-minute round without saying how it is run. Formats change faster than these pages; the invitation you receive overrides all of them, and the length in it decides the cut.

The playbook assumes the round is scored on the probes. Some deep dives are mostly a conversation about your career with a few technical questions, as the Sierra candidate report describes for its walkthrough round; in that case breadth across projects can matter more than depth in one, and the single-project preparation is over-invested.

It also assumes you can show evidence. Under strict confidentiality the measured-result segment shrinks to relative changes and method, and the round shifts weight to the design and failure probes. Choose the project whose design story survives without numbers in that case, and state at the start which numbers you cannot share.
