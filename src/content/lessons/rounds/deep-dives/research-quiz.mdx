## The format and how old the evidence is

Some research-engineer loops have used short questions in probability, statistics and machine learning, each followed by a question asking why the answer holds. The public evidence for the format is thin and old, and this lesson uses it for the shape of the questions rather than as a description of any current loop.

The source is a Blind post from 7 April 2021 by an engineer describing a DeepMind research engineer process. The author says the quiz stage had by then been replaced with two coding screens and two ML screens; a commenter in August 2021 said the quiz still ran depending on whether the role was at DeepMind or Google Research, and described it as two hours. The ML screens in the post carry the pattern this lesson trains. The depth screen opened with probability, including a balls-in-urns Bayes question, and the author's advice was that you need the mathematical intuition as well as the answer. In the breadth screen, the author reports at least 7 to 8 constraints added over every solution. And on L1 versus L2 regularisation, saying that L1 gives sparse features was not enough: the interviewer wanted to know how the gradients act on the weights to produce the sparsity.

That last example defines the card. A recalled fact ("L1 is sparse") scores nothing; the mechanism behind it, shown on numbers, is the answer, and the follow-up pushes on the mechanism with a new constraint.

## The anatomy of a card

A card has four parts. A stem with every input needed to derive a number, so the answer is computed and not recalled. A time target, which forces the arithmetic shortcuts that the follow-up then asks you to justify. A typed answer with a tolerance, because a vague verbal answer hides the step that went wrong. And an intuition follow-up that either asks why the number comes out as it does or changes one condition and asks what happens.

The follow-up is the part to practise, and the L1 case shows why. Take one weight at 0.03, a learning rate of 0.1 and an L1 coefficient of 0.5. The soft-thresholding (proximal) step subtracts learning rate times coefficient, 0.05, from the weight's magnitude and clips at zero: max(0, 0.03 − 0.05) = 0. The same weight under an L2 penalty (λ/2)w² is multiplied by 1 − 0.1 × 0.5 = 0.95 per step, which never reaches zero and takes ln(30) / −ln(0.95) ≈ 67 steps just to fall below 0.001. The reason is the shape of the gradient: L1 pushes with constant magnitude λ whatever the weight's size, L2 pushes in proportion to the weight. That sentence, plus the arithmetic, is the answer the 2021 report says was wanted.

The first constraint an interviewer can add is the optimiser. Plain stochastic gradient descent with the L1 subgradient added to the loss gradient does not produce exact zeros: the weight crosses zero, the sign of the subgradient flips, and it oscillates around zero. McMahan and colleagues state this directly in Google's 2013 ad click prediction paper and list FOBOS and truncated gradient as methods that do produce sparsity. The sparsity comes from the proximal step, not from the penalty alone.

```python
def sign(x):
    return (x > 0) - (x < 0)

def soft(x, t):
    return sign(x) * max(0.0, abs(x) - t)

lr, lam, target = 0.1, 0.05, 0.02     # data loss 0.5 * (w - target)**2
w_sub = w_prox = w_l2 = 0.3
for step in range(1, 201):
    w_sub = w_sub - lr * ((w_sub - target) + lam * sign(w_sub))       # L1 by subgradient
    w_prox = soft(w_prox - lr * (w_prox - target), lr * lam)          # L1 by proximal step
    w_l2 = w_l2 - lr * ((w_l2 - target) + lam * w_l2)                 # L2, penalty (lam/2) w**2
    if step in (50, 199, 200):
        print(f"step {step}: subgradient {w_sub:+.5f}  proximal {w_prox:+.5f}  L2 {w_l2:+.5f}")
```

Run in Python 3.12, it prints:

```
step 50: subgradient -0.00015  proximal +0.00000  L2 +0.02014
step 199: subgradient +0.00321  proximal +0.00000  L2 +0.01905
step 200: subgradient -0.00011  proximal +0.00000  L2 +0.01905
```

The data term pulls the weight toward 0.02. Because 0.02 is inside the L1 threshold, the L1 optimum is exactly zero; the proximal iterate reaches it and stays, while the subgradient iterate flips sign between steps 199 and 200. L2 settles at 0.02 / 1.05 = 0.01905, shrunk but never zero. A second constraint (the data streams and each feature appears rarely) moves the answer to a lazy per-coordinate update such as FTRL-Proximal, which stores one number per coefficient and sets a weight to zero whenever its accumulated gradient is inside the threshold.

The cards span maths, statistics, computer science and ML. Two of them are traps in production as much as in interviews. The cross-entropy card's follow-up (a first logged loss of 25 against a uniform-output value of ln 32,768 = 10.4 nats) is the check to run at step 0 of any training job. The Adam card's follow-up has a number worth computing rather than remembering: without bias correction the update is inflated by (1 − β1^t) / sqrt(1 − β2^t), which does not fall monotonically from step 1.

```python
from math import sqrt
b1, b2 = 0.9, 0.999
for t in (1, 10, 100, 1000, 5000):
    print(t, round((1 - b1**t) / sqrt(1 - b2**t), 2))
# 1 3.16 / 10 6.53 / 100 3.24 / 1000 1.26 / 5000 1.0
```

The first moment fills in about 1 / (1 − β1) = 10 steps and the second in about 1 / (1 − β2) = 1,000, so the ratio rises to about 6.5 near step 10 before decaying. A model trained without correction and a high learning rate takes its largest relative steps in the first few dozen updates.

## Building your own deck

The site's review schedule handles repetition: rate this lesson at the end and it comes back when due. The deck itself is better written than collected, because writing the stem forces you to choose inputs that make the answer derivable. Four rules keep the cards honest.

Every stem carries its inputs. "What is the KV cache for this model?" is recall; "32 layers, 8 KV heads of dimension 128, bf16, 8,192 tokens" is a card, and its answer, 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 bytes × 8,192 = 1 GiB, is computed.

Every follow-up changes one thing. Double the context, switch the optimiser, make the data stream, halve the labelling budget. The breadth screen in the 2021 report stacked constraints on each solution; a card with a static follow-up trains the first answer only.

Time targets are set from your own runs, not from a guess about the room. Time a first pass, then set the target at the time you take when you know the method, and track how many cards you answer correctly inside it.

Tolerances are explicit. A card that accepts "about 4" for a half-width of 3.92 points is training the right habit; a card with no tolerance turns into a recall test of the exact digits.

## In the room

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"You train a logistic model over two million sparse features with SGD and an L1 penalty. Serving can hold at most ten thousand non-zero weights, retraining must finish in an hour, and the data arrives as a stream you see once. Your model has almost no exact zeros. What do you change, and what do you give up?"
</Callout>

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The penalty is not the problem; the optimiser is. Subgradient SGD oscillates around zero instead of landing on it, so I switch to a proximal or FTRL-Proximal update that zeroes a weight whenever its accumulated gradient is inside the threshold, then tune the L1 coefficient until the non-zero count fits ten thousand. I give up some accuracy to the coefficient that hits the cap, and I measure how much on held-out data.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Two constraints conflict: a hard cap on non-zero weights for serving, and a single pass over a stream within an hour, which rules out batch solvers such as coordinate descent over the full data.</p>
<p>The almost-no-zeros symptom is the subgradient behaviour: the weight crosses zero, the subgradient's sign flips and it never settles. A proximal step (soft-thresholding after each update) fixes it. With two million features and sparse examples, applying the threshold to every coordinate on every step wastes the hour, so the update has to be lazy, touching only the coordinates present in the example. FTRL-Proximal is built for this: it keeps one accumulated number per coefficient and solves each coordinate in closed form, returning exactly zero when that number is inside λ1. Google's 2013 paper reports, on its own prototyping data, that FOBOS kept 38% more non-zero coefficients than FTRL-Proximal with no AucLoss detriment, and RDA 3% more with a 0.6% detriment.</p>
<p>The cap is then met by tuning λ1, and that is where the cost lands: a coefficient large enough for ten thousand non-zeros may cost accuracy that a twenty-thousand budget would not. I would show the accuracy against non-zero count curve on held-out data and put the cap's cost in writing. A hard post-hoc truncation to the top ten thousand by magnitude is the fallback if the curve is bad, with the risk that magnitude is a poor proxy for importance when features have different scales.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li>The curve shows the ten-thousand cap costs more accuracy than the product will accept, and serving memory cannot grow this quarter. Which do you push back on, and what do you accept in writing?</li>
<li>Feature scales differ by three orders of magnitude. What does that do to a single λ1, and what would you need to see before trusting the selected features?</li>
<li>A colleague argues for hashing the features into ten thousand buckets instead of L1. What would you need to see in an offline comparison to switch?</li>
</ul>

## When this is wrong

The source is one candidate report from 2021, and its author says the quiz stage had been replaced by then. It describes one company's process at one time, and a commenter's account of a quiz depending on the hiring organisation is unverified. Any loop you enter in 2026 may have no quiz at all. The cards are useful whatever the format because the follow-up pattern (answer, then why, then a changed condition) recurs in ML screens and deep dives; the format itself is not something to plan around.

Timed cards reward speed on derivations you have rehearsed. They do not train the slower skill of working an unfamiliar problem aloud, and a deck that grows to hundreds of memorised stems turns back into recall. Keep the deck small and rewrite cards when the answers start arriving before the arithmetic.

The L1 discussion assumes a convex model. In deep networks trained with Adam-style optimisers, the L1 subgradient is added to the gradient like any other term and then rescaled per parameter, so the oscillation above applies and exact zeros need an explicit proximal or pruning step; structured sparsity and pruning are separate topics with their own trade-offs.
