## Twelve minutes on the wrong part

Scenario: "Tell me about a challenging AI project." The candidate describes the company, the team, the product, the data, the model they inherited, and the politics around it. Twelve minutes in, the interviewer asks "and what did you do?" and there are three minutes left. The project was good. The interviewer writes down "unclear ownership, no results".

Behavioural rounds are scored on evidence, and they run out of time. The cure is preparation that feels excessive: a handful of true stories, each written out, each in three lengths, each with a number and a failure in it.

## STAR is a commit message for your career

Amazon's own interview guide calls the STAR method the cornerstone of its behavioural interviews: situation, task, action, result. It adds two instructions that carry over to every company: say "I" for your own contribution, and include precise metrics. Amazon also asks candidates to connect stories to its Leadership Principles, of which its current page lists 16, from Customer Obsession to Success and Scale Bring Broad Responsibility.

A good commit message has the same shape. One line of context so the reviewer knows why the change exists, a clear statement of what changed, and evidence it works. Nobody wants three paragraphs about the history of the module. The difference is that in a story you are the change being reviewed, so the action has to be yours and specific enough to be checked by follow-up questions.

Proportions matter more than format. Situation and task together should be short: who the users were, what hurt, what you owned. Action takes most of the time: the decisions you made, the options you rejected, how you knew it was working. Result closes with a number against a baseline, how it was measured, and what went wrong.

## Check your own draft

Write one story into the builder. The checks are simple text rules for gaps that invite hard follow-up questions: a result without a number, "we" where the interviewer needs "I", a situation longer than the action, no failure, no rejected alternative. They cannot tell you whether the story is good. They can tell you where a follow-up question will land.

Then say it aloud with a timer. A story that reads well can still run long when spoken, and the time you actually take is the number that matters in the room.

## Three lengths for every story

Prepare each story the way this site prepares interview answers: a 30-second version, a 3-minute version, and the material for follow-ups. The 30-second version is for "give me the short version" and for the opening of a project deep-dive. The 3-minute version is the default answer. The follow-up material is what you need when the interviewer starts digging: the alternatives you rejected, the cost, the numbers behind the numbers, what you would do differently.

For an AI-engineering loop, five or six stories cover most questions:

- A shipped LLM feature, with how you evaluated it and what failed after launch.
- A trade-off you made under a constraint (accuracy against latency, quality against cost, scope against a deadline).
- A time you were confident and wrong.
- A time you pushed back on a stakeholder, for example about whether an AI feature was feasible or safe.
- A conflict with a colleague and how it ended.
- A time you decided not to use a model, or chose the simpler approach.

Each story can answer several questions. "Tell me about a failure" and "tell me about a time you were wrong" can share one, told with a different emphasis.

## Values and mission rounds

AI labs add rounds about values and mission, and the published material is thin, so prepare from primary sources. Anthropic's careers page lists seven values: act for the global good; hold light and shade; be good to our users; ignite a race to the top on safety; do the simple thing that works; be helpful, honest, and harmless; and put the mission first. OpenAI's interview guide says it looks for "collaboration, effective communication, openness to feedback, and alignment with our mission and values."

Prep vendors, drawing on candidate reports, describe Anthropic values-round themes such as why Anthropic, moral conflict, pressure from executives, hard feedback, and times the candidate was wrong, and report that the round may be run by non-technical interviewers. Reported questions include what concerns you have about the company's mission or direction, and how you balance delivery speed against security. Treat those lists as practice prompts, not as the company's question bank.

Two habits help. Read the company's own statements and have a considered view, including where you disagree, because "what concerns you about our mission?" rewards a specific, thoughtful concern over praise. And connect your stories to stated values only where the connection is real; "do the simple thing that works" maps naturally onto a story where you chose a heuristic over a model, and forcing every story onto a value list sounds rehearsed.

The written application counts too. Anthropic's forward-deployed post asks a "Why Anthropic?" question and says great answers are often 200 to 400 words; its candidate guidance asks applicants to write the first draft themselves and then refine with Claude if they wish.

## In the room

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Tell me about a time you were confident and wrong."
</Callout>

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Pick a real, technical case where you held a position firmly and the evidence proved you wrong. Shape, as a scenario: "I was sure our retrieval misses were an embedding-model problem and argued for switching models. I agreed to label 50 failures first. Most were documents missing from the index because of an ingestion filter I had written. I fixed the filter, recall on our set went up, and I now label failures before proposing fixes." The parts that score: you stated a clear belief, you tested it, you changed your mind on evidence, and the habit changed afterwards.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Choose the right story. It should be a real mistake with real stakes, not a disguised strength, and ideally one where your own work was part of the cause. Technical examples suit an engineering loop: an architecture you championed, a model choice, a diagnosis.</p>
<p>Show the confidence honestly. Say what you believed and why it seemed right at the time. Interviewers are checking that you can hold a view, not that you never have one.</p>
<p>Show how you found out. The best version is one where you set up the test that proved you wrong: you proposed labelling failures, running the eval, or asking the user. That turns the story from luck into method.</p>
<p>Own the cost. If the mistake cost the team a week, say so.</p>
<p>End with the change in your behaviour, specific enough to check: "I now label twenty failures before I propose a fix", not "I learned to be humble".</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li><em>How did you tell the people you had argued with?</em> Directly, with the evidence, and with credit to anyone who had disagreed.</li>
<li><em>When would you hold your position against pushback?</em> When the evidence supports it; say what evidence would change your mind, and go and get it.</li>
<li><em>Has that habit ever slowed you down?</em> An honest yes, with how you decide when a quick fix is acceptable without the full check.</li>
</ul>

## When this is wrong

STAR is a structure, not a script. Interviewers interrupt, and a story recited in four labelled parts sounds rehearsed. Know the parts well enough to tell them in a natural order and to jump straight to the action when the interviewer asks "what did you do?"

Norms differ across companies and cultures. Some interviewers want a crisp first-person account; some teams value hearing how the group worked and how you made others effective. "I" for your own actions is still right, but leave room for the people around you, especially in stories about conflict.

The company details in this lesson are what each company publishes, checked on 28 October 2026. Prep-vendor descriptions of values rounds are reports, and question lists attributed to a company are not the company's question bank. Use them for practice, and prepare from your own experience and the company's own words.

What I would tell myself before the loop: prepare the stories in writing, then tell them the way I would to a colleague who asked.
