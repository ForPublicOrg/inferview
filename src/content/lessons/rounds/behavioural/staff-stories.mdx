## What the story has to carry

A staff behavioural round spends most of its minutes in follow-ups, so the story is less the answer than an index into evidence the follow-ups will ask for. Preparation follows from that: a short cut that names each piece of evidence once, a longer cut that walks the chain, and an appendix of facts you never recite but can open when asked.

The published and reported signals agree on what the evidence is. Hello Interview's OpenAI L5 guide, built from community-submitted question lists and OpenAI's own guide, asks for personal ownership, the alternatives you considered and the impact, and for readiness to explain rejected approaches, delivery problems and what you would do differently. On influence it asks for a disagreement told as reasonable on both sides, not as a case where the others came round, and for engineers you enabled to own work they could not have owned before. OpenAI's own guide lists collaboration, effective communication, openness to feedback and alignment with its mission and values. Amazon's guide asks for "I" statements and precise figures, and puts a typical response at about two to three minutes.

Staff loops give these stories their own slots. Synthesia's August 2026 post for a principal or staff engineer lists a 60-minute Delivery and Impact interview (past projects, impact, ways of working) and a 45-minute Leadership Retrospective (collaboration and growth) next to the coding and design rounds. Before this lesson, attempt [the shipped LLM feature story](/problems/rnd-016) and [the accuracy-or-latency trade-off](/problems/rnd-017) cold, with a timer, and keep what you wrote.

## The prompts staff loops report

Three reported prompts from AI companies show where the bar sits. Each comes from a candidate report or an aggregation of them, not from a company's question bank, and each is taught here with your own material.

**Release criteria.** Hello Interview's OpenAI staff list includes "If you were in charge of deciding whether to release a new AI model, what criteria would you use to make that decision?" It is phrased as a hypothetical, and an answer that rests on no release you actually held or shipped has nothing for the follow-ups to inspect.

**A concern raised under pressure.** One candidate's report of an Anthropic machine learning engineer loop, on aced.io and dated about a year before this lesson's check, describes a culture-fit round run by an engineer. It opened with "Tell me about an ethical or policy concern you raised under pressure" and followed with whether there was executive pressure on an output or a timeline, how the team reacted and how the candidate escalated. The candidate's own story was a data output under launch pressure, taken as far as the chief counsel.

**Adoption.** The same report's hiring-manager round asked whether business users were involved, about adoption and about time to production. The Hello Interview guide places the changes other teams made in response to your work in the project discussion.

## Three cuts of one story

The cuts follow a design document's summary, body and appendix; the difference is that the reader interrupts, so each part must stand alone.

The **two-minute cut** carries one sentence of scope (what you owned, for how many users or teams), the decision and the alternative you rejected with the reason it lost, the result as a number against its baseline with how it was measured, the role outside your team that had to change its plans, and what went wrong. It carries no history of the organisation.

The **five-minute cut** adds the evidence chain (what you measured before deciding, and what you did not know), the other side of the disagreement in a sentence its owner would sign, the cost you accepted and who carried it, and what happened after launch: adoption, the rollback, the second iteration.

The **appendix** is a page of facts: dates, sizes, metric definitions, the eval set and how it was built, the rollout stages, the incident if there was one, the roles that disagreed, what you would do differently, and the evidence that would make you decide the other way today. Follow-ups such as "what was the baseline", "who disagreed, and were they right" and "what would you change" are answered from here, and facts reconstructed under questioning drift from the ones in the cuts.

## Building each story

### Release criteria

Two layers make the answer. The first is release gates you have run on a product: an eval suite with per-slice thresholds, a regression budget written down before the result was known, a staged rollout with rollback triggers, and a named person who signs. The second is the frontier frameworks the labs publish, which show the shape of the decision at the model level. OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, as updated on 15 April 2025, sets two thresholds, High and Critical capability; covered systems that reach High must have safeguards that sufficiently minimise the associated risk before deployment, a cross-functional Safety Advisory Group reviews Capabilities and Safeguards Reports and makes a recommendation, and OpenAI Leadership decides. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, at version 3.4 effective 8 July 2026, ties capability thresholds to required safeguards and has published Risk Reports since version 3.0.

The story is a release you held or shipped with a known risk. The appendix holds the criteria as they were written before the decision, the result against each, who decided, and what happened in production. The hypothetical answer then generalises your criteria: what is measured, the threshold and why it sits there, who decides when the threshold and a commitment conflict, and what reverses the decision. Rehearse two pushbacks: "Your criteria would have blocked your last three launches; which one do you relax?" and "The eval passes and a customer reports the failure in week one; was the release wrong or the eval?"

### A concern raised under pressure

The concern goes in the story as a checkable claim (the output double-counts refunds; the classifier was evaluated on data it was trained on), not as a feeling. The appendix holds the evidence you had and what you lacked, the order in which you raised it and with whom, the deadline and what slipping it would have cost, what happened, and whether you turned out to be right. The reported follow-ups (executive pressure, the team's reaction, the escalation path) are each a branch of that appendix. A concern raised and overruled is a usable story if you can say what you wrote down, what you did next and how the relationship with the person who overruled you went afterwards.

### Adoption

The facts are who the intended users were, how adoption was measured and what it was against your plan at a stated point, what you changed when it fell short, and what you retired. A story in which adoption stalled, you found out why from the users and changed the thing is stronger evidence of staff scope than a launch that went to plan, because it shows the part of the work that happened after your code merged.

### Influence across a disagreement

Write the other side's best argument in a sentence its owner would accept, then the evidence that moved the decision and what you conceded. A story where you were partly wrong and the final design took something from the other side fits the "reasonable on both sides" signal in the Hello Interview guide better than one where the evidence vindicated you completely.

## Choosing and rehearsing the set

Lay the stories against the prompts as a matrix: stories as rows; release criteria, a concern under pressure, adoption, influence, a failure and a trade-off as columns. Each story covers two or three columns by shifting emphasis, and each column needs two stories, so a second question on the same theme in a later interview does not get the same story. When a column has one story or none, that is the gap to fill from your work history before any rehearsal.

Rehearse the pushback, not the cuts. For each story, write the three follow-ups you least want and answer them in the appendix; Amazon's guide suggests recording yourself or rehearsing with a friend, and a friend who interrupts is the closer test. Where numbers are confidential, give ratios and relative changes and say that you are doing so.

<p>The two constraints are a contractual date and a measured regression on a slice that matters more than its traffic share. ## When this is wrong

The reported prompts rest on thin evidence: one aggregated question list for OpenAI and one candidate's account of one Anthropic loop. Prompts change by team and by quarter, and the set you prepare should be driven by your own work history first.

Some loops ask a fixed question per competency against a stated list, as Amazon does with its 16 Leadership Principles across four to six interviews. There the two-minute cut carries more of the weight and the appendix less, because the interviewer moves on to cover the next principle.

The three-cut model assumes stories of staff scope exist. A senior-scope story told precisely survives follow-ups that an inflated staff-scope story does not, and the appendix is where inflation is found: the dates do not line up, or the number was someone else's.

The widget's checks are word matches chosen by this site. They catch a missing number or a missing alternative; they cannot tell whether the alternative was real.
