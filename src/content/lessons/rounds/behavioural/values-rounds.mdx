## What a values round checks

A values round compares decisions you have made with what the lab says it values. A position ("I care deeply about safety") cannot be checked; a decision with a date, a trade-off and a cost can, and the follow-ups are built to check it. Preparation is therefore two jobs: read what the lab has published closely enough to hold a view on it, and find the decisions in your own history that bear on it.

What the labs say about the round is short. Anthropic's careers page describes its non-technical interviews as conversational, focused on how you think through problems and what draws you to the work, and calls the process "a two-way conversation". OpenAI's interview guide says it cares about "collaboration, effective communication, openness to feedback, and alignment with our mission and values", and lists its Charter as recommended reading.

Reports add detail and must be read as reports. One candidate's account of an Anthropic machine learning engineer loop describes a culture-fit round run by an engineer that focused on ethics, executive pressure and emotional intelligence, and a hiring manager who asked how the candidate thinks about AI safety, ethics, guardrails and governance. Hello Interview's OpenAI staff list includes "Tell me about a time you had to consider AI safety implications in your work and how you addressed them". A vendor page on OpenAI's forward deployed engineer loop reports an hour-long hiring manager and values round covering ambiguity and mission fit.

## What each lab says it values

Read these the way you would read an API's documented contract before depending on it; the difference is that the contract is versioned quickly, and you will be asked where you disagree with it.

Anthropic publishes seven values. Each maps to a kind of decision you may have made, and the mapping is where the preparation happens.

OpenAI's Charter has four principles: broadly distributed benefits, long-term safety, technical leadership and cooperative orientation. Two commitments in it are concrete enough to hold a view on. Under long-term safety, OpenAI commits to stop competing with and start assisting a value-aligned, safety-conscious project that comes close to building AGI first, with "a better-than-even chance of success in the next two years" as a typical trigger. Under cooperative orientation, it expects safety and security concerns to reduce its traditional publishing.

## The scaling policies

The scaling policies are where the stated values become procedures, and they are the material most directly about safety trade-offs.

Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy was first published on 19 September 2023. Version 3.0, effective 24 February 2026, was a comprehensive rewrite that added Frontier Safety Roadmaps and Risk Reports. Versions 3.1 to 3.4 followed on 2 April, 29 April, 26 May and 8 July 2026: five versions in the 134 days from 24 February to 8 July. Version 3.1 states that Anthropic remains free to pause development even where the policy does not require it. Version 3.4 changed internal sharing of unredacted Risk Reports from all regular-clearance staff to at least 200 employees. A separate Noncompliance Reporting and Anti-Retaliation Policy, updated on 24 March 2026, expands the reporting channels and adds a pathway for employees to make informal inquiries about potential violations: the lab's written escalation path for the "concern raised under pressure" prompt.

OpenAI's Preparedness Framework, as updated on 15 April 2025, tracks capabilities that are plausible, measurable, severe, net new, and instantaneous or irremediable. It sets two thresholds, High and Critical; systems at High need safeguards that sufficiently minimise the risk before deployment. A Safety Advisory Group of internal safety leaders recommends and OpenAI Leadership decides. The framework also says OpenAI may adjust its requirements if another developer releases a high-risk system without comparable safeguards, after confirming the landscape has changed, acknowledging the adjustment publicly and keeping safeguards more protective.

These clauses are where a defensible view is easiest to form, because each encodes a trade-off: the competitive-release adjustment trades unilateral restraint against relevance; the 3.4 sharing change trades internal transparency against the handling of sensitive material; the informal-inquiry pathway trades formality against the cost of raising a concern. A candidate who can state the trade-off, say which side they would take and say what would change their mind has an answer to "what concerns you about our approach?" that rests on the lab's own text.

## Turning a value into a number

Values answers become defensible when the cost on each side is stated. A constructed case shows the arithmetic.

<Predict id="rounds-beh-harm-window" question="If the feature ships now and the fix lands in 14 days, how many harmful sessions do you expect in that window?" answer={280} tolerance={5} unit="sessions">
<Fragment slot="prompt">
<p>Scenario inputs: an assistant feature serves 400,000 sessions a day. Replaying 60,000 realistic sessions against it produced 3 completions that disclose another user's data. A fix is ready in 14 days.</p>
</Fragment>
<p>The point estimate is 3 / 60,000 = 1 in 20,000 sessions. That gives 400,000 / 20,000 = 20 a day, and 20 × 14 = 280 over the window. Three events are a weak basis for a rate: the exact 95 percent Poisson interval for 3 observed events is 0.62 to 8.77, so the expected count over the window runs from 280 × 0.62 / 3 = 58 to 280 × 8.77 / 3 = 818. An answer that cites the range, names who bears the harm and states what the 14-day delay costs is specific and defensible; "I would prioritise safety" is neither.</p>
</Predict>

## Preparing answers

**Specific.** Each answer rests on a decision with a date, a trade-off and a number, from the [staff stories](/learn/rounds/behavioural/staff-stories) set. A value with no decision behind it is left unclaimed.

**Honest.** Include the time you did not raise a concern, or raised it late, and what that cost. A record in which you were right every time is not a record anyone can check.

**Defensible.** Each answer survives two follow-ups: "what if leadership had overruled you?" and "what evidence would change your mind?". It agrees with the lab's text where you agree and names the clause where you do not.

Five prompts cover the reported ground: why this lab, tied to a specific published commitment and the role (the Hello Interview guide advises against a rehearsed speech about AGI); a concern about the lab's direction, sourced to a clause; a safety or ethics trade-off you made in your own work; a time you acted under ambiguity; and a disagreement with someone senior.

## In the room

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Your agent feature launches in nine days for a customer who has signed. A replay of 60,000 realistic sessions finds 3 where the agent sends one tenant's data to another. The fix needs three weeks. The product lead wants to ship with monitoring. What do you do, and when have you made a call like this?"
</Callout>

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>I would not ship the path that leaks. I would offer to ship on the date with the tool that sends data across the tenant boundary disabled, which keeps the rest of the feature, and put the cost in writing: the customer loses that capability for three weeks. Monitoring detects a leak after it has happened, and a disclosure cannot be recalled. Then the story of a time I made a comparable call, with what it cost.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The constraints are the signed date and a measured cross-tenant disclosure rate. At 3 in 60,000 the point estimate is 1 in 20,000 sessions, with a wide interval because only three events were seen. The harm is irreversible for the affected tenant and falls on someone who is not in the room.</p>
<p>The options are shipping as is with monitoring, slipping three weeks, or shipping with the leaking capability removed. Monitoring fails because detection follows disclosure. Slipping protects the tenant and costs the contract date. Removing the capability keeps the date and most of the value, at the cost of a feature the customer may have bought for.</p>
<p>I would take the third, write the risk note with the measured rate and its range, agree with the product lead which evidence brings the tool back (the fix passing the replay plus a clean run on a larger set), and tell the customer what is missing and why. The comparable story from my own work goes here, with the cost I accepted and whether I would decide the same way now.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="followups">
<ul>
<li>The customer signed for exactly that capability, and the account lead says they will walk. Which do you accept in writing, the leak risk or the lost account, and who signs it?</li>
<li>The fix passes the replay. What would you need to see in the first two weeks of production to keep the tool on, and what turns it off again?</li>
<li>A senior engineer argues the replay overstates the rate because the sessions were adversarially sampled. How do you settle it without deciding by rank?</li>
</ul>
</Fragment>
</Depth>

## When this is wrong

Published values describe what a lab commits to and how it wants to be seen; they do not say how every team works or how a round is scored. The round assesses your examples, and mapping every story onto a value list adds nothing the examples do not already show.

The policies move. The RSP went through five versions in 134 days in 2026, and the Preparedness Framework cited here is the April 2025 update. Date every clause you quote, and check it the week before the interview.

The reports behind the round descriptions are one candidate's account and two vendor pages. Some loops have no separate values round and fold the questions into the hiring manager conversation, as the vendor page on the OpenAI forward deployed loop describes.

A specific disagreement is defensible only if it is yours and you can argue the other side. A concern adopted for the interview does not survive the second follow-up.
