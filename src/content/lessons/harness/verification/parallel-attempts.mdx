## The problem the paper takes on

Large Language Monkeys (Brown, Juravsky, Ehrlich and colleagues, Stanford, Oxford and Google DeepMind; first posted July 2024, revised December 2024) asks what happens if you spend inference compute on independent attempts instead of on a bigger model. Its answer splits the question in two, and the split is the whole lesson for harness design.

**Coverage** is the fraction of problems where at least one attempt is correct. It is measured with an oracle that knows the answer, so it is an upper bound. **Precision** is whether you can find the correct attempt among the wrong ones without that oracle. Success rate, the number a user experiences, needs both. Where a cheap automatic verifier exists, such as a proof checker or a test suite, precision is nearly free and coverage turns into success. Where it does not, coverage is a ceiling you cannot reach.

A harness that runs parallel attempts is a coverage machine bolted to a selector. The paper's results say the selector is the part to design.

## The mechanism, in engineering terms

The method is deliberately plain: the same prompt, the same hyperparameters, positive temperature, many independent samples per problem. For SWE-bench Lite one sample is a whole multi-turn trajectory in the Moatless Tools agent framework, and the authors capped attempts at 250 per issue to bound cost.

Coverage at k is computed with the unbiased pass@k estimator from Chen et al.: draw n samples, count c correct, and compute the probability that a random subset of k contains at least one correct sample. In Python:

```python
from math import comb


def pass_at_k(n, c, k):
    """Probability that k samples drawn from n, of which c are correct, include a correct one."""
    if n - c < k:
        return 1.0
    return 1 - comb(n - c, k) / comb(n, k)


print(pass_at_k(250, 4, 1), pass_at_k(250, 4, 5))   # a hard problem: 4 of 250 correct
```

That prints `0.016` and about `0.078`. A problem the model solves one time in about sixty contributes almost nothing at k = 1 and is likely covered by k = 250. Coverage curves rise because hard problems with small but nonzero per-attempt probability get covered one by one; the paper fits the curves with an exponentiated power law in k.

For selection without a verifier the paper tried three methods on GSM8K and MATH with 10,000 samples per problem from Llama-3 models: majority vote over final answers, best-of-n by a reward model, and reward-weighted majority vote. All three plateau. The figure caption says they saturate before 100 samples, while coverage keeps climbing toward 100%.

## The numbers

On SWE-bench Lite the paper reports DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct at 15.9% with one sample and 56% coverage with 250, above the 43% single-attempt state of the art at the time. Coverage here means at least one of the 250 trajectories passed SWE-bench's own tests. A deployed system would not have those tests, so 56% is what a perfect selector could reach, not what the authors' system would ship.

The paper also prices it. With Moatless Tools fixed, five attempts from DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct solved 29.62% of Lite for $10.8 in total, against 24.00% for one GPT-4o attempt at $39 and 26.70% for one Claude 3.5 Sonnet attempt at $51 (Table 1). Those prices are from mid-2024 and the comparison assumes the five attempts can be told apart, which on SWE-bench means the benchmark's tests did the selecting.

The paper's section on verifiers is two cautions that apply to any harness using tests to select. 34 of the 300 Lite problems, 11.3%, had flaky test suites, and in 30 of those the dataset's own gold patch sometimes failed; the authors scored flaky problems by majority over 11 runs. On CodeContests, 35 of the 122 problems with Python 3 solutions had dataset-provided correct solutions that failed the tests. A test-based selector inherits both: flaky tests reject correct attempts at random, and over-specified tests reject correct attempts systematically.

## Claude 4's parallel test-time compute

Anthropic's Claude 4 announcement (2025-05-22) reports SWE-bench Verified at 72.5% for Opus 4 and 72.7% for Sonnet 4, with a scaffold of a bash tool and a string-replacement edit tool, no extended thinking, scored over all 500 problems. Its "high compute" numbers use the Large Language Monkeys recipe with a selector:

1. Sample multiple parallel attempts.
2. Discard patches that break the repository's visible regression tests, the rejection-sampling approach from Agentless, with no hidden test information.
3. Pick from the survivors with an internal scoring model.

That gives 79.4% for Opus 4 and 80.2% for Sonnet 4. For Opus 4 the lift is 6.9 points, about 34 to 35 of the 500 problems. The announcement does not say how many attempts were sampled, what the scoring model is, or what a high-compute run costs.

Two observations follow. The filter is a verifier with known false passes: a patch that breaks no existing test can still fail to fix the issue, which is why a scoring model sits after it. And 79.4% is a different quantity from 72.5%. It is the result of a system that runs several agents and a judge per task. Comparing it with another vendor's single-attempt number compares a system to a model call.

The take-home README makes the same point with one model held fixed. On its 2-hour version, which starts at 18,532 cycles, Opus 4.5 reached 1,790 cycles in a casual Claude Code session, 1,579 after 2 hours in Anthropic's test-time compute harness, 1,487 after 11.5 hours, and 1,363 in an improved harness. Every number is "Opus 4.5", and they span a 1,790 / 1,363 = 1.31 times difference in result.

## Selection needs a verifier

This simulation makes the paper's argument with a scenario mix of task difficulty, chosen so pass@1 lands near 15%: 45% of tasks the model never solves, and the rest at 2%, 20% or 70% per attempt. A test filter always passes correct patches and passes a wrong patch 5% of the time. The selector picks uniformly among the survivors, which is a selector with no signal beyond the filter.

```python
import random

MIX = [0.0] * 45 + [0.02] * 20 + [0.2] * 20 + [0.7] * 15   # scenario: per-task chance that one attempt is correct


def run(k, false_pass, tasks=2000, seed=7):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    covered = solved = 0
    for _ in range(tasks):
        p = rng.choice(MIX)
        attempts = [rng.random() < p for _ in range(k)]
        covered += any(attempts)
        # the filter: a correct patch always passes the visible tests, a wrong one passes with probability false_pass
        survivors = [ok for ok in attempts if ok or rng.random() < false_pass]
        if survivors:
            solved += rng.choice(survivors)   # a selector with no signal beyond the filter
    return covered / tasks, solved / tasks


for k in (1, 5, 25, 250):
    cov, ok = run(k, false_pass=0.05)
    print(f"k={k:>3}  coverage {cov:.1%}  selected and correct {ok:.1%}")
```

It prints coverage of 14.8%, 29.6%, 42.6% and 55.4% at k = 1, 5, 25 and 250, and selected-and-correct of 14.8%, 27.8%, 35.8% and 36.5%. Coverage keeps rising toward the 55% ceiling the mix allows. Success flattens after 25 attempts, because on hard tasks the one correct attempt is outnumbered by wrong attempts that slipped through the filter: with 249 wrong attempts and a 5% false-pass rate, about 12 wrong patches survive next to it. The gap between the two columns at k = 250 is 55.4 − 36.5 = 18.9 points, more than the 55.4 − 42.6 = 12.8 points of coverage that attempts 26 to 250 added, and closing it is the scoring model's job.

## What changed in practice

Vendors now report two kinds of number, and the methodology note says which. Anthropic labels high compute and describes its selection steps. OpenAI's 2026 audit of SWE-bench Verified adds the other half: near the top of a benchmark, scores also reflect tests that reject correct fixes and training-data exposure, so the oracle is part of every number. A team evaluating agents should report pass@1 and, separately, best-of-k with the selector named, at a stated cost per task.

Inside harnesses the recipe shows up wherever attempts are cheap to isolate: separate worktrees or containers per attempt, a deterministic filter (build, typecheck, existing tests), then a model or a person choosing among survivors. The paper's result decides where effort goes: past a few attempts, a better filter or scorer buys more than more attempts.

## When this is wrong

The paper's coverage numbers come from an oracle verifier. On SWE-bench that oracle is the hidden test suite, which the paper itself found flaky on 11.3% of Lite problems, and which OpenAI's later audit found over- or under-specified on many hard Verified problems. Coverage curves on a flawed oracle measure the oracle as much as the model.

Independence is an assumption the paper adopts for simplicity, and its own discussion lists solution diversity first among the improvements it did not try: all attempts used the same prompt and differed only by sampling temperature. Real harness attempts share a model, a context and a repository state, so they fail together more than a binomial model predicts.

The models are from 2024 and the prices are mid-2024 API prices. The direction of the results (coverage rises log-linearly, unverified selection plateaus) has held since; the magnitudes are tied to those models.

Finally, parallel attempts multiply side effects. A test filter is safe to run k times on isolated copies; an attempt that sends a message, writes to a shared database or opens a pull request is not. Attempts belong in sandboxes with no external effects until one is selected, which is a permission design question before it is a compute one.
