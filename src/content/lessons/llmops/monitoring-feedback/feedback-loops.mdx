## The decision

Every feedback signal a product collects gets a job, and there are four available: choosing which traces a person reads, appearing as a metric on a dashboard, becoming an eval case, or becoming a training reward. The decision is which signals get which jobs, and what has to sit beside a signal before it is promoted from the first two jobs to the last two.

The default this playbook argues for: every signal may route traces to a reader; confirmed failures become eval cases; a reaction signal goes on a dashboard only paired with an outcome metric; and a reaction signal becomes a training reward only with controls that can block the launch.

## What the signals measure

Eugene Yan's catalogue separates explicit feedback, which the product asks for (ChatGPT's thumbs and its regenerate button), from implicit feedback, read from what users do anyway: a Copilot suggestion accepted as is, accepted with small edits or ignored; a Midjourney grid re-rolled, varied, or upscaled and downloaded. To those, a production assistant adds edit-before-use, abandonment, escalation to a human and re-contact on the same issue.

All of them measure the user's reaction, at the moment of the answer, from users who chose to react. Three public results show how far that can sit from the outcome. OpenAI's April 2025 GPT-4o update added a reward from thumbs data that weakened the reward holding sycophancy in check, while offline evals and an A/B test looked good. In METR's 2025 trial, 16 experienced developers took 19% longer with AI tools allowed and afterwards estimated they had been 20% faster. In Ziegler and colleagues' Copilot study, the acceptance rate of shown suggestions was the usage measure that best predicted perceived productivity, at a correlation of 0.24 across 2,047 responses, ahead of every measure of whether the code persisted. [Success metrics for a probabilistic feature](/learn/evals/evals-in-ci/success-metrics) works through all three; this playbook is about what to build given them.

## The inputs that change it

**Who reacts.** Explicit feedback comes from a minority whose reasons you do not know. The Evals FAQ's sampling table lists the limit plainly: selecting on negative feedback misses problems users do not report.

**Precision as a failure filter.** The share of flagged traces that a reviewer confirms as failures. It is unknown until someone reviews flagged traces, and it differs per signal.

**Whether the system can move the signal without moving the outcome.** A confident or agreeable answer earns a thumbs-up whether or not it is right. Any signal the model's style can move is unsafe as a target, because optimisation will find the style.

**Outcome lag.** Re-contact within seven days, a query result exported, code that survived review: these are closer to the outcome and arrive later. The longer the lag, the stronger the pull toward the reaction metric.

**Access.** Anthropic's September 2025 postmortem notes that privacy controls restrict engineers to interactions users submitted as feedback. Where that holds, feedback is not only a filter; it is the only access path to raw traces, and the feedback channel's design decides what the team can ever see.

## The options and when each wins

**Route traces to a reader.** Always wins, because a person confirming the failure turns a noisy signal into a label. The condition is a random slice in every review batch, as the FAQ recommends, so that failure modes nobody reports still get read and each filter's precision can be estimated.

**Dashboard metric beside an outcome.** Wins once a filter's precision is measured and an outcome metric with a stated lag exists. The rule that makes it safe: a change in the reaction metric without a matching change in the outcome is investigated as a change in who reacts, or in the UI, before anyone calls it quality.

**Eval case.** Wins for every confirmed failure. The FAQ's instruction is to start the repeatable eval set from error discovery and add confirmed failures as they are found; the loop below is that instruction made operational.

**Training reward.** Wins only when the signal is close to the outcome and comparative: Midjourney's upscale-and-download is a user choosing one image among four, which is a preference over outputs, not a reaction to tone. Even then it needs three controls: a held-out outcome eval the reward cannot see, a probe set for agreement with false premises, and expert review that can block the launch. OpenAI's stated change after April 2025, treating model behaviour issues as launch-blocking, is the third control; Willison's reading is that the miss was letting automated evals and A/B tests overrule the expert testers who said the model felt off.

## Worked decision

Scenario, with invented numbers stated as inputs: an assistant that writes SQL for analysts handles 20,000 sessions a week. It records thumbs-down, edit-before-run and abandonment. Two weeks of review, including a random slice, gave these estimates: 8% of sessions fail; analysts thumb down 15% of failed sessions and 1% of successful ones; they edit the query before running it in 40% of failed sessions and 10% of successful ones.

<Predict id="ops-feedback-precision" question="What share of thumbs-down sessions are real failures, in percent?" answer={56.6} tolerance={0.5} unit="%">
<p>Failures: 20,000 × 0.08 = 1,600, of which 15%, 240, get a thumbs-down. Successes: 18,400, of which 1%, 184, get one. Precision is 240 / 424 = <b>56.6%</b>, and recall is 15%: 1,360 failures a week never receive a thumbs-down. Edit-before-run flags 640 + 1,840 = 2,480 sessions at 25.8% precision and 40% recall, so it finds more failures and wastes more review time per failure.</p>
</Predict>

Two reviewers have 300 traces a week between them. The allocation follows precision and blind spots: 150 drawn from the 424 thumbs-down sessions (about 85 confirmed failures at 56.6%), 75 from edit-before-run (about 19), and 75 random (about 6). The random 75 find few failures, and they are the only traces that can reveal a failure mode neither signal flags and the only check on whether the two filters' precision is holding.

Now promote thumbs-down rate to a metric and watch a prompt change that makes answers more assertive. Suppose the failure rate rises from 8% to 9% and, because confident queries look right, analysts thumb down 10% of failures instead of 15%. Thumbs-down sessions go from 424 to 20,000 × 0.09 × 0.10 + 20,000 × 0.91 × 0.01 = 180 + 182 = 362. The metric improves from 2.12% to 1.81% of sessions while failures rise from 1,600 to 1,800. Used as a reward, the same signal pays the model for the assertiveness. That is the sycophancy mechanism at the scale of one team, and it needs no bad intent from anyone.

The written decision for this case: thumbs-down and edit-before-run route traces to the review queue with a fixed random share; the dashboard shows thumbs-down rate only beside re-run-with-changes within the session and the weekly reviewed failure rate; no feedback signal enters training this quarter. What the team gives up is a cheap, fast quality number for leadership; the reviewed failure rate arrives weekly with an interval, and someone has to defend that to a VP who wanted a daily line.

## Closing the loop

A confirmed failure is worth something only once it can block a regression. The loop has five steps, and each one leaves an artefact:

1. A reviewer labels the trace with a failure mode from the [error-analysis taxonomy](/learn/evals/eval-thinking/error-analysis), or adds a mode.
2. Someone reduces it to the smallest input that reproduces it, with retrieval results and tool responses frozen, and writes the expected behaviour as a check: code if a rule can decide it, a validated judge if not.
3. The case enters the eval set tagged with its source, date and failure mode.
4. The fix ships: prompt, retrieval, tool, data or model.
5. The case joins the [merge gate](/learn/evals/evals-in-ci/merge-gates), in the hard tier if that failure must never recur.

The loop's health is the time from confirmation to a gated case, and whether gated cases stay fixed.

## What to measure after deciding

Per filter, weekly: precision against reviewer labels, and the confirmed failures it found. From the random slice: the share of confirmed failure modes that no filter flagged; when it rises, the filters have drifted from the failures. Feedback volume per session, per segment and per UI version, because a moved button changes the rate and nothing else. Every reaction metric beside its outcome metric, with the outcome's lag on the chart. The loop itself: cases added per week, median days from confirmation to gate, and gated cases that regress. And a fixed probe set of false-premise requests in the scheduled replay, scored for agreement, which is the cheapest standing check on sycophancy.

## When this is wrong

Some implicit signals are outcomes. When accepted code must then pass tests before merge, or a generated query's result is exported into a report someone reads, the signal has an outcome attached, and treating it only as a filter wastes it. The Copilot result does not say persistence is a worse signal; it says persistence tracks how developers feel less well than acceptance does, which is a statement about feelings.

At low volume the filters barely matter. With a few hundred sessions a week, two reviewers can read most of them, and the precision arithmetic is overhead.

The controls on a feedback reward are not free. A probe set needs maintenance as the product changes, and expert sign-off is a queue on every release. A team without the reviewers to staff those controls should not train on feedback at all, which is a stronger conclusion than "with controls".

And the scenario numbers carry the argument only as an illustration. The direction of the effect, a style change lowering complaints while raising failures, depends on users trusting confident answers; in a product whose users check every output, thumbs-down tracks failure more closely, and the review allocation should follow the measured precision rather than this example.
