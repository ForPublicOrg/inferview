## The shape of the curve

An LLM feature's quality curve does not behave like a performance-tuning curve, where each profile run points at the next hotspot. It behaves like a bug tracker after a beta: a handful of failure modes produce most of the failures, and once they are fixed, what remains is dozens of modes that each appear in a fraction of a percent of conversations. The first phase is bounded by engineering time. The second is bounded by how fast the team can see a rare failure, fix it without breaking something else, and prove the fix worked, and all three get slower as the modes get rarer.

The best public prior for the length of the second phase is LinkedIn's 2024 account of its job-search and content assistant. The team reached 80% of the basic experience within the first month and then spent an additional four months trying to surpass 95% completion of the full experience. They write that they underestimated how quality would rise quickly and then plateau, and that the early pace gave a false sense of being almost there. The product's stated expectation was that more than 99% of answers would be great.

Two cautions travel with those numbers. LinkedIn does not define "80% of the experience" as a pass rate on a fixed set, so it is a milestone, not a measurement you can compare with your own judge's score. And "the full experience" in the second milestone is a larger scope than "the basic experience" in the first, so part of the four months is scope, not polish.

## What LinkedIn's team had to work with

The system was a fixed three-step pipeline: route the query to one of dozens of agents, retrieve from internal services and search, generate the answer. Smaller models did routing and retrieval and larger ones did generation, so a failure could originate in any of three models or in the services between them.

Evaluation ran at three speeds. Engineers did fast, coarse checks for direction. Annotators gave finer judgements with about a one-day turnaround, and the internal linguist team built tooling that let them evaluate up to 500 conversations a day. Members were the final judge, and some member metrics took three or more days to read for a single change. Automatic evaluation was, in their words, still a work in progress, with some success on hallucination detection.

That evaluation capacity is the binding input for a last-mile estimate, and it is easy to leave out because it is not engineering headcount.

## Why the last mile is slow: a toy model

Scenario, with numbers chosen to show the shape and not reconstructed from LinkedIn, which did not publish its failure distribution. At 80% quality, 20 points of failure remain, split into three bands:

| Band | Modes | Share of conversations each | Points in total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frequent | 5 | 2.4% | 12 |
| Middle | 20 | 0.25% | 5 |
| Tail | 60 | 0.05% | 3 |

Assume a shipped fix removes 80% of its mode's failures, because prompt and retrieval fixes rarely remove a mode entirely, and that modes are fixed in order of size.

The five frequent fixes recover 5 × 2.4 × 0.8 = 9.6 points, reaching 89.6%. The twenty middle fixes recover 20 × 0.25 × 0.8 = 4.0 points, reaching 93.6%. Each tail fix recovers 0.05 × 0.8 = 0.04 points.

<Predict id="ops-last-mile-tail-fixes" question="How many tail fixes take the feature from 93.6% to 95%?" answer={35} tolerance={0} unit="fixes">
<p>The gap is 95 − 93.6 = 1.4 points, and each tail fix is worth 0.04, so 1.4 / 0.04 = <b>35</b> fixes. The path to 95% is 5 + 20 + 35 = 60 fixes, and the last 35 of them buy 1.4 points between them while the first 5 bought 9.6. Fixing all 60 tail modes gives 80 + 9.6 + 4.0 + 2.4 = 96%, below a 99% target: in this census no number of local fixes reaches it.</p>
</Predict>

At a sustained three verified fixes a week (a scenario rate), 60 fixes take 20 weeks, and the first 25 of them take a little over 8 weeks while delivering 13.6 of the 15 points. The chart a stakeholder sees in week 8 says 93.6% and climbing; the remaining 12 weeks move it 1.4 points.

The conclusion is sensitive to two inputs. If fix efficiency is 0.6 instead of 0.8, the frequent and middle bands recover 7.2 + 3.0 = 10.2 points, the whole tail 60 × 0.03 = 1.8 more, and the ceiling is 92%, so 95% needs a structural change: a stronger model, a fine-tuned component, a narrower scope or a different decomposition of the pipeline. If the tail is 30 modes at 0.1% instead of 60 at 0.05%, the same 3 points need half as many fixes. The estimate is only as good as the failure-mode census behind it, which is why the first month's error analysis, not its quality score, is the input to the timeline.

## Each tail mode costs more to see

A mode that appears in 0.05% of conversations shows up in a reviewed sample about once per 2,000 conversations. To see it three times, enough to tell a mode from a one-off, you need about 3 / 0.0005 = 6,000 reviewed conversations. At 500 a day that is 12 days of the full linguist capacity. Reviewing those 6,000 surfaces the whole tail at once, so discovery is a batch cost paid once, not per mode.

Verification is the cost that recurs. A tail fix cannot be checked on random traffic: its expected effect is 0.04 points, far below anything a sampled pass rate can see. It is checked on targeted cases built from the confirmed failures, plus a regression check that the fix did not break anything else, and the regression check is where sample size bites.

<Predict id="ops-last-mile-margin" question="How many independently graded conversations does a 95% pass rate need for a 95% interval of plus or minus 1 point?" answer={1825} tolerance={10} unit="conversations">
<p>n = 1.96² × 0.95 × 0.05 / 0.01² = 3.8416 × 0.0475 / 0.0001 ≈ <b>1,825</b>. At 500 graded conversations a day that is between three and four days of capacity for one reading of one version, and a half-point regression still sits inside the interval.</p>
</Predict>

Comparing two versions on different traffic makes this worse. Detecting a 0.5-point drop from 95% with 80% power at a two-sided 5% level needs about (1.96 + 0.84)² × 2 × 0.95 × 0.05 / 0.005² ≈ 29,800 conversations per arm. Two arms are 59,600 gradings, about 119 days at 500 a day, for one comparison. Running both versions on the same frozen inputs and counting only the items where they disagree changes the arithmetic: if the fix flips 1% of items in either direction, the paired comparison needs about (1.96 + 0.84)² × 0.01 / 0.005² ≈ 3,140 items graded under both versions. That is the case for a frozen regression set graded by a validated judge in the last mile, with the human team spent on discovery and on checking the judge. The [A/B statistics lesson](/learn/llmops/releases-experiments/ab-statistics) covers the paired analysis.

## Fix at the cheapest layer that removes the mode

LinkedIn's most useful number is not the timeline. About 10% of the structured outputs that call internal skills were invalid YAML or violated the schema. Re-prompting on failure was rejected because it added latency and GPU load. A defensive YAML parser that repaired the common mistakes, plus hints in the prompt about those mistakes, brought the error rate to about 0.01%, a factor of 10% / 0.01% = 1,000.

That fix removed an entire frequent mode with deterministic code, verified by unit tests rather than graded conversations, at no per-request model cost. The ordering it suggests for every mode in the census: a deterministic repair or validation layer first, then retrieval or tool changes, then prompt changes, then model changes. The order runs from cheapest to verify to most expensive, and from least to most likely to regress other modes.

Some last-mile fixes spend latency instead. LinkedIn found chain-of-thought improved quality and reduced hallucinations but raised perceived latency, and their arithmetic shows how quickly reasoning tokens amplify serving changes: at 200 reasoning tokens, a 10 ms rise in time between tokens is 200 × 10 ms = 2 s. That rise came with load during a public ramp, triggered timeout alerts, and capacity had to be added quickly. A quality fix that adds reasoning tokens moves the latency budget and the GPU budget along with the score.

## A timeline estimate, written down

```python
# Fixes needed and weeks to a target, from a failure-mode census.
def last_mile(start, modes, efficiency, target, fixes_per_week):
    quality, fixes = start, 0
    for share in sorted(modes, reverse=True):
        if quality >= target - 1e-9:
            break
        quality += share * efficiency
        fixes += 1
    reached = quality >= target - 1e-9
    return fixes, round(fixes / fixes_per_week, 1), round(quality, 2), reached

census = [2.4] * 5 + [0.25] * 20 + [0.05] * 60   # points per mode, scenario
print(last_mile(80, census, 0.8, 95, 3))   # (60, 20.0, 95.0, True)
print(last_mile(80, census, 0.6, 95, 3))   # (85, 28.3, 92.0, False)
```

The second line is the result that matters in planning: with less effective fixes the target is unreachable by fixing modes one at a time, and the plan needs a structural option and its cost on the table before the date is promised.

## When this is wrong

The toy model treats failure modes as independent and fixes as additive. In practice a prompt change that fixes one mode can reintroduce another, so the net gain per fix is lower than the gross removal, and only the regression check reveals it. The model also assumes the census is stable, while traffic changes add modes during the months of the last mile; an estimate built in month one undercounts the tail.

A structural change resets the curve. A stronger model, a fine-tuned router or a narrower product can remove many tail modes at once, and LinkedIn's own list of next steps (fine-tuning, moving simpler tasks to in-house fine-tuned models) is that kind of change. Teams that plan only local fixes miss the cheaper path; teams that plan only on the next model release miss their date when it ships late or moves a different set of modes.

LinkedIn's numbers are one team's account of one product with 2024 models, rubrics written for that product, and milestone definitions the post does not spell out. They are a prior for the shape and the order of magnitude, not a rate to scale. And when the target is measured by a judge, the judge's agreement with humans caps what the score can certify: if the judge and the human labels disagree on more items than separate 95% from 97%, the last two points are not measurable with that judge.
