## The problem the paper answers

LoRA's pitch is memory: freeze the base, train a low-rank product BA beside each chosen weight matrix, and keep optimizer state for a few million parameters instead of billions. The open question in 2024 was what the pitch costs in quality, and whether "matches full fine-tuning" held outside the benchmarks where it was first shown. Biderman et al. (TMLR 2024) ran the comparison on two hard target domains, code and maths, in both regimes a team actually uses: instruction fine-tuning on about 100,000 prompt-response pairs, and continued pretraining on up to 20 billion unlabelled tokens.

## The mechanism in engineering terms

A LoRA update to a d_out × d_in matrix is BA with B of shape d_out × r and A of shape r × d_in. It adds r × (d_in + d_out) trainable parameters and can only move the weights within an r-dimensional subspace. That is the whole trade: a hard cap on the rank of the change, in exchange for memory and for an adapter small enough to swap per request.

<Predict id="ops-lora-share" question="On a 4096 × 4096 attention projection, what percentage of the matrix's parameter count does a rank-256 LoRA adapter train?" answer={12.5} tolerance={0.2} unit="%">
The adapter has 256 × (4096 + 4096) = 2,097,152 parameters; the matrix has 4096 × 4096 = 16,777,216. That is 12.5%. At rank 16 it is 16 × 8,192 = 131,072, or 0.78%. Biderman et al. measured that full fine-tuning's change to layer 26's query projection in Llama-2-7B needed about 2,000 of 4,096 directions to explain 90% of its variance, so even rank 256 is about an eighth of what full fine-tuning used there.
</Predict>

The paper's spectral analysis is the core finding. Full fine-tuning learns weight perturbations whose rank is 10 to 100 times that of typical LoRA settings. A low-rank adapter cannot represent that change, so on a task that needs it, LoRA learns less. The same constraint explains the other half of the title: a change confined to a small subspace disturbs the rest of the model less, so LoRA forgets less.

Compute is not where LoRA saves. Thinking Machines counts per-pass FLOPs for a square N × N matrix as 2N² + 6NR for LoRA against 3N² for full fine-tuning, because LoRA still runs the full forward and the backward through the frozen weights and skips only the weight gradient. At N = 4096 and R = 16 that is (2 + 6 × 16 / 4096) / 3 = 2.023 / 3 ≈ 0.67 of the full cost. The saving is memory and the artefact's size.

The abstract's "LoRA substantially underperforms full finetuning" is qualified by "in the standard low-rank settings", and the table shows why the qualifier matters. At rank 16, the rank most tutorials use, code instruction tuning reached 0.358 against 0.470. At rank 256 it reached 0.498. On continued pretraining, LoRA trails at every rank the paper tried.

The paper's own recommendations: use LoRA for instruction fine-tuning rather than continued pretraining; if memory allows, target all modules at rank 256, because ranks 16 to 64 were not enough for code; set alpha to 2r; and sweep the learning rate from 1e-5 to 5e-4, taking the highest value that trains stably. Its best LoRA rates were 5e-4 for code and 2e-4 for maths, against 5e-5 and 1e-5 for full fine-tuning, and LoRA was the more sensitive of the two to the rate.

## What changed in practice: LoRA Without Regret

Thinking Machines (John Schulman and colleagues, September 2025) asked when the gap closes rather than whether it exists, and found conditions under which it does:

- **Small and medium supervised datasets.** On Tulu3 and OpenThoughts3, LoRA matched full fine-tuning. When a dataset exceeds the adapter's capacity, LoRA falls behind; the loss does not hit a floor, training simply becomes less efficient.
- **All layers, especially the MLP.** Attention-only LoRA at rank 256 (0.25B trainable parameters on Llama-3.1-8B) underperformed MLP-only LoRA at rank 128 (0.24B). Parameter count was not the variable; placement was. Configs that adapt only the query and value projections are still common.
- **About ten times the learning rate.** The optimal LoRA rate was consistently about 10 times the full fine-tuning optimum (fitted multiplier 9.8), and nearly independent of rank. A LoRA run copied from a full fine-tuning config at 2e-5 is undertrained by design.
- **Small batches.** LoRA tolerated large batches worse than full fine-tuning, and the gap did not depend on rank.
- **Reinforcement learning at tiny rank.** LoRA matched full fine-tuning in RL even at rank 1. The authors' capacity argument: RL feeds the model roughly one bit per episode, so 10,000 MATH problems with 32 samples each is about 320,000 bits, against about 3 million parameters in a rank-1 adapter on Llama-3.1-8B.

The two sources do not contradict each other once the conditions are lined up. Biderman et al.'s widest gaps are continued pretraining on billions of tokens, which is exactly "a dataset that exceeds LoRA capacity", and low ranks on hard code tasks. Their rank-256 instruction-tuning results sit close to full fine-tuning, which is the regime where Thinking Machines found parity.

## When this is wrong

Both sources measured a handful of bases (Llama-2-7B for Biderman et al.; Llama-3.1-8B among the models Thinking Machines reports) and a handful of task families. The conditions transfer as hypotheses about your run, not as guarantees, and the cheap test is a rank sweep plus one full fine-tune ceiling on your own held-out set.

"Forgets less" was measured on three multiple-choice benchmarks. A product's general ability is narrower and more specific (its tool-call format, its refusal behaviour, its tone), and those can regress under LoRA too. Evaluate the regressions you care about directly.

The memory argument weakens as hardware changes. On an 8-GPU node with sharded optimizer state, full fine-tuning of an 8B model is routine, and the decision rests on forgetting, serving and the artefact, not on whether it fits.

Thinking Machines' learning-rate ratio assumes their parametrisation: alpha 32 with alpha/r scaling and their initialisation. Libraries that scale by alpha/sqrt(r), or set alpha to 2r as Biderman et al. do, shift the effective rate, so the ratio is a starting point for a sweep, not a constant to hard-code.
