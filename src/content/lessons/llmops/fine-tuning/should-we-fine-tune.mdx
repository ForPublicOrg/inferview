## The decision

Three changes compete for the same failure: change the prompt, change what the model sees at request time (retrieval and tools), or change the weights. Fine-tuning is the one that is expensive to undo. It produces an artefact tied to one base model, it needs a data pipeline and an eval set that outlive the run, and it is retired when its base is retired. The decision is whether the failure is one weights can fix, and whether the traffic pays for owning that artefact.

The split that decides most cases is what the model gets wrong. If it lacks a fact (a price, a policy, last week's release), the gap is knowledge. If it has what it needs but answers in the wrong format, label set, tone or length, or needs thousands of tokens of instructions to behave, the gap is behaviour. Weights are a good place for behaviour and a poor place for knowledge, and the published evidence for the second half is unusually direct.

## The inputs that change it

**What is failing.** Read failing traces and tag each one as knowledge, behaviour or both before anyone opens a training notebook. A pile of behaviour tags with a stable output space (40 queues, one JSON schema, one house style) points at weights. A pile of knowledge tags points at retrieval, whatever the request said.

**The evidence on knowledge.** Ovadia et al. compared unsupervised fine-tuning with retrieval for adding knowledge to 7B models. On 910 questions about US events from August to November 2023, after the models' training cutoffs, Mistral 7B scored 0.481 with no help, 0.504 after fine-tuning on the source documents, 0.588 after fine-tuning on ten paraphrases of each chunk, and 0.875 with retrieval. Llama 2 7B fell from 0.353 to 0.219 after plain fine-tuning on the same documents. Fine-tuning on paraphrases and then adding retrieval scored 0.830 for Mistral, below retrieval alone. Gekhman et al. supply the mechanism: examples carrying facts the model does not know are learned slowly, and as they are fitted the model hallucinates more on facts it did know. The [new-knowledge walkthrough](/learn/llmops/fine-tuning/new-knowledge) takes that paper apart.

**The evidence on narrow tasks.** LoRA Land trained 4-bit LoRA adapters for 10 base models on 31 tasks, 310 models in all. The paper reports that the adapters beat their base models by 34 points and GPT-4 by 10 points on average. Two conditions travel with the number: the comparison is against GPT-4 as prompted by the authors in April 2024, not against current frontier models, and the tasks are ones the authors could score automatically. The same paper served 25 Mistral-7B adapters from one 80 GB A100 with LoRAX, which shares the base weights and loads adapters per request. That serving fact matters as much as the quality one, because it changes who pays for the GPU.

**Volume and latency.** A prompt that carries a long instruction block and few-shot examples pays for those tokens on every call. A tuned model can drop them, and a tuned small model can replace a prompted large one. OpenAI's optimisation guide lists both, shorter prompts and a smaller, cheaper, faster model trained for one task, as reasons to fine-tune. Whether either pays is arithmetic, done below.

**Where the run happens.** As of 2026-10-28, OpenAI's deprecations page says self-serve fine-tuning closed to organisations that had never used it on 7 May 2026; from 2 July 2026, creating a job needs inference on a fine-tuned model within the past 60 days; and on 6 January 2027 active existing customers can no longer create jobs. Fine-tuned models keep serving until their base model is deprecated, and a notice dated 14 October 2026 retires a group of GPT-4 series models on 14 January 2027, with their fine-tuned versions on the same date. An OpenAI fine-tune started this week is a model with a fixed expiry and no retraining path after 6 January 2027. The remaining paths are another provider's tuning service, with its own base-model lifetimes, or an open-weight model you train and serve, which means owning GPU serving.

**What other teams do.** Menlo Ventures' survey of 495 US enterprise AI decision-makers, published December 2025, found prompt design dominant, then retrieval, with fine-tuning among the techniques it calls niche and used mainly by frontier teams. The survey gives no percentage for this. Read it as a base rate: most teams that ask the question end up not fine-tuning.

The options combine. The common production shape is weights for format and labels, retrieval for facts. Ovadia's 0.830 against 0.875 is a warning about the other combination: fine-tuning on the same documents retrieval serves did not add to retrieval there.

## A worked decision

The case is a scenario; only the GPU price is published. A router assigns each support ticket to one of 40 queues, at 600,000 tickets a month. A hosted model does it today with a 5,500-token prompt (queue definitions and 30 examples) plus a 500-token ticket, so 6,000 input tokens a call, and a one-word output. At a scenario price of $1.00 per million input tokens, a ticket costs $0.006, so 600,000 tickets cost $3,600 a month. Measured on 1,000 labelled tickets the router is right 91% of the time; product wants 94%.

The alternative is a LoRA adapter on an 8B open-weight model trained on 20,000 historical tickets with their final queue, served on one dedicated H100 SXM at Lambda's on-demand $4.29 per GPU-hour (checked 2026-10-28).

One GPU for a 730-hour month costs 4.29 × 730 = $3,131.70. A prompted ticket costs 6,000 × $1.00 / 1,000,000 = $0.006. Break-even is 3,131.70 / 0.006 ≈ 521,950 tickets a month.
</Predict>

At 600,000 tickets the single GPU saves 3,600 − 3,131.70 = $468.30 a month. That does not pay for a data pipeline, an eval set and a retrain when the queue list changes, and one GPU is not a production service. A second GPU for availability doubles serving to $6,263.40 and moves break-even to 6,263.40 / 0.006 ≈ 1,043,900 tickets a month. On cost alone, the answer is no until traffic nearly doubles, or until the GPUs are shared with other adapters the way LoRAX shares them.

Accuracy is the better argument. If the router sits at 91% because 40 queue boundaries do not fit in instructions and 30 static examples, a model trained on 20,000 labelled tickets is the standard fix for a label-boundary problem, and LoRA Land is the reason to expect a small tuned model to compete. A cheaper experiment comes first: replace the 30 static examples with the 10 most similar resolved tickets retrieved per request, and measure on the same 1,000 tickets. If that reaches 94%, keep the prompted path and own no GPUs.

The memo that ends the meeting: the failure is behaviour (queue boundaries), not knowledge; dynamic examples get two weeks; if they stay under 94%, train the adapter on an open-weight base whose licence legal has read, serve it on two GPUs, and accept serving costs above today's $3,600 because the goal is accuracy; retrain whenever the queue list changes, and evaluate on tickets newer than the training data.

## What to measure after deciding

- Paired accuracy of the tuned model against the prompted baseline on the same held-out cases, drawn from traffic after the training data's cutoff. The baseline is the prompted model you would otherwise ship, not the untuned base with no prompt.
- Regressions outside the task: refusals, format compliance, tool-call validity and anything else the product still asks the model to do.
- For any output with factual content, the hallucination rate on facts the base model already got right, which is the failure Gekhman et al. measured.
- Cost per correct outcome, including the second GPU and the retraining cadence, not cost per call.
- The base model's deprecation date and the time a retrain takes, written in the runbook next to the person who owns it.

## When this is wrong

The knowledge and behaviour split blurs at the edges. Internal vocabulary, product codes and house jargon are knowledge, but small and stable, and Ovadia's paraphrase result (0.504 to 0.588 for Mistral when each chunk appeared ten ways) says repetition helps a model absorb some of it. A glossary in the prompt or retrieved per request is still usually cheaper to keep current than a retrain.

The evidence has a scale. Ovadia et al. measured 7B models, Gekhman et al. one PaLM 2 model on closed-book question answering, and LoRA Land compared against GPT-4 in April 2024. None of them measured reinforcement fine-tuning or current frontier models, and the gap between a tuned 8B model and a prompted frontier model in late 2026 is something you measure on your own task, not something these papers settle.

The worked case assumes dedicated GPUs priced on demand. Reserved pricing, serverless adapter hosting or one GPU shared across ten adapters moves the break-even by large factors, so redo the arithmetic with your contract before the cost line decides anything.

Platform facts in this playbook carry a date because they moved twice in 2026. Re-read the provider's deprecations page before a plan depends on any of them.
