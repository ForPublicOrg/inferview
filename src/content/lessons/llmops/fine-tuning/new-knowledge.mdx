## The problem the paper answers

"Fine-tune it on our docs so it knows our products" assumes supervised fine-tuning is a way to write facts into a model. The open question was whether a model that answers worse after such a run has been given bad data or has met something structural. Gekhman et al. (EMNLP 2024) built a controlled experiment to separate the two: hold the fine-tuning set size fixed, vary only the share of examples whose answer the model did not already know, and measure what happens to accuracy on everything else.

## The mechanism in engineering terms

The setup is closed-book question answering on EntityQuestions with a PaLM 2-S base model: 12 relations for training and in-distribution testing, 7 more relations held out to test transfer. The fine-tuning set has 6,142 examples; the test sets have 10,481 in-distribution and 5,848 out-of-distribution examples.

The part worth copying is the labelling. Before fine-tuning, each training question is put to the base model through 10 random 4-shot prompts, with a greedy answer and 16 samples at temperature 0.5 per prompt. The share of correct answers sorts each example into one of four categories, which the authors call SliCK:

- **HighlyKnown**: the greedy answer is always right.
- **MaybeKnown**: the greedy answer is sometimes right.
- **WeaklyKnown**: the greedy answer is never right, but sampling sometimes is.
- **Unknown**: no decoding ever produces the right answer.

The natural training split came out 24% HighlyKnown, 23% MaybeKnown, 17% WeaklyKnown and 36% Unknown. Over a third of a plausible-looking QA dataset was facts the model did not have.

Three findings follow. First, Unknown examples are fitted much more slowly than Known ones: by the time the model has fitted most Known examples, it has fitted only a small fraction of the Unknown ones. Second, while they remain unfitted they do little harm; the damage arrives as the model fits them. With half the set Unknown, test accuracy was 43.0 at the dev-set peak (5 to 10 epochs) and 38.8 at convergence (50 epochs). Third, the harm is not confined to the Unknown questions. Fitting answers the model cannot ground in what it knows teaches it to answer without that grounding, and the out-of-distribution relations, which share no facts with training, degrade the same way.

## The key number

The authors fit test accuracy as a linear function of how many Known and Unknown training examples the model has fitted, each divided by the dataset size:

accuracy = β₀ + β_kn × (N_kn / |D|) + β_unk × (N_unk / |D|)

In distribution the fit gives β₀ = 36.9, β_kn = 7.3 and β_unk = −8.3, with R² of 0.86. Out of distribution it gives 36.2, 3.2 and −3.0, with R² of 0.95. Fitting an Unknown example costs about as much accuracy as fitting a Known one gains.

Two more results change what a team does with the finding. Training only on MaybeKnown examples gave the best early-stopped accuracy (43.6, against 40.5 for HighlyKnown only and 43.5 for the natural mix; single runs, so the margins are small), which says examples the model half-knows teach it to use what it has, while examples it fully knows teach little. And relabelling Unknown examples as "I don't know" held accuracy on the questions the model chose to answer at 61.8 at both early stopping and convergence, against 43.0 and 38.8 with the original labels, while the share of questions answered fell only from 58.7% to 55.6%.

## What changed in practice

**Knowledge goes in retrieval.** Ovadia et al.'s numbers say the same thing from the other side. On 910 questions about events after the models' cutoffs, fine-tuning Mistral 7B on the source documents moved accuracy from 0.481 to 0.504, retrieval moved it to 0.875, and plain fine-tuning took Llama 2 7B from 0.353 down to 0.219. A product catalogue, a policy manual or a price list changes more often than anyone retrains, and every fact a fine-tune absorbs is one more fact the model will state confidently after it changes.

**Probe the dataset against the base.** The SliCK labelling is a curation step any team can run before supervised fine-tuning: sample the base model on each training example's question several times and flag the examples it never gets right. Those are the examples that will teach confident guessing. Drop them, move the facts they carry into retrieval, or relabel them as abstentions where abstaining is the behaviour you want. The paper's annotation took about 15 million inference steps across its whole corpus; a product dataset of a few thousand examples at 10 samples each is tens of thousands of calls.

**Early-stop on a task metric.** The harm arrives late in training. A dev set scored on the task, with the checkpoint taken at its peak, avoids most of it; the last checkpoint after a fixed epoch count is where it lives.

## Explaining it to a product manager in two minutes

Fine-tuning is good at teaching a model how to answer and bad at teaching it what is true. When a training example asks something the model does not know, the model learns slowly, and once it has learned the answer it also learns that answering confidently without knowing is acceptable; a Google and Technion study measured accuracy falling as more such examples were learned, including on questions unrelated to the training data. In a separate study, retrieval, meaning looking the facts up at answer time, beat fine-tuning on new facts by a wide margin. So for "knows our products" we keep product facts in a searchable store the model reads at answer time, which also updates the day the catalogue changes, and we fine-tune, if at all, for tone and format.

## When this is wrong

The study used one model, PaLM 2-S, on short-answer closed-book QA about entities. Long-form answers, reasoning tasks and much larger models may fit Unknown examples on a different curve, and the paper does not test them. The coefficients are specific to this setup.

Supervised fine-tuning on a few thousand examples is not the only way weights learn facts. The authors' conclusion is that models acquire factual knowledge mostly in pretraining, and continued pretraining on a large domain corpus is a different regime, with its own forgetting costs, covered in the LoRA walkthrough.

Ovadia et al.'s paraphrase result (0.504 to 0.588 for Mistral 7B with ten rewrites per chunk) shows repetition helps a model absorb new facts. For a small, stable vocabulary the model must use fluently, such as internal product codes, paraphrase-heavy training can be worth its cost; for anything that changes, it is a retrain schedule nobody keeps.

SliCK's categories depend on the probe. Ten 4-shot prompts with a fixed sampling budget is the paper's choice; a fact the model knows only under a specific phrasing will be labelled Unknown, so a probe built from your product's actual phrasing is better than one built from templates.
