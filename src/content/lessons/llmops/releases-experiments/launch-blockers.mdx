## A gate that could only say yes

On Thursday 24 April 2025 OpenAI began rolling out an update to GPT-4o in ChatGPT and finished on the Friday. By the Sunday it was clear the model was sycophantic in ways OpenAI describes as validating doubts, fuelling anger and urging impulsive actions. A system prompt change went out late on the Sunday, a full rollback started on Monday 28 April, and the rollback took around 24 hours. OpenAI's postmortem of 2 May is unusually specific about the review process, and the useful part for anyone who owns a release gate is the decision it describes.

The update combined several candidate improvements (better use of user feedback, memory and fresher data), each of which had looked beneficial on its own. One added a reward signal from users' thumbs-up and thumbs-down. The review then ran its usual instruments. Offline evaluations, including those testing behaviour, generally looked good. Small A/B tests indicated that the users who tried the model liked it. Safety evaluations, which are blocking, focus mostly on direct harms from malicious users. Expert testers spent time with the model, and some said its behaviour felt slightly off, though sycophancy was not explicitly flagged; they were more concerned with tone and style. There was no deployment evaluation tracking sycophancy.

OpenAI then states the question it faced: whether to withhold the update despite positive evaluations and A/B results, based only on the subjective flags of the expert testers. It launched, and calls that the wrong call. Simon Willison's reading is that the biggest miss was letting automated evals and A/B tests overrule the human checks.

The [success metrics lesson](/learn/evals/evals-in-ci/success-metrics) uses the same incident to show why a reaction metric rewards agreement, and the [alignment topics lesson](/learn/safety/hallucination/alignment-topics) uses it for the training-signal side. This lesson is about the gate.

## Why every instrument passed

Each instrument measured what it was built to measure. The offline suite had no class for the failure. The A/B test read thumbs, preferences and usage, which a more agreeable model improves. The safety evals that block launches were scoped to direct harms, and OpenAI says its evaluations of misbehaviour such as hallucination and deception had been used to track progress rather than to block.

The structural problem is the asymmetry between the two kinds of evidence. A quantitative instrument had a blocking path: a failing safety eval stops the launch without a meeting. A qualitative finding had none. It arrived as a comment, framed as tone, with no severity, no reproduction count and no owner whose job was to act on it. So the decision was framed as an exception: hold the launch against positive numbers on the strength of a feeling. A gate built that way defaults to yes, and every reviewer who has watched a hold be argued down learns to phrase findings more softly next time.

The failure class was also not new. OpenAI writes that it had discussed sycophancy risks in GPT-4o for a while and had research on mirroring and emotional reliance that had not become part of the deployment process. A known failure class without a deployment eval is the cheapest gap to close, and the most common one in application teams too: the team knows what its model does badly, and the gate does not check it.

## What OpenAI changed

The commitments are a gate design. Behaviour issues (hallucination, deception, reliability and personality are named) become blocking concerns in the safety review. OpenAI commits to blocking launches on proxy measurements or qualitative signals even when A/B metrics look good. Sycophancy evaluations join the deployment process. Spot checks and interactive testing get more weight in the final decision. An opt-in alpha phase collects direct feedback from users who ask to give it. Model updates are announced, subtle or not, with known limitations.

Two of those commitments are the ones an application team can copy directly: a blocking path for a qualitative finding, and an eval for every known failure class before the change ships.

## A qualitative finding with a place in the gate

The design below is this lesson's proposal for a team shipping model, prompt and fine-tune changes on its own product. It is not OpenAI's process, which the postmortem describes only at the level above.

**A behaviour spec with failure classes.** A short document listing the behaviours the product must not show, each with two or three example transcripts: agrees with a false claim the user asserts, abandons a correct answer under pushback, validates a risky plan, invents a policy, refuses a supported task. This is the team's equivalent of a model spec, and its value is that a reviewer's finding can cite a class instead of describing a feeling.

**A blocking eval per known class.** Each class has a small paired eval run against production before any change: the same prompts through both configurations, scored on the behaviour, with a non-inferiority margin. Multi-turn where the behaviour is multi-turn, since pushback is a second turn. With memory or retrieved user context on and off where the product has them, because OpenAI found memory made sycophancy worse in some cases.

**A structured review.** Named reviewers, a fixed probe set drawn from the failure classes plus a time-boxed free exploration, and a finding format: transcript, the spec class or "new class", severity (blocking or not), and how many times the reviewer reproduced it. The format is what turns "felt off" into something a meeting can act on.

**An owner who can hold the launch.** The behaviour owner, who is not the launch owner, decides whether a blocking finding holds. A held launch has three exits: the finding becomes eval cases within an agreed number of days and the candidate passes them; the launch owner and behaviour owner sign a written waiver that names the risk, the monitoring metric and the rollback trigger; or the finding does not reproduce in front of a second reviewer. Silence is not an exit.

**The default inverted.** With an open blocking finding the burden is on shipping, not on the reviewer. That single rule is what the April 2025 process lacked.

## How much a review can see

<Predict id="ops-re-blockers-probes" question="How many conversations from that class must a review contain to see at least one failure with 95% probability?" answer={149} tolerance={2} unit="conversations">
<Fragment slot="prompt">
<p>Scenario inputs: a candidate abandons a correct answer under pushback in 2% of the conversations where a user pushes back. Each review conversation of that kind independently shows the failure or not.</p>
</Fragment>
<p>The chance of seeing no failure in n conversations is 0.98ⁿ. Setting 0.98ⁿ = 0.05 gives n = ln 0.05 / ln 0.98 = 148.3, so <b>149 conversations</b>. A review with 30 such conversations sees the failure with probability 1 − 0.98³⁰ = 45%; at a 1% rate, 1 − 0.99³⁰ = 26%.</p>
</Predict>

The arithmetic cuts both ways. Hands-on review is a detector for failures that are common in the conversations reviewers have, and it misses rare ones; rare failures need an eval with enough cases or production monitoring. But when a handful of reviewers each notice the same thing in a few dozen conversations, the arithmetic says the behaviour is probably not rare in those kinds of conversation. That is an argument for treating a reproduced qualitative finding as a measurement with a small sample, not as an opinion.

The A/B test cannot substitute. A behaviour that appears in a narrow slice of conversations changes a whole-population thumbs rate by an amount the test is not powered to see, and if users like the behaviour, it moves the metric the wrong way.

## Converting a finding into an eval

The conversion is where the waiting time goes, so plan it. Take the reviewer's transcripts and write variants that keep the trigger and vary the topic: a few dozen prompts per class, labelled by the behaviour owner on a binary pass or fail. By the arithmetic above, a set that size catches a behaviour that is common in its trigger and misses a rare one, which is the right first target for a finding a reviewer could see by hand. Run them paired through production and candidate. A candidate that fails more of them than production by more than the margin stays held. The labels cost expert minutes per case, and the [labelling lesson](/learn/evals/eval-thinking/labelling-operation) covers what that operation costs; the point here is that the conversion has a calendar cost, so the gate states it, and a team that cannot convert in the time allowed signs a waiver instead of quietly shipping.

Each converted class stays in the suite. After a year the blocking suite is the team's record of every behaviour that has ever held a launch, which is the most useful eval set it owns.

## Users who ask to test

An opt-in alpha gives direct feedback from users who chose to give it. It is a qualitative instrument with a self-selected population: enthusiasts who use the product heavily and say so. It finds failures in workflows the team does not use, and it cannot estimate rates or stand in for the A/B test. Route its findings into the same format and the same owner as internal review, or they become a second channel of comments with no path to block.

<p>So the first step is to measure the slice: take the reviewers' transcripts, write thirty to fifty variants of a customer disputing a correct refund decision, and score production and candidate on whether the policy answer holds after pushback. That fits in a day with two reviewers labelling.</p>
<p>If the candidate holds the policy as well as production, the finding is closed and the launch goes. ## When this is wrong

A blocking path for qualitative findings can become a veto by taste. The finding format, the reproduction requirement and the conversion deadline exist to stop that; without them the gate trades one failure (shipping a known problem) for another (holding launches on preference), and the second is quieter but still costly.

Reviewers drift. The people doing hands-on review learn the model and stop noticing what a new user would, and OpenAI's own account says its testers noticed tone and style rather than sycophancy. Rotate reviewers and keep the fixed probe set, so the review does not depend on who has fresh eyes.

Some behaviours do not show in any pre-launch window. Reliance and over-trust develop over weeks of use, and the postmortem names emotional reliance as a research area rather than a deployment eval. For those, the gate is production monitoring with a rollback trigger, and the honest launch note says the behaviour was not measured.

The evidence is one vendor's account of one consumer release with a training change behind it. A team changing a prompt on a pinned API model faces smaller shifts, and its qualitative review can be lighter; the structure (a format, an owner, an exit) still applies, at whatever size the change deserves.
