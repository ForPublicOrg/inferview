## The design a reviewer signs

An A/B test of a model or prompt change is reviewed on five choices made before the first user is assigned: the unit of randomisation, the sample size, one primary metric with a short list of guardrails, the sample ratio check, and an error calculation at the unit that was randomised. Get those right and the result means what it says. Get the last one wrong and a test can report significance on noise, which is the most common way LLM experiments fail review, because the natural unit of an LLM product (a conversation, a turn, a tool call) is not the unit anyone should randomise.

## Randomise by user or account

Assignment by stable hash is the usual mechanism; the choice is what to hash. Request-level assignment is wrong for almost every LLM feature, for reasons that do not arise in a ranking test.

**History crosses arms.** In a multi-turn conversation, turn 3 is generated from turns 1 and 2. If those came from the other arm, turn 3 measures a blend. Memory features extend the same leak across sessions: arm A writes a memory, arm B reads it next week.

**Users adapt to the model.** A user who learns that the assistant needs a precise question asks precise questions in both arms. Per-request assignment measures the population's average adaptation, not either model.

**Accounts share context.** In a B2B product, colleagues share documents, saved prompts and retrieved knowledge. One user's arm-B outputs pasted into a shared doc become another user's retrieval context in arm A. Randomise by account when users in an account share state, and accept that the number of independent units is now the number of accounts.

Use a salt per experiment in the hash, so that two concurrent experiments do not assign the same users to their treatment arms together.

## Sample size before launch

For a binary outcome such as task resolution, the users per arm to detect a change from p₁ to p₂ with a two-sided test at alpha 0.05 and power 0.8 come from the pooled-variance formula: n = (z₀.₉₇₅ × √(2p̄(1 − p̄)) + z₀.₈ × √(p₁(1 − p₁) + p₂(1 − p₂)))² / (p₂ − p₁)², with z₀.₉₇₅ = 1.960, z₀.₈ = 0.842 and p̄ the mean of the two rates.

For 70% to 72%: p̄ = 0.71, so the first term is 1.960 × √0.4118 = 1.258 and the second is 0.842 × √0.4116 = 0.540. Their sum is 1.798, squared 3.232, divided by 0.02² = 0.0004: **8,080 users per arm**, rounded up. Halving the effect to 70% to 71% needs 32,647 per arm, about four times as many, because n scales with 1/δ². Raising power to 0.9 at the original effect needs 10,816.

The minimum detectable effect is a product decision, stated in the experiment document with its reason: a model change that saves money needs to show it is not worse by more than an agreed margin, which is a one-sided non-inferiority test with its own sample size; a change that costs more needs to show a lift worth the cost. Running until the result looks interesting is neither.

The offline analogue uses the same algebra. Miller's paper gives n = (z_α/2 + z_β)² (ω² + σ²_A/K_A + σ²_B/K_B) / δ², where ω² is the variance of true scores across questions, σ² the variance of a model's answers to one question and K the number of resamples per question. With ω² = 1/9, no within-question variance, a 3-point difference, alpha 0.05 and power 0.8, the paper's worked example needs about 969 questions, and it recommends at least 1,000 for a new eval. The K terms are the part that has no online equivalent: offline you can buy precision by resampling the same question, online each user arrives once.

## Errors at the unit you randomised

Anthropic's evaluation team reports that clustered standard errors on popular evals can be over three times as large as naive ones. Miller's Table 4 gives the real numbers on Anthropic models: on DROP, where several questions share a passage, the naive standard error is 0.44 and the clustered one 1.34, a ratio of 3.05; MGSM goes from 0.86% to 1.62%; RACE-H from 0.46% to 0.51%. A ratio of 3.05 in standard error is 3.05² = 9.3 in variance: the naive analysis claims the precision of 9.3 times as many independent questions as DROP has.

The online version is the same mistake with conversations. Randomise by user, measure resolution per conversation, and treat conversations as independent, and the test is overconfident by the design effect. Kish's formula for clusters of average size m with intraclass correlation ρ is Deff = 1 + (m − 1)ρ. Users who resolve one conversation tend to resolve the next, so ρ is positive, and the more conversations each user contributes, the less each extra conversation is worth.

## One primary metric, a few guardrails

The primary metric is the one the ship decision rests on, measured at the user or account level, and it is an outcome rather than a reaction: resolution without re-contact, task completion, revenue. The [success metrics lesson](/learn/evals/evals-in-ci/success-metrics) builds that set; the experimental consequence is that a second primary metric halves the alpha each one gets under a Bonferroni correction, so a test with two primaries needs more users or accepts a higher false positive rate, stated in advance.

Guardrails are tested for non-inferiority with written margins: cost per resolved task, p95 time to first token, escalation rate, refusal rate, and any safety flag rate. They are the metrics a cheaper or faster model is most likely to worsen while the primary metric looks fine.

The metric to keep out of the primary slot is the in-product reaction. OpenAI describes its small A/B tests of ChatGPT model updates as reading aggregate thumbs up and down, side-by-side preferences and usage patterns; for the April 2025 GPT-4o update those tests indicated that the small number of users who tried it liked it, and it was rolled back for sycophancy within days. A thumbs metric measures liking accurately, and a model that agrees more is liked more. The [launch blockers lesson](/learn/llmops/releases-experiments/launch-blockers) covers what the gate needs beyond the A/B test.

## Check the sample ratio first

A sample ratio mismatch is a difference between the planned split and the observed one, and Fabijan and colleagues, from experiments at four companies on more than 25 products, describe it as a symptom with many causes, any of which can make a bad change look good. Check it before any metric, with a chi-squared goodness-of-fit test on the user counts.

A 50/50 test logs 10,240 users in A and 9,760 in B. Expected: 10,000 each. χ² = 240² / 10,000 + 240² / 10,000 = 11.52, one degree of freedom, p = 0.00069. A 51.2/48.8 split looks harmless on a dashboard and is very unlikely to come from a fair split of 20,000 users.

The causes specific to LLM features are worth checking in this order, because each also biases the outcome metric. Exposure logged when the response completes, so the arm with slower time to first token loses users who abandoned while waiting, and those were the least satisfied users. Errors or timeouts in one arm that drop the request before logging. A fallback that retries failed arm-B requests on the production model and logs them under A. Bot or abuse filters that run on response content and so remove different users from each arm. Assignment at the first message and exposure at the first response, so users who never got a response are missing from one arm only.

A mismatch is not corrected by reweighting. Find the cause, fix the logging or the assignment, and restart the test.

## Peeking

A fixed-horizon test is valid only at its horizon. Reading the p-value every day and stopping at the first value below 0.05 inflates the false positive rate well past 5%, because each look is another chance for noise to cross the line. Either commit to the sample size and read once, or use a sequential method designed for repeated looks, and write which one in the experiment document before launch.

## The arithmetic in code

```python
import math
import random
from statistics import NormalDist

Z = NormalDist().inv_cdf

def users_per_arm(p1, p2, alpha=0.05, power=0.8):
    """Two proportions, two-sided test, pooled variance under the null."""
    za, zb = Z(1 - alpha / 2), Z(power)
    pbar = (p1 + p2) / 2
    num = za * math.sqrt(2 * pbar * (1 - pbar)) + zb * math.sqrt(p1 * (1 - p1) + p2 * (1 - p2))
    return math.ceil((num / (p2 - p1)) ** 2)

def srm_p(n_a, n_b, share_a=0.5):
    """Chi-squared goodness of fit, one degree of freedom."""
    n = n_a + n_b
    e_a, e_b = n * share_a, n * (1 - share_a)
    chi2 = (n_a - e_a) ** 2 / e_a + (n_b - e_b) ** 2 / e_b
    return math.erfc(math.sqrt(chi2 / 2))

def clustered_diff(arm_a, arm_b):
    """Each arm is a list of (resolved, conversations) per user. Returns the difference
    in per-conversation rates, its user-clustered standard error and the naive one."""
    def rate_and_var(users):
        s = sum(r for r, _ in users)
        m = sum(c for _, c in users)
        p, k = s / m, len(users)
        return p, k / (k - 1) * sum((r - p * c) ** 2 for r, c in users) / m ** 2
    (pa, va), (pb, vb) = rate_and_var(arm_a), rate_and_var(arm_b)
    ma, mb = sum(c for _, c in arm_a), sum(c for _, c in arm_b)
    naive = math.sqrt(pa * (1 - pa) / ma + pb * (1 - pb) / mb)
    return pb - pa, math.sqrt(va + vb), naive

print(users_per_arm(0.70, 0.72))        # 8080
print(users_per_arm(0.70, 0.71))        # 32647
print(round(srm_p(10_240, 9_760), 5))   # 0.00069

# 2,000 users per arm, 5 conversations each; each user's resolution propensity
# varies, so one user's conversations are correlated.
rng = random.Random(7)
def arm(base, n=2000):
    users = []
    for _ in range(n):
        p = min(max(rng.gauss(base, 0.25), 0.0), 1.0)
        users.append((sum(rng.random() < p for _ in range(5)), 5))
    return users

diff, se, naive = clustered_diff(arm(0.70), arm(0.72))
print(f"diff {diff:.4f}  clustered se {se:.4f}  naive se {naive:.4f}")
```

On Python 3.12 the last line prints a difference of 0.0150 with a clustered standard error of 0.0091 and a naive one of 0.0065. The naive test gives z = 2.31, p = 0.021, and ships; the clustered test gives z = 1.65, p = 0.099, and does not. The simulation is underpowered on purpose: 2,000 users per arm is well under the 3,556 the design effect asks for.

## When this is wrong

The normal approximation behind every formula here assumes reasonably large counts. For rare outcomes, such as a safety flag in 0.1% of conversations, the counts in each arm are small enough that exact or simulation-based tests are the right tool, and a guardrail on a rare event usually cannot be powered at all; it is monitored, not tested.

The within-user correlation is an input you rarely know before the first test. An A/A test on the same metric measures it, and until one exists, the cluster-robust error computed at analysis time is the safe default, at the cost of a sample size that may turn out too small.

Randomising by account removes interference inside accounts but not between them. If accounts share public knowledge bases, marketplaces or reviews, one arm's outputs can still reach the other, and the test underestimates the effect of a full rollout.

A short test measures novelty. Users may try a new assistant behaviour more in its first week than in its tenth, and outcomes that take weeks to appear (churn, re-contact after a month, reliance) are outside any test that ends in days.

Some changes cannot be A/B tested within the deadline at all, because a provider retires the control model. The [release ladder](/learn/llmops/releases-experiments/release-ladder) treats that case; the statistics here say only how much evidence the available days can buy.
