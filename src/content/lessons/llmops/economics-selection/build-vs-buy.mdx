## The decision is three decisions

"Build or buy" gets argued as one question and is three, with different inputs and different people who own the answer:

1. **Buy an AI product or build the feature.** A support product with an agent inside it, a coding assistant, a meeting summariser, against a feature the team builds on model APIs.
2. **Call a hosted API or host weights.** For a feature the team builds, whether the model runs at a provider or on GPUs the team operates.
3. **Adapt or train.** Prompting and retrieval, fine-tuning an existing model, or pretraining one.

The order matters. The second decision only exists for features that survive the first, and the third only once the second says where the model runs. Teams that start from the third ("we should have our own model") skip the two questions that usually end the conversation.

Menlo Ventures' survey of 495 U.S. enterprise AI decision-makers in November 2025 found 76% of AI use cases purchased rather than built, up from 53% the year before. That is a description of what enterprises did, from an investor in AI companies, and it says nothing about which of those purchases paid off. It does set the default a proposal to build will be compared with.

## The inputs that change it

**Differentiation.** Whether the feature's quality is the reason customers choose the product. The applied-LLMs authors list text-to-SQL, documentation chatbots and knowledge-base support bots as general problems others are already solving; building one is justified only if the version a vendor sells cannot do the job on your data.

**Data sensitivity and residency.** Where inputs and outputs may be processed and stored, for how long, and who may read them. A bought product holds transcripts under its own terms; a hosted API holds them under the provider's retention and regional processing options; self-hosted weights keep them inside the network. The applied-LLMs authors note self-hosting may be the only way to use a model on data that cannot leave.

**Volume and growth.** Fixed costs pay off only above a volume. That holds for an in-house team against a per-outcome price ([Unit economics of an AI feature](/learn/llmops/economics-selection/unit-economics)) and for GPUs against a per-token price ([Self-host or API](/learn/inference/gateways-finops/self-host-breakeven)). Growth matters because the break-even is computed against a moving price.

**Team skills.** Operating a serving fleet, building evals and running fine-tuning are three different skills, and a team that has none of them pays for learning in calendar time.

**Time to value.** A purchased product can be in a trial within weeks; a built feature needs an eval set before anyone can say it works.

**Deprecation and vendor risk.** Anthropic gives at least 60 days' notice before retiring a publicly released model; Claude Sonnet 4.5 was deprecated on 30 September 2026 and retires on 30 November 2026. OpenAI closed self-serve fine-tuning to new organisations on 7 May 2026 and ends new jobs for existing customers on 6 January 2027, while inference on existing fine-tuned models continues until their base models are deprecated. Every option rests on someone's schedule: the provider's, the product vendor's, or the team's own.

## The options and when each wins

**Buy the product** when the feature is not differentiating, a vendor sells it with a published price and outcome definition, the data terms are acceptable, and volume is below the point where an in-house team's fixed cost is covered. What you keep doing: owning the outcome metric, running a trial on your own traffic, and keeping a copy of the transcripts if the contract allows it.

**Build on a hosted API** when the feature differentiates or the products on sale do not fit the workflow, and the provider's data terms are acceptable. This is the default for most built features. Huyen's platform description is the list of what the team then owns: context construction, guardrails, a router, a gateway, caches, observability. She notes a gateway is relatively straightforward to build and available off the shelf, which is a reminder that the platform pieces are themselves build-or-buy decisions.

**Host weights** when data cannot leave the network, when volume is high and steady enough to keep GPUs busy, or when the team needs a model frozen beyond any provider's retirement schedule. The applied-LLMs authors add rate limits and deprecations as reasons. The price is a serving fleet and the guardrails a provider would otherwise run, which Huyen flags as the team's to build.

**Adapt rather than train** almost always. The applied-LLMs authors' rule is no GPUs before product-market fit; their example is BloombergGPT, pretrained on 363 billion tokens by a team of nine and outclassed on those tasks by gpt-3.5-turbo and gpt-4 within a year. Inside adaptation the order is prompting, then retrieval, then fine-tuning, which they call heavy machinery because it brings annotation, training runs, evaluation and eventually self-hosting. Fine-tuning wins when a measured gap remains on a task with stable inputs, labelled data exists or can be bought, and the team accepts owning the tuned model's lifecycle. On OpenAI that path is now closed to new organisations, so a plan that names provider fine-tuning has to name the provider and check the date.

## The costs teams forget

Per-unit comparisons leave out the lines that do not scale with volume:

- **People.** The engineers who build the feature and then keep changing it as the product changes; the domain reviewers who label failures.
- **Evals.** An eval set has to exist before launch and be maintained after, because traffic drifts and the model changes under it. Every model migration reruns it.
- **On-call.** A built feature pages someone when quality drops at 2 a.m.; a self-hosted model pages someone when a GPU node fails too. A bought product moves that page to the vendor and leaves you with the escalation path.
- **Migrations.** Every retirement notice starts a project: rerun evals, fix the prompts that regress, stage the rollout. With 60 days of notice, the team needs that pipeline ready before the notice arrives.

Write these as line items in the decision, with the names of the people who will carry them.

## Worked decision

Scenario: a 400-person B2B software company with three requests on the table.

**Customer support deflection.** 30,000 conversations a month, helpdesk already in place, no differentiation, transcripts may be held by a processor under the existing DPA. Decision: buy. At a 60% resolution rate that is 18,000 outcomes a month, and the unit-economics lesson's $50,000-a-month in-house team alone would add $50,000 / 18,000 = $2.78 to each one, against a $0.99 price. What the team keeps: a four-week trial on real traffic, resolution measured on a 7-day reopen definition alongside the vendor's own count, and a quarterly review of the per-outcome bill against that measured rate.

**Contract clause extraction inside the product.** It is the reason customers buy the product, the documents are customer contracts, and two enterprise customers require EU processing. Decision: build on a hosted API whose regional processing and retention terms match those contracts (the selection playbook covers how to read them), with a labelled set of 300 clauses per customer type (scenario) as the eval, and a nightly batch path for back-catalogue documents at the Batch API's 50% discount. Not self-hosted: volume is about 300 million tokens a month (scenario), far under the 5.83 billion where one node breaks even, and nobody on the team has run a serving fleet.

**"Our own model."** A director asks for a fine-tuned model to make extraction a moat. Decision: not this year. The written condition for revisiting: a measured accuracy gap on the eval that prompting and retrieval did not close after a stated effort, at least a few thousand labelled examples, and a named owner for the tuned model's retraining and migration. What the team gives up is a story for the board about proprietary models; what it keeps is a team that ships the feature this quarter.

## What to measure after deciding

For each bought product: resolution or acceptance on your own definition, monthly, beside the vendor's count; the per-outcome bill against that measured rate; and the contract's renewal and data-export terms, calendared.

For each built feature: cost per successful outcome with the fixed costs included; volume against the break-even for the next option (in-house versus product, API versus weights), recomputed when prices change; migration lead time, measured on the last retirement; and the share of the team's time spent on platform pieces a vendor sells.

For self-hosted weights: GPU utilisation through the day, not the average; incidents per month that a provider would have absorbed; and the API price for the same quality on your eval, rechecked every quarter.

## When this is wrong

The three decisions are not always separable. A bought product can include the model choice and the data terms in one contract, so "buy" also answers where the model runs; and some products expose an API, which blurs buy into build.

Volume break-evens assume the alternatives deliver the same quality. When the open model on your GPUs scores below the API model on your eval, the comparison needs the unit-economics arithmetic: a lower token cost bought with a lower success rate can be a loss.

The survey numbers describe 2025 enterprise behaviour in the U.S. and come from an investor; they set a default, not a reason. And regulated settings can override every input here: a regulator or a customer contract that requires a model to be frozen and validated for a stated period can force self-hosting at any volume.
