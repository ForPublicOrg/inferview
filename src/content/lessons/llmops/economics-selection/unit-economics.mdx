## Cost per call is the wrong denominator

A finance dashboard that reports dollars per model call answers a question nobody is asking. The business buys outcomes: a ticket closed, an invoice extracted, a pull request merged. The cost of one outcome has three parts, and the token bill is often the smallest.

- **Cost per attempt.** Every model call in the attempt, including retries, tool calls, retrieval and any judge or guardrail call, plus the non-model services the attempt touches.
- **Attempts per success.** If 60% of attempts succeed, each success carries the cost of 1 / 0.6 = 1.67 attempts.
- **The cost of a failure.** A failed attempt is not free once the tokens are paid for. In support it becomes a human escalation; in extraction, a review queue; in code, a reverted change. A failure can cost more than doing the work without the model, because a person picks up a half-finished task.

Written down:

```python
def per_outcome(attempt_cost, success_rate, failure_cost, baseline_cost):
    """Return (AI cost per AI success, blended cost per unit of work, saving per unit)."""
    per_success = attempt_cost / success_rate
    blended = attempt_cost + (1 - success_rate) * failure_cost
    return per_success, blended, baseline_cost - blended
```

The first number is the one to compare with a vendor's per-outcome price. The second is what one unit of work now costs the company, whoever finishes it. The third is the case for the feature.

## A worked cost model

Scenario, all inputs invented and labelled as such except list prices: a support agent on Claude Sonnet 5.5. A conversation averages six model calls. Each call sends 4,000 tokens of system prompt and tool definitions that stay cached, 5,000 tokens of history and retrieved articles that do not, and returns 500 tokens. Retrieval, a reranker and the ticketing API cost $0.01 per conversation. The agent resolves 60% of conversations; the rest go to a person at $6.00 each, which includes reading the transcript the agent left. Before the agent, every conversation went to a person at $5.50.

Per call, at the list prices:

| Line | Tokens | Price per million | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cached prefix (cache hit) | 4,000 | $0.10 | $0.0004 |
| Uncached input | 5,000 | $2.00 | $0.0100 |
| Output | 500 | $10.00 | $0.0050 |
| **Call total** | | | **$0.0154** |

Six calls make $0.0924 of tokens; with $0.01 of services, an attempt costs $0.1024. Uncached input is 65% of a call's cost; the cached prefix, 42% of the input tokens, is under 3% of it.

Then the three numbers:

- AI cost per AI-resolved conversation: $0.1024 / 0.6 = **$0.171**.
- Blended cost per conversation: $0.1024 + 0.4 × $6.00 = **$2.50**.
- Saving per conversation against the all-human baseline: $5.50 − $2.50 = **$3.00**.

The human escalation is $2.40 of the $2.50, or 96%. The token bill could double and the saving would fall by 9 cents. That proportion holds for any feature where a failure lands on a person, and it is why a cost review that starts from the model invoice usually optimises the wrong term.

<Predict id="ops-unit-breakeven-lift" question="A stronger model costs twice as much per attempt. How many percentage points of resolution rate must it add to pay for itself?" answer={1.71} tolerance={0.05} unit="points">
<p>The extra cost per conversation is one more attempt's worth, $0.1024. Each additional point of resolution moves 1% of conversations from a $6.00 escalation to resolved, which saves $0.06 per conversation. Break-even is $0.1024 / $0.06 = <b>1.71 points</b>. A model at twice the price that lifts resolution from 60% to 62% saves money; one that lifts it to 61% does not.</p>
</Predict>

The same arithmetic sets a floor. The agent breaks even with the all-human baseline when $0.1024 + (1 − s) × $6.00 = $5.50, which gives s = ($0.1024 + $0.50) / $6.00 = 10.0%. Below one resolution in ten, the agent loses money, because every escalation costs 50 cents more than an unassisted ticket.

## What the market charges for an outcome

Intercom sells Fin at $0.99 per outcome and publishes the definition: the customer confirms the issue is resolved, the customer stops asking for help after Fin replies, or Fin completes a configured procedure, including one that ends in a handoff. Each conversation is billed once.

That definition is the first thing to read, because it decides what $0.99 buys. "Stops asking for help" counts a customer who gave up. A procedure that ends in a handoff counts even though a person then works the ticket. A team comparing the vendor's $0.99 with its own $0.171 has to price both on the same definition of success, or the comparison measures the definitions.

Suppose the vendor reached the same 60% on this traffic under the team's strict definition. Its charge per conversation would be at least 0.6 × $0.99 = $0.594, against $0.1024 of variable cost to run the agent in-house. The in-house number leaves out the people who build and operate it. If two engineers, eval maintenance and an on-call share cost $50,000 a month (scenario), building pays only above $50,000 / ($0.99 − $0.171) = 61,025 outcomes a month, which is 101,709 conversations at a 60% rate. Below that volume buying is cheaper, even though the vendor's per-outcome price is 5.8 times the in-house variable cost per outcome.

Three questions change that comparison more than the price does:

1. Whose resolution rate is higher on this traffic, measured on the same definition during a trial on real conversations, not a demo set.
2. Whether outcome pricing aligns incentives: a per-outcome vendor is paid when the customer goes quiet, which is also when a customer gives up.
3. Where the transcripts live. A bought product holds them, and the team's eval set and failure taxonomy are built from them.

## The price moves; the human term does not

Epoch AI measured the price of reaching a fixed benchmark score falling between 9x and 900x per year, depending on the milestone, with GPT-4-level performance on GPQA Diamond falling 40x per year. The authors note the fastest drops are the most recent and may not persist. The applied-LLMs authors made the same observation earlier with two points: davinci-level performance went from $20 to under 10 cents per million tokens in about 18 months.

Apply the slowest of Epoch's rates to the worked model. A 9x fall takes the token bill from $0.0924 to $0.0103 and the attempt from $0.1024 to $0.0203. The blended cost moves from $2.50 to $2.42, about 3%. A five-point gain in resolution moves it by 0.05 × $6.00 = $0.30, nearly four times as much.

So the price trend matters for one decision and hardly at all for two:

- **Build or buy** compares a fixed in-house cost with a per-outcome price. Falling token prices cut only the in-house variable term, which is already small, so they do not rescue a low-volume build.
- **Self-host or API** compares a fixed GPU cost with a per-token price, and that decision does flip with the price trend. [Self-host or API: the break-even and how fast it moves](/learn/inference/gateways-finops/self-host-breakeven) has the arithmetic.
- **Ship the feature or not** depends on the success rate and the failure cost, which no price curve touches.

Constant capability is the condition on every one of those price figures. Teams rarely hold capability constant: they move to the new model at the same price point, so their bill falls less than the curve and their resolution rate should rise instead. Record which of the two the last upgrade bought.

## Instrumenting it

The cost model is only as good as the attribution behind it. Four things make it measurable:

- **A request bill of materials.** Tag every model call, tool call and retrieval with the outcome it belongs to (conversation id, document id) and its purpose. The provider's usage fields give cached, written and uncached tokens per call; the gateway adds the tag. Without the tag, background calls such as titles, summaries and judges disappear into an average.
- **An outcome event with a lag.** Resolved means not reopened within a stated window, which delays the number by that window. Report attempts and costs as they happen, and outcomes when they mature.
- **The failure cost from the other system.** Escalation cost comes from the support team's handling time, not the AI team's estimate, and it differs between a transcript the agent summarised and one it did not.
- **The distribution, not the mean.** A conversation that loops for 30 calls costs as much as five six-call ones. Alert on the tail of calls per outcome.

## When this is wrong

The model assumes escalation cost is marginal: that a resolved conversation saves a person's time the company would otherwise pay for. In the short run support headcount is fixed, and a resolved conversation becomes cash only once hiring slows or the team takes on other work. A cost model presented to finance needs a line saying when the saving becomes cash.

Success is not binary. A conversation can be resolved wrongly: a refund issued that policy did not allow, an answer the customer acted on that was false. Those failures cost more than an escalation and surface weeks later. A model that counts them as successes overstates the saving, and the error grows with the agent's autonomy.

Attempts are not independent. A customer who failed once and comes back costs a second attempt and arrives angrier, so the second escalation costs more. The 1 / success-rate multiplier understates cost when repeat contacts cluster on the hardest issues.

At low volume none of this is the decision. With a few thousand conversations a month, the people cost of building and operating anything dominates every per-unit term, and the question is whether to run the feature at all.
