## The decision

A staff question about AI strategy is rarely about a prompt. One synthesised question bank phrases it as "A regulated financial company wants a model strategy for five LLM features", and the same bank asks for a governance process that satisfies auditors without slowing shipping. Both resolve into four decisions that fit on one page:

1. **Portfolio.** Which use cases go first, ranked by value and risk, and which wait.
2. **Platform primitives.** What is built once and shared: a model gateway, a prompt registry, an eval service, tracing, and data controls.
3. **Decision rights.** Who moves a production label, who approves a new model or vendor, who launches to all users, and who signs a waiver.
4. **Measures.** What leadership looks at each month, per feature and for the platform.

Each decision constrains the next. The portfolio decides which primitives are needed first; the primitives decide what evidence is cheap to produce; the evidence decides which decisions can be delegated to a team instead of a committee.

## Inputs that change it

**Audit and regulation.** The SOC 2 Trust Services Criteria have no AI-specific criteria, and the Cloud Security Alliance's August 2026 note describes auditors improvising. What practitioners report being asked for is concrete: the approval that promoted a model to production, change tickets showing each deployment was approved, tested and reversible, and per-inference logs with the model version, a redacted prompt or its hash, tool calls and the outcome. A firm that will be audited needs those fields from day one, and producing them per team is the expensive way.

**Vendor dependency.** A strategy that names a capability names a vendor's roadmap. OpenAI closed self-serve fine-tuning to new organisations on 7 May 2026 and stops new jobs for existing customers on 6 January 2027. A portfolio item whose plan says "fine-tune on the vendor" now carries a date and a migration.

**How many teams call models, and how.** One feature with one team needs a library and a review. Twelve teams with their own keys, retry code and logging need a gateway. The break-even is computed below.

**Data classes.** Which data may leave for a hosted model, under which retention configuration, decides which use cases are possible on which vendors before value is discussed.

**Labelling capacity.** Every customer-facing feature needs an eval, and every eval needs someone qualified to label. In a lender that is credit and compliance staff whose hours are already allocated.

## The options

### Ordering the portfolio

Score each candidate use case on three axes. Value: the outcome it moves (handle time, error rate, revenue), with the volume it applies to. Risk: who sees the output, whether it can act, whether the action is reversible, whether it touches a regulated decision. Readiness: whether an outcome can be measured and whether labelled examples exist.

Two orderings are defensible.

**Low risk first.** Internal, assistive features where a staff member reads every output. They build the gateway, the traces and the review habit cheaply, and an error costs an employee's time. The cost: the value is small, leadership sees little for a quarter, and the platform is shaped by features that never needed its strictest controls.

**One high-value feature first, with the full controls.** A customer-facing feature built through every gate. Leadership sees value early and the platform is built against its hardest requirement. The cost: the platform is shaped around one feature's needs, the first release carries the most risk while the process is newest, and a failure there sets the whole programme back.

Low risk first wins when audit evidence does not yet exist and the platform team is new. One high-value feature wins when an existing control environment (change management, logging, vendor review) already covers most of what the feature needs, so the new work is the model-specific part.

### Choosing the shared primitives

Chip Huyen's description of a generative AI platform adds components in order: context enhancement, guardrails, a model router and gateway, caching, write actions, observability, orchestration. Her advice to start without an orchestrator generalises: share the components whose absence produces incidents or audit findings, and leave the ones that only affect productivity to teams until two of them build the same thing.

| Primitive | What sharing buys | Share first when |
|---|---|---|
| Gateway | One place for keys, quotas, cost attribution and fallback | More than one team calls a vendor, or any data class is restricted |
| Tracing | Per-call record with model version, prompt version and outcome | Any feature will be audited or has an incident owner |
| Prompt registry | Versioned prompts with labels, so a release is a label move | Prompts change faster than code deploys |
| Eval service | Paired runs of a team's suite against a candidate configuration | Two or more teams have built their own runner |
| Data controls | Redaction, approved vendors per data class, retention settings | Any restricted data reaches a prompt |

The gateway and tracing come first in a regulated firm because the per-inference evidence above is produced at the gateway or nowhere.

### Decision rights

Write a table with one row per decision and four columns: who proposes, who decides, what evidence the decider needs, and who can waive. The rows that matter: moving a production prompt label, adopting a new model version, adding a new vendor, launching to all users, moving a feature to a higher risk tier, and signing a waiver. The [decision rights lesson](/learn/llmops/platform-strategy/decision-rights) builds the table per tier.

### Risk tiers and the NIST functions

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework (1.0 released January 2023, for voluntary use, and being revised under the White House AI Action Plan according to NIST's page) has four functions. GOVERN is cross-cutting; MAP frames the context and risks of a system; MEASURE applies quantitative and qualitative methods; MANAGE allocates resources to the mapped and measured risks. NIST says the actions are not a checklist. That is useful for strategy: the framework names the work, and the tiers decide how much of it each feature does.

A three-tier scheme is enough to start. Tier 1: internal, assistive, a person reads every output. Tier 2: customer-facing text without actions or regulated decisions. Tier 3: the model acts, or its output feeds a regulated decision. MAP for each feature is the tier assignment with its reasons. MEASURE scales with the tier, from spot checks to a paired blocking eval. MANAGE is the rollout plan and rollback trigger. GOVERN is the decision-rights table itself, with GOVERN 2 (accountability structures) and GOVERN 6 (third-party software and data) as the categories an auditor will ask to see mapped.

## A worked decision

Worked case (inputs invented): a mid-sized lender with five proposed features. A policy Q&A assistant for staff; call summaries for contact-centre agents; extraction of fields from loan application documents; a customer chat for account questions; and an agent that drafts collection letters for a person to send. Three teams own them, and each currently calls a hosted model with its own key.

Tiers: policy Q&A and call summaries are tier 1. Customer chat is tier 2. Extraction feeds a credit decision and collection letters reach customers in a regulated process, so both are tier 3 even though a person reviews the letters.

Order: call summaries first (high volume, measurable handle time, tier 1), extraction second because its value is large and its outputs can be checked field by field against a labelled set, customer chat third, collection letters last because the regulatory exposure is highest and the review step makes the value smallest.

So the platform starts smaller than a team: one engineer on the gateway and tracing, owned by the existing infrastructure group, with the registry and eval service deferred until a second team needs them.

### The first 90 days

**Days 1 to 30: inventory.** Every model call in the company: team, vendor, model version, data class, who sees the output, and whether it is in change management. The CSA note warns that shadow AI tools never brought into change management can fall outside a SOC 2 report's scope, which is a finding waiting to happen. Assign tiers. Agree the decision-rights table with compliance and the three team leads, and get it signed.

**Days 31 to 60: the gateway on the critical path.** Per-team keys, quotas and cost attribution, and the per-inference log fields. Tier 2 and 3 traffic must go through it; tier 1 is encouraged to. Call summaries ship through it, with a weekly read of a sample of summaries by contact-centre leads.

**Days 61 to 90: one tier 3 feature through the full ladder.** Extraction gets a labelled set from credit operations, a paired eval against the current manual process, a shadow period and a rollback trigger. The first monthly report goes to the VP.

Not in 90 days: customer chat, collection letters, a self-hosted model, a fine-tuning pipeline. Saying so on the page is part of the strategy.

## What to measure after deciding

Per feature: the outcome it was chosen for, measured against the manual baseline, and the incident count with time to rollback. For the platform: the share of tier 2 and 3 traffic whose logs carry complete fields, and the lead time for a team to ship a change with its evidence. For leadership: spend per feature per outcome, not per token. The [shared platforms lesson](/learn/llmops/platform-strategy/shared-platforms) argues for the platform's single metric.

## When this is wrong

A company with one model feature and one team does not need a platform or a tier scheme; it needs a review and a log. Most of this page applies when a second team starts calling models.

Ranking by value assumes value can be estimated. For a new product line it cannot, and the ranking becomes a ranking of who argued best. In that case rank by risk and readiness, and treat value as the experiment.

The NIST framework is voluntary, under revision, and not a regulation. A sector regulator's rules (model risk management in banking, for example) override any mapping here, and the tier criteria should be written with the firm's own compliance team rather than copied.

The audit evidence list comes from practitioner reports of what auditors ask for, not from AICPA criteria. Different auditors ask for different things, so confirm the list with yours before building to it.
