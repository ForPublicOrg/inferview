## Three decisions that need an owner

A model feature has three decisions that change what users get without a code review catching them: moving the production label to a new prompt version, adopting a new model or model version, and widening a launch to all users. In most teams each is made by whoever has the access. Governance is the act of deciding, per decision and per risk tier, who may make it and on what evidence, and making the tooling enforce that.

The constraint that shapes the design is that the process must satisfy an auditor and must not be cheaper to route around than to follow. The Cloud Security Alliance's August 2026 note on SOC 2 says the Trust Services Criteria contain no AI-specific criteria and auditors are improvising; it also says AI tools never brought into change management can fall outside a report's scope. A process teams route around produces exactly that: an audited process for some features and unaudited ones beside it.

## Tiers by consequence

Tier on what an error costs and who absorbs it, not on which model or technique is used:

- **Tier 1.** Internal and assistive: a staff member reads every output before it affects anyone. Errors cost an employee's time.
- **Tier 2.** Reaches customers as text, with no actions and no input to a regulated decision. Errors cost trust, support load, and sometimes a public incident.
- **Tier 3.** The model takes an action (sends, pays, changes a record) or its output feeds a regulated decision such as credit or employment. Errors can be irreversible or unlawful.

Four questions place a feature: who sees the output first, can it act, can the effect be reversed, and does it feed a decision a regulator governs. The tier is recorded with the answers, because the answers are what a reviewer challenges, and a feature moves up a tier when any answer changes. A tool call added to a tier 2 chat makes it tier 3 that day.

## The rights table

| Decision | Tier 1 | Tier 2 | Tier 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Move the production prompt label | Any team member, with an eval run attached | A second engineer on the team approves; paired eval passes | The feature's named risk owner approves; paired eval and slice review pass |
| Adopt a new model version from an approved vendor | Team lead | Team lead, with the paired eval and a canary | Risk owner and the platform; paired eval, shadow period, rollback trigger written |
| Add a new vendor or data class | Platform and security | Platform and security | Platform, security and compliance |
| Launch to all users | Team lead | Product owner, after a staged rollout | Risk owner and product owner, after the release ladder |
| Waive a failed gate | Team lead | Product owner and risk owner, in writing | Accountable executive, in writing, with an expiry |

The table is enforced where the decision is executed. Langfuse's protected labels are one example of how: once a label is protected, viewer and member roles cannot move or delete it, while admins and owners can. That gives two levels of right, not three, so tier 3 needs either a separate project per tier with different role assignments or an approval step in the deploy pipeline that calls the label API on the approver's behalf. Protected labels are not on Langfuse's Hobby or Core plans; they need Pro with the Teams add-on, Enterprise, or the self-hosted Enterprise Edition, which is a cost line in the governance plan, not a footnote.

## Evidence each tier produces

The evidence list is short when the tooling produces it as a side effect and long when people write it. What practitioners report auditors requesting, per the CSA note: the approval that promoted a model to production with its lineage, change tickets showing each deployment was approved, tested and reversible, per-inference logs with model version, a redacted prompt or prompt hash, tool calls and outcome, and vendor risk records including data-retention settings.

Mapped to the tiers and to NIST AI RMF functions:

- **MAP** (establish the context and frame the risks): the tier record with its four answers, for every feature, written once and updated on change. All tiers.
- **MEASURE** (quantitative, qualitative or mixed methods): tier 1, a spot-check log; tier 2, a paired eval result attached to each label move; tier 3, the paired eval plus slice results and a reviewed sample of shadow outputs.
- **MANAGE** (allocate resources to the mapped and measured risks): tier 2 and 3, a rollout plan with the rollback trigger and its owner; tier 3, the monitoring dashboard named in the plan. MANAGE 4 asks that risk treatments are documented and monitored, which is the waiver register.
- **GOVERN** (cross-cutting): the rights table, the role assignments in the registry and gateway, and the vendor review. GOVERN 2 is accountability structures; GOVERN 6 and MANAGE 3 cover third-party software and data, which is every hosted model.

NIST states that the actions are not a checklist and that the functions can run in any order once GOVERN is in place. Treat the mapping as the index an auditor reads, not as a list of documents to produce.

Most of this evidence exists if the platform from the [shared platforms lesson](/learn/llmops/platform-strategy/shared-platforms) exists: the gateway writes the per-inference log, the registry records who moved which label when, the eval service attaches the paired result to the version. What remains for people to write is the tier record, the rollout plan, and the waiver.

## Why teams route around a committee

A single review board for every change looks safe and is the design most likely to fail an audit, because it is the design teams avoid.

<Fragment slot="prompt">
<p>Scenario inputs: 40 model changes a week. A five-person committee spends 15 minutes on each change. The tiered alternative: 28 changes are tier 1 and need no human approver beyond the author; 10 are tier 2 and get one reviewer for 15 minutes; 2 are tier 3 and go to the same five people for 30 minutes each. Enter the committee figure.</p>
</Fragment>
<p>Committee: 40 × 0.25 h × 5 people = <b>50 person-hours</b> a week. Tiered: 10 × 0.25 × 1 = 2.5, plus 2 × 0.5 × 5 = 5, so 7.5 person-hours, and the tier 3 changes get twice the time each. The committee also adds its meeting cadence to every change's lead time; a weekly meeting means a change waits up to a week however small it is.</p>
</Predict>

The committee spends most of its hours on tier 1 changes where its review adds nothing, and gives tier 3 the same fifteen minutes. Teams respond predictably: they batch changes to reduce meetings, which makes each change larger and harder to roll back, or they move the change out of the committee's view (a prompt in code instead of the registry, a direct vendor key). Both make the audited process less true.

## Waivers

A gate that cannot be waived will be bypassed; a gate that can be waived by anyone is not a gate. The waiver names the failed check, the risk accepted, the monitoring that watches it, the rollback trigger, the signer at the level the table names, and an expiry date. Waivers go in a register that is reviewed monthly, and an expired waiver either becomes a fix or is re-signed by the same level. The register is the most useful governance artefact for leadership: it shows where the process is hurting and where risk is being carried.

<p>Tier the features this week, lock the production label on tier 3 features (claims triage first) so only named approvers can move it, and keep an incident path: an on-call approver who can move it back to the last approved version at any hour, with the move logged and reviewed the next day. Tier 1 and 2 stay as they are for six weeks, with label moves logged. The auditor sees the control where the risk is; product keeps fast rollback; what I give up is control over tier 2 for six weeks, which I write down as a known gap.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The conflict is audit scope against incident speed under a six-week deadline. Two defensible designs. <b>Lock tier 3 only</b>, with an incident rollback right: small, done in a week, and the evidence is the registry's own audit log. Its cost is that tier 2 has no second approver yet, which the auditor may raise. <b>A two-person rule on every label move</b>: covers everything, and every incident fix waits for a second person at night. Its failure mode is that teams move prompts into code to avoid it.</p>
<p>I would take the first and distinguish rollback from roll-forward: moving the label back to the last approved version is pre-approved, because that version already passed its gate; moving it to a new version during an incident needs the risk owner. The failure mode of my design is an incident where the last approved version is the one that is broken (a vendor change affects both), so the runbook includes a kill switch that routes claims to manual triage, which is a product decision signed now, not at 2 a.m.</p>

## When this is wrong

The tier scheme assumes outputs have one consequence. A shared component (a summariser used by both an internal tool and a customer feature) takes the highest tier of any consumer, or the tiers leak.

Agents strain the rights table. The CSA note calls the lack of a clear responsible party for autonomous actions an accountability gap, and a table that assigns the label move does not assign the action the agent took at runtime. For tier 3 agents the owner of each tool's effects needs its own row.

The NIST mapping is voluntary and NIST is revising the framework; sector regulation, where it applies, sets the floor and may require approvals this table makes optional. And the evidence list comes from practitioner reports of what auditors ask for, which varies by auditor; confirm it with yours.

Small teams cannot staff three levels of approver. Two levels (author, and one named owner for anything customer-facing) with a logged label history is honest at that size; a table with roles nobody fills is worse than no table.
