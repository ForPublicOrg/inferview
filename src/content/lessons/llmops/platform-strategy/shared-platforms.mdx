## What a platform team is for

When a dozen teams call models their own way, the company holds a dozen sets of vendor keys, a dozen retry policies, a dozen log formats and no single answer to "what did we spend on model calls last month, by feature". The shared platform exists to make four things true once: every call is attributable to a team and a feature, every call is traceable to a model version and a prompt version, spend and rate limits are enforced per team, and a team can compare a candidate configuration against production on its own eval set without writing the runner.

It is an internal infrastructure platform in the usual sense, with one difference that shapes everything below: the thing it serves changes behaviour without a code change. A prompt label move or a vendor's model update alters what a feature does, so the platform sits on the release path in a way a database platform does not. That is why it can turn into a queue.

## The contract

The useful charter states what the platform guarantees and what it does not own. Two halves:

**The platform guarantees:**

- **A gateway** with one interface across approved vendors. Huyen lists the gateway's jobs as a unified interface, access control, cost management and fallback when a vendor rate-limits or fails, with load balancing, logging and analytics alongside. A team gets a key scoped to its feature, with a budget and rate limits. In LiteLLM's proxy, for example, that is a virtual key carrying `models`, `max_budget`, `budget_duration`, `tpm_limit`, `rpm_limit` and a `team_id`, with spend readable per key and per team; it needs a PostgreSQL database, which the platform runs.
- **A trace per call** with the fields every team needs and no team should design: team, feature, model and version, prompt name and version, token counts, latency, and the outcome event when the product reports one.
- **A prompt registry** where a release is a label move. In Langfuse's model, versions are immutable, `production` is the label applications fetch, and rollback is moving the label back. Langfuse also notes that caching means the first traces after a move can still use the old version, so the platform documents its propagation time.
- **An eval service** that runs a team's suite paired against production and candidate, and reports per-slice differences with intervals.
- **A model catalogue** with each model's status and retirement date. OpenAI's notice that new fine-tuning jobs end on 6 January 2027 is the kind of change the catalogue carries, with the list of features affected.

**Product teams own:** their prompts, their eval cases and graders, their labels, their outcome metrics, the decision to ship, and their incidents. The platform does not write a team's rubric and does not approve a team's prompt.

That second list is what keeps the platform out of the release queue. A platform that owns "prompt quality" will be asked to review every prompt.

## Paved path or mandate

Every primitive is either required or offered. The choice per primitive:

**Mandate** where the risk is shared across teams. Vendor keys (a leaked key is everyone's incident), data egress to a vendor (one team's mistake is the company's breach notice), and traces for any feature in an audited risk tier. The cost is real: a mandate makes the platform's availability every feature's availability, so the gateway needs its own on-call, an error budget, and a documented bypass for when it is down.

**Paved path** everywhere else. The SDK wrapper, eval templates, the registry for internal tools, the router. Teams adopt it because it is faster than the alternative. The cost is that some teams will not, and the platform has to accept a long tail it cannot see into.

A defensible middle: mandate the gateway and traces for anything customer-facing; pave everything else; allow an exception for a team that produces the same evidence through its own stack, written down with an owner and a review date. Without the exception, teams that have a real reason to go around (a vendor the gateway does not support yet, a latency budget the extra hop breaks) go around anyway and stop reporting it.

## How the platform becomes a queue

The common failure is a platform that inserts itself as a reviewer. A request to add a vendor, raise a quota, change a fallback, or move a label becomes a ticket, and tickets queue.

<p>Scenario inputs: twelve teams each file 3 changes a week that need platform review (36 a week). The platform team can review 24 a week. Changes are reviewed in arrival order, and the backlog starts at zero.</p>
</Fragment>
<p>The backlog grows by 36 − 24 = 12 a week, so after eight weeks it holds 12 × 8 = 96 changes. A change filed then waits behind 96 at 24 a week: 96 / 24 = <b>4 weeks</b>, and the wait keeps growing by half a week every week. No staffing increase short of 36 reviews a week stops the growth, which is why the fix is removing the platform from the path, not hiring for it.</p>
</Predict>

Removing the platform from the path means turning each review into a policy that runs without a person. Quota increases up to a ceiling are self-serve and logged. A new model version from an approved vendor is added to the catalogue once, and teams opt in with their own eval run as the evidence. A label move is the product team's decision, gated by the eval service's result and the team's own approver, not by the platform. The platform reviews only what changes shared risk: a new vendor, a new data class, a change to the gateway itself.

## The eval service

Eight teams with notebooks is the starting point one question bank describes. The notebooks differ in sampling, in how they pair runs, in whether they report an interval, and in whether a judge's agreement with human labels was ever measured. The service standardises the runner, not the content:

- A team registers a suite: cases, graders, and the slices it reports.
- The service runs production and candidate on the same cases with the same seeds where the vendor supports them, and reports the paired difference per slice with an interval.
- Judges carry their measured agreement with a labelled set and the date it was measured; a judge with no measurement is reported as unvalidated.
- Results attach to the candidate's registry version, so a label move can require a passing run.

What it does not do: decide what a team should measure. A service that ships a default "helpfulness" judge for everyone produces a number teams quote and nobody trusts.

## The platform's own metric

The question "if you could own only one metric, what would it be?" is reported as the top question on one interview-prep bank's AI list in 2026. Three candidates are defensible, each with its failure:

- **Coverage:** the share of model traffic (or of audited-tier traffic) that goes through the gateway with complete trace fields. It measures whether the platform is doing its job for risk and audit. It is gamed by mandate: coverage reaches 100 percent the week the mandate lands, while teams quietly slow down.
- **Lead time:** median time from a team deciding to ship a model change to the change running in production with its evidence attached. It measures whether the platform helps or hinders. It is gamed by weakening the evidence, so it needs the coverage number beside it as a floor.
- **Product outcome:** the sum of features' outcome metrics. It is what leadership wants and the platform cannot move directly; a platform judged on it is judged on other teams' product choices.

Lead time, with coverage of audited-tier traffic as a constraint that must not fall, is the pair I would defend. The failure mode of that choice: lead time measured from "decided to ship" misses the teams that never start a change because the path looks slow, so the platform also counts features shipping outside the gateway each quarter.

## Writing the charter

A charter that fits on one page has seven parts: the problem in one sentence; the customers (named teams); the guarantees, each with a number the team will be held to (gateway availability, added latency at the median and tail, propagation time for a label move); the non-goals (prompt quality, rubric design, launch approval); decision rights (what the platform decides alone, what it decides with security, what product teams decide); the metric and its floor; and a review date. The non-goals are the part that keeps the team out of the queue, and they are the part leadership will try to remove.

</Callout>

<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Measure the hop before deciding: if the gateway's added latency at the tail is within what search can absorb, the conversation ends there. If it is not, grant search a written, time-boxed exception: a direct call with a key the platform issues and rotates, traces emitted by search's client in the platform's format, and a date by which the gateway's latency is fixed or the exception is renewed. Security gets key control and traces; search gets its launch; the platform owns the latency fix.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The two defensible answers are hold the mandate and fix the hop in five weeks, or grant the exception. Holding the mandate keeps one path and costs search's launch if the fix slips, which puts the platform's schedule risk on another team's quarter. The exception keeps the launch and costs a second path the platform must monitor; its failure mode is that the exception becomes permanent and the next team cites it.</p>
<p>The deciding measurement is the gateway's added latency at the percentile search budgets for, under search's traffic shape, compared with search's budget. If the hop costs more than search can absorb, look at where: connection reuse, a synchronous logging write, or a cross-region placement are fixable in weeks; an extra region hop by design is not. The written exception names the owner, the expiry and the evidence that replaces the gateway (key issued by the platform, client-side traces in the standard schema), and security signs it.</p>

## When this is wrong

A company with two or three model features gets most of the value from a shared client library and a log schema; a platform team there is overhead. The break-even in the [strategy playbook](/learn/llmops/platform-strategy/staff-strategy) is the check.

Teams that self-host models have a different platform: serving, batching and GPU scheduling dominate, and the gateway is a small part of it. This lesson assumes hosted APIs as the main path.

A gateway in front of every call adds a dependency every feature shares. Huyen notes that one retry doubles the API calls; a gateway that retries on top of a client that also retries multiplies them. The platform owns the retry and fallback policy end to end, or neither layer should retry.

The metric section assumes the platform has the authority its charter claims. Where leadership will not back the non-goals, the platform becomes the review queue regardless of its charter, and the honest move is to say so before taking the role.
