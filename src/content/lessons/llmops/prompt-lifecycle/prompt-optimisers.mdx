## An optimiser is a search loop with your metric as the objective

Strip the names off and every automatic prompt optimiser has the same four parts: a parameter space (instruction text, few-shot demonstrations, or both, per module of the program), a proposer that generates candidates in that space, a metric that scores a candidate's outputs, and a selection rule over a set of scored examples. The output is a new prompt version. Nothing in the loop knows what the task is for; it knows what the metric rewards on the examples it was given.

That makes it hyperparameter search with two differences. The parameters are text, so the proposer is itself a model and the space has no gradient. And the objective is usually a proxy (exact match, a judge's verdict, a rubric score) for the thing users care about, so the search will find the proxy's weak points as reliably as it finds real improvements. An exact-match metric that ignores extra text will select prompts that hedge; a judge that prefers longer answers will select prompts that pad. The first requirement for running an optimiser is a metric you have already validated against human labels, for the same reason you would not run hyperparameter search against a buggy loss.

## What each optimiser changes and what signal it uses

The published optimisers differ in three choices: which parameters they search, what feedback the proposer sees, and how candidates are selected.

| Optimiser | Searches | Proposer sees | Selection |
|---|---|---|---|
| APE (2022) | one instruction | input and output demonstrations | best execution accuracy on a training subset |
| OPRO (2023) | one instruction | past candidates with their scores, in the prompt | best score; history grows each step |
| DSPy BootstrapFewShot | demonstrations per module | nothing; a teacher program runs on training examples | keep traces that pass the metric, up to a cap |
| MIPROv2 (2024) | instructions and demonstrations per module | the program's structure and the data | a surrogate model fitted to mini-batch scores proposes the next trial |
| TextGrad (2024) | any text variable in a graph | natural-language critiques, propagated backwards | apply the critique as an update |
| GEPA (2025) | instructions per module | full trajectories plus metric feedback text | a Pareto frontier of candidates that each win on some example |

APE generates candidate instructions with one model, scores each by how well a model following it performs, and keeps the best; the paper reports results better than or comparable to human-written instructions on 19 of 24 NLP tasks. OPRO puts the trajectory of the search into the meta-prompt: previous instructions with their scores, so the proposer can see which direction scored higher. The paper reports its best prompts beating human-designed ones by up to 8% on GSM8K and up to 50% on Big-Bench Hard.

BootstrapFewShot is the simplest to reason about, and its source is short. A teacher (by default the program itself) runs on training examples; when the metric passes, the trace of every predictor step becomes a demonstration; the loop stops at `max_bootstrapped_demos` (default 4); remaining slots up to `max_labeled_demos` (default 16) are filled with labelled examples that did not bootstrap. The metric is the filter on what the model will be shown as a correct example, so a lenient metric puts wrong demonstrations into the prompt.

MIPROv2 searches instructions and demonstrations jointly. Proposals are drafted with knowledge of the program's structure and the data, each trial is scored on a mini-batch rather than the full set, and a surrogate model fitted to those noisy scores chooses the next combination to try. The paper reports it beating baseline optimisers on five of seven multi-stage programs with Llama-3-8B, by up to 13% accuracy. TextGrad treats a model's critique of an output as a gradient and passes it back through a graph of text variables; the paper reports GPT-4o's zero-shot accuracy on Google-Proof QA moving from 51% to 55%. GEPA, the subject of the next lesson, reflects on full trajectories in natural language and keeps every candidate that is best on at least one example.

Every one of these figures is the authors' own result on their benchmarks and models.

## Selection eats the dev set

The selection step is where most optimiser results go wrong, and it is independent of which optimiser you use. Score 20 candidates on the same 100 examples and keep the best: even if all 20 are equally good, the winner's score is the maximum of 20 noisy draws, and that maximum sits above the true accuracy. The held-out set takes the difference back.

With its defaults (20 candidates, 100 examples, true accuracy 60%), the winner scores 69.0% on dev and 60% everywhere else: 9 points of apparent gain from selection alone. A candidate that is genuinely 3 points better outscores all 19 others only 12% of the time. Raise the dev set to 300 and the inflation falls to 5.2 points and the better candidate wins 23% of the time; at 1,000 examples, 2.9 points and 53%. Real candidates are correlated (they fail on many of the same examples), so the inflation is smaller than the independent case, but its sign does not change.

This is what DSPy's sizing guidance is about. Its docs suggest starting with BootstrapFewShot at about 10 examples, BootstrapFewShotWithRandomSearch at 50 or more, and MIPROv2 for runs of 40 trials or more with 200 examples or more "to prevent overfitting". The MIPROv2 source sets its automatic modes to 6 candidates on a 100-example validation set (light), 12 on 300 (medium) and 18 on 1,000 (heavy). If you call it without a validation set, it takes the last 80% of your trainset (capped at 1,000) as validation and bootstraps from the first 20%: hand it 50 examples and it bootstraps demonstrations from 10 and selects on 40.

Neither number is the one that decides whether to ship. The dev set is part of the optimiser's training data once it has been used for selection. A third set, never scored during the search, is the only unbiased estimate of the winner, and it has to be large enough to resolve the gain you care about.

## Published numbers, with their conditions

DSPy's docs show ReAct moving from 24% to 51% after MIPROv2 in light mode, on 500 question-answer pairs sampled from HotPotQA, in what they call an informal run on DSPy 2.5.29 with `gpt-4o-mini`. The same page gives a RAG example from 53% to 61% and a BootstrapFinetune classification example from 66% to 87%, also informal runs. It puts the cost of a typical simple optimisation run on the order of $2 and around ten minutes. These are illustrations from the library's authors, not benchmarks, and the run cost scales with the number of trials, the validation set size and the length of each program call.

OpenAI's prompt optimizer guide states the conditions in its own words: the result depends on the quality of the graders, optimised prompts should be evaluated and reviewed before production, and the optimised prompt can do worse than the original on specific inputs. It needs only three rows with a grader result or annotation each, which is enough to run it and nowhere near enough to select on. The dataset-backed optimizer is being retired with OpenAI's Evals platform: read-only for existing users on 31 October 2026 and shut down on 30 November 2026, per the guide as checked on 2026-10-28.

## The two pieces you write yourself

Bootstrapping and the held-out gate are short. The gate compares the baseline and the winner on the same held-out cases and counts only the cases that flipped, which is the paired test from the evals track.

```python
from math import comb

def bootstrap_demos(teacher, trainset, metric, max_demos=4):
    """BootstrapFewShot's core: keep a teacher trace only if it passes the metric."""
    demos = []
    for ex in trainset:
        if len(demos) >= max_demos:
            break
        pred = teacher(ex["q"], demos=[])
        if metric(ex, pred):
            demos.append({"q": ex["q"], "a": pred})
    return demos

def heldout_gate(base_ok, cand_ok, alpha=0.05):
    """Paired check on a held-out set the optimiser never scored."""
    fixed = sum(1 for b, c in zip(base_ok, cand_ok) if c and not b)
    broke = sum(1 for b, c in zip(base_ok, cand_ok) if b and not c)
    m = fixed + broke
    # One-sided exact sign test: P(at least `fixed` wins if each flip were a coin).
    p = sum(comb(m, i) for i in range(fixed, m + 1)) / 2 ** m if m else 1.0
    return {"fixed": fixed, "broke": broke, "p": round(p, 4), "ship": p < alpha}

# A deterministic mock: the teacher is right on even numbers only.
teacher = lambda q, demos: q * 2 if q % 2 == 0 else q * 2 + 1
metric = lambda ex, pred: pred == ex["q"] * 2
train = [{"q": q} for q in range(10)]
print(bootstrap_demos(teacher, train, metric))
# [{'q': 0, 'a': 0}, {'q': 2, 'a': 4}, {'q': 4, 'a': 8}, {'q': 6, 'a': 12}]

base = [True] * 120 + [False] * 80
cand = [True] * 112 + [False] * 8 + [True] * 22 + [False] * 58
print(sum(base), sum(cand), heldout_gate(base, cand))
# 120 134 {'fixed': 22, 'broke': 8, 'p': 0.0081, 'ship': True}
```

The candidate passes the gate (60% to 67% on 200 held-out cases, 22 fixed against 8 broken), and it still broke 8 cases that used to pass. Those 8 are OpenAI's warning in concrete form, and they are read before the version ships: if they cluster in one customer segment or one input type, the average gain is not the decision.

The winner then enters the registry as a new version, with its dev score, held-out score, optimiser name, run configuration and the IDs of the dev and held-out sets recorded on the version. It goes through the same promotion gate as a hand-written edit.

## When this is wrong

The widget's arithmetic treats candidates as independent and examples as exchangeable. Candidates from one optimiser share most of their text and fail on many of the same examples, so the inflation is smaller than shown; examples drawn from a few long documents or a few users are correlated, so the effective sample size is smaller than the count, and the half-width is wider than shown.

A held-out set is only unbiased once. After a team has looked at held-out results across several optimiser runs and kept the best, the held-out set has become a second dev set. Rotate it, or keep a final set that is opened only at release.

Optimised prompts are fitted to one model. A version that wins on one model is a candidate on the next, not a result, which is why the optimiser's output carries the model ID it was tuned on in the registry.

Some tasks do not have a metric worth trusting. Open-ended writing, multi-turn support and anything whose quality shows up only in downstream outcomes can be scored by a judge, but the optimiser will optimise the judge. Where the judge's agreement with people is weak, hand-written prompts with error analysis cost less than validating an optimiser's output.
