A synthetic-data programme makes three decisions: whether each round's generated data replaces the data before it or is added to it, what filters the generator's output, and what the model is evaluated on. The first decides whether the programme degrades over rounds. The second decides the quality of what is trained on, because generation is cheap and the filter is where the cost and the judgement go. The third decides whether anyone can tell.

## Replace or accumulate

"Model collapse" is used loosely. Gerstgrasser et al. (April 2024) use it for one thing: test error that keeps growing as each model is trained on data from the one before. They separate two ways of running the loop.

**Replace.** Round n trains only on samples from round n−1's model. The real data is used once, in round 1.

**Accumulate.** Round n trains on the real data plus the synthetic data of every earlier round. The pool grows; nothing is thrown away.

For a sequence of linear regression models, they show that replacing gives a test error proportional to the number of rounds n, while accumulating gives the same constant times 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + … + 1/n², a sum that never exceeds π²/6. The intuition is in the weights: round k's estimation error enters every later round, but under accumulation it is one of k equally sized slices of the pool, so later errors are diluted instead of inherited whole.

The experiments on language models are small but direct. A 9M-parameter GPT-2 trained for one epoch per round on TinyStories, a 470M-token corpus of short stories, had test cross-entropy 1.82 after round 1. Replacing, it rose to 2.39 by round 4 and 2.91 by round 10. Accumulating, it was 1.74 at round 4, slightly better than where it started. Llama 2 models at 12M, 42M and 126M parameters showed the same pattern: the 126M model went from 1.71 to 2.23 by round 4 when replacing, and to 1.59 when accumulating. The authors report similar results for diffusion models generating molecule conformations and for VAEs on images, with one difference: the VAEs still degraded slightly under accumulation.

Two results in the same table matter for engineering choices.

**Low temperature makes replacement much worse.** Sampling the synthetic data at temperature 0.3 instead of 1.0, the GPT-2 model reached 5.82 by round 4 and 9.85 by round 10 when replacing. Low temperature drops the tails of the distribution, and each round trains on a narrower slice of the last. A team that lowers the generator's temperature "for consistency" is choosing that regime.

**Size is not the whole explanation.** Accumulation trains on more data each round, so the paper also ran a replace variant whose synthetic set grows to match. It still degraded, to 2.18 at round 4, though more slowly than plain replacement. Keeping the real data is what bounds the error, not only having more rows.

## What the bound assumes

The result is easy to over-read in both directions. It does not say synthetic data is harmless, and it does not say it is doomed.

The theory is ridgeless linear regression with isotropic features. The experiments sample stochastically; the authors leave deterministic generation untested. Accumulation grows the training set every round, so its compute is not matched with the replacement runs. And the VAE result shows that accumulating is not a guarantee for every model family.

For a fine-tuning programme the operational reading is narrower: keep every real example in every round's set; add synthetic data rather than regenerating the set from the newest model; and measure on real data, because the bound is about test error on the real distribution and is invisible on a synthetic split.

## Generation is cheap; the filter decides

Nemotron-4 340B (NVIDIA, June 2024) is the large-scale case. Over 98% of the data used in its alignment was synthetic. The human data was about 20,000 examples: roughly 10,000 for supervised fine-tuning and 10,000 HelpSteer2 preference pairs used to train a reward model. That reward model, Nemotron-4-340B-Reward, predicts five attributes (helpfulness, correctness, coherence, complexity, verbosity) and scored 92.0 on RewardBench in the report. The pipeline then used it as the filter:

- **Prompts** were generated by Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct from seed lists, including 3,000 topics, 12,000 Python keywords and 17,000 math keywords, so coverage was designed rather than sampled.
- **Dialogues** were generated and those below a reward threshold were dropped.
- **Preference pairs** were judged by ground truth or a verifier where one existed (GSM8K, MATH, instruction-following checks), and otherwise by a judge. When an LLM was the judge, each pair was scored in both orders and kept only if the verdicts agreed. On the Chat-Hard subset, the reward model as judge was right 0.87 of the time against 0.54 for the LLM judge, and the team switched to the reward model in later rounds.
- **Rounds** were iterative: the first generator was Mixtral, the second an intermediate 340B model trained on the first round's data.

Read as a design, the human data did not disappear; it moved upstream into the filter. The generator was cheap and replaceable. The filter was trained on human preferences, was a different model from the generator in the first round, and used ground truth wherever it existed.

AlpaGasus (Chen et al., 2023) shows the same principle on a small scale and the control that makes it credible. Alpaca's 52,002 examples were themselves generated by a model; AlpaGasus had ChatGPT score each one 0 to 5 for accuracy and kept the 9,229 at 4.5 or above. The 7B model trained on the filtered set scored 38.78 on MMLU against 36.93 for a random subset of the same size. Without that size-matched baseline, a filter's effect cannot be separated from the effect of training on less data.

## Designing the loop

A synthetic-data loop for a fine-tune, with the decisions in order:

1. **Seeds from real data**, scrubbed through the same path as any production data ([curation pipeline](/learn/llmops/data-curation/curation-pipeline)), stratified by intent so rare intents get seeds.
2. **Generation** at a temperature chosen against a measured diversity target, such as distinct intents or distinct n-grams per thousand examples, not chosen low for tidiness.
3. **Verification** by ground truth where it exists: code that runs, prices checked against the pricing table, answers checked against the source document.
4. **A judge or reward model that is not the generator**, scored in both orders for pairwise judgements, with its threshold chosen on a sample labelled by a person and its pass rate on known-wrong answers measured.
5. **Near-duplicate removal** within the synthetic set and against every real set, because twenty variants of one seed at low temperature are paraphrases.
6. **The contamination check** against every eval set and any public benchmark the team reports.
7. **Accumulate**: the round's survivors are added to the real data and the earlier rounds, with a provenance tag on every row.
8. **Evaluate on real data from the latest period**, never on a held-out split of synthetic data.

```python
def run_round(real, pool, generate, verify, judge, threshold, eval_sets, near_dup):
    seeds = stratified_sample(real, by="intent")
    candidates = [c for s in seeds for c in generate(s)]
    kept = [c for c in candidates if verify(c) is not False and judge(c) >= threshold]
    kept = dedup_near(kept, against=real + pool)
    leaks = [c for c in kept if any(near_dup(c, e) for es in eval_sets for e in es)]
    if leaks:
        raise ContaminationError(len(leaks))
    return pool + [dict(c, provenance="synthetic", round=current_round()) for c in kept]
```

The training set for the round is `real + pool`; the pool only grows, and a cap, when cost forces one, removes the oldest synthetic rounds first and never the real data.

## The contamination check

A generator that has read the internet may reproduce public benchmark items, and a generator seeded from production may reproduce the conversations that make up the eval set. Either way the eval stops measuring generalisation. The check is the same near-duplicate gate as in the curation pipeline: MinHash candidates across the synthetic set and every eval set, confirmed by a similarity threshold, with any hit failing the build. Lee et al. found 4.60% of C4's validation examples had a near-duplicate in its training split without any generator involved; a generator seeded from the eval's own distribution can only raise that.

Two decisions sit inside the check. Seeds must come from the training side of a user and time split, never from conversations that later fed the eval set. And a hit is handled by dropping the synthetic row, not the eval item, because the eval set is the fixed point the programme is measured against.

## When this is wrong

The collapse experiments train small models from scratch on a synthetic corpus of short stories. A fine-tune of a large pretrained model on a few thousand synthetic examples is a different regime: the pretrained weights act as a large fixed store of real data, which is closer to accumulation than to replacement even when the fine-tuning set is entirely synthetic. The rule to keep real data still holds; the size of the effect in fine-tuning is not in this evidence.

A filter independent of the generator is not independent of everything. A reward model trained on general human preferences carries those preferences, including a pull toward length and confident tone, into every round. Nemotron's reward model predicts verbosity as a separate attribute for this reason, and a domain programme needs its own check for what its filter rewards.

When ground truth exists, most of this lesson is unnecessary. Generated code that passes its tests, generated SQL that returns the right rows, or generated extractions that match the source span need no judge, and a programme built on verifiable tasks can use far more synthetic data with far less risk than one built on open-ended answers.
