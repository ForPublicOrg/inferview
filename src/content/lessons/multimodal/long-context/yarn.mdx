## The problem the paper solves

Position Interpolation made context extension cheap by dividing every RoPE frequency by the scale factor *s*. The YaRN paper's diagnosis is that this is the right operation for the slow pairs and the wrong one for the fast ones. Stretching every dimension equally removes high-frequency information: the pairs that tell the model whether a token is one or two positions back now rotate *s* times more slowly, and the weights were trained on the faster rotation. The paper reports that PI fine-tunes reached only about *s* = 8 before quality degraded.

The other known fix at the time, "NTK-aware" scaling, changed the base instead. It left the fastest pair alone and slowed the slowest by the full factor, but the paper found it pushed some dimensions slightly out of bound, gave worse fine-tuning results than PI, and needed a nominal factor larger than the extension it delivered.

YaRN asks what each pair needs, given how many full turns it made inside the trained window.

## The mechanism in engineering terms

**Count turns per pair.** For pair *d* with wavelength λ<sub>d</sub> = 2π · b<sup>2d/|D|</sup> and a trained window of *L* tokens, the paper defines *r*(*d*) = *L* / λ<sub>d</sub>, the number of full rotations that pair made in training.

**Ramp between two thresholds.** γ(*r*) is 0 when *r* &lt; α, 1 when *r* &gt; β, and (*r* − α) / (β − α) in between. The new frequency is (1 − γ) · θ / *s* + γ · θ. For the Llama family the paper recommends α = 1 and β = 32, which transformers uses as its defaults (`beta_slow` 1, `beta_fast` 32). A pair that turned more than 32 times in training is left exactly as it was: it has seen every angle many times and its job is local order. A pair that never completed a turn is interpolated fully, like PI. The pairs between are blended. This is the "NTK-by-parts" part.

**Sharpen attention.** Interpolation makes attention logits flatter as the window grows, so the paper scales them by a temperature: √(1/*t*) = 0.1 ln *s* + 1, applied to both queries and keys, which in practice means scaling the rotary cos and sin tables. Transformers computes this `attention_factor` from the factor when a config does not set it. There is no runtime cost: the tables are computed once.

**Optionally, scale dynamically.** Dynamic scaling recomputes *s* on every forward pass as max(1, *l*′ / *L*), where *l*′ is the current sequence length, so a short sequence runs unscaled and quality degrades gradually past *L* instead of all at once. The paper adds the operational condition: with a key-value cache, cache keys and values before applying RoPE, because the embedding changes whenever *s* does.

## The numbers the paper reports

The headline in the abstract: YaRN reaches its extensions with 10 times fewer tokens and 2.5 times fewer training steps than previous methods. The appendix's concrete comparison is 400 fine-tuning steps against PI's 1,000 for similar perplexity.

The training runs: Llama 2 7B and 13B at *s* = 16 for 400 steps at batch size 64, on PG19 split into 64k-token segments; *s* = 32 continued from that checkpoint for 200 more steps, 600 in total. The 128k models were therefore trained on 64k sequences and evaluated past them. That is 400 × 64 × 65,536 ≈ 1.7 billion training tokens for the factor-16 run, a useful order of magnitude when someone asks what a context extension costs.

For the Llama 2 shape (base 10,000, head dimension 128, *L* = 4,096), a pair is untouched when *r* &gt; 32, that is when λ &lt; 128 tokens: 2π · 10,000<sup>i/64</sup> &lt; 128 gives *i* &lt; 64 · ln(128 / 2π) / ln 10,000 ≈ 20.9. Pairs 0 to 20, 21 of 64, keep their frequency exactly; PI would have slowed all of them.

## What changed in practice

YaRN became the default name for "extend a RoPE model" in model cards and configs. Two current examples show how differently it is used.

**gpt-oss (August 2025)** uses YaRN only where it matters. Its attention alternates banded-window layers, with a band of 128 tokens, and fully dense layers, and the model card says YaRN extends the dense layers to 131,072 tokens. A banded layer never sees a relative distance above its band, so it has nothing to stretch; the dense layers carry long-range position and get the extension.

**Qwen3 (2025)** documents YaRN as something the operator adds: native 32,768, a block with factor 4 for 131,072, and a warning that every notable open-source framework implements it statically, so the factor applies to every input and may hurt short ones. The card's advice is to add the block only when long inputs are needed and to lower the factor to 2 if typical length is about 65,536. Its hosted endpoint, the card says, supports dynamic YaRN by default.

The second example is the one that reaches production teams. Static YaRN is a configuration change with a measurable quality cost on short traffic, which is why the debug problem [mml-001](/problems/mml-001) is about a deployment that applied it to everything.

## Why not just raise the base

This is the question the paper answers by construction. Raising the base is the NTK-aware method: for *s* = 4 on the Llama 2 shape, b′ = 10,000 × 4<sup>128/126</sup> ≈ 40,890. It keeps pair 0, slows the slowest pair by exactly 4, and slows the pairs between by less than 4, so some slow pairs still reach angles they never saw (the module's widget counts 17 of the 18 slow pairs at that shape). It cannot leave a band of fast pairs exactly untouched, and it has no temperature. YaRN keeps what NTK-aware got right, treating fast and slow pairs differently, and replaces the smooth base change with an explicit decision per pair based on how many turns it made in training.

A base raised before pretraining is a different thing: Llama 3's base of 500,000 was learned with. The question is only about changing the base of weights trained at a smaller one.

## When this is wrong

The paper's evidence is mostly perplexity and benchmark scores for Llama 2 models from 2023. Perplexity on long documents is necessary and not sufficient for long-context use. Whether YaRN preserves a given model's ability to reason over a long input is a question for the evaluation lesson, answered per model.

The α and β values are tuned for the Llama family. A model with a different base, head dimension or training length may need other thresholds; using the defaults is a choice, not a law.

The temperature formula is empirical. It was fitted for Llama models in the paper, and other model families may need a different curve or none.

And "YaRN" in a config means whatever the engine implements: static or dynamic, with or without the temperature, with defaults that may differ between versions. The paper describes one method; deployments describe many.
