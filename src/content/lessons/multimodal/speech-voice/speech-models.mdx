A cascaded voice agent runs four models per turn: a recognizer that turns audio into text, a turn detector that decides the user has finished, an LLM, and a synthesizer. A speech-to-speech model folds all four into one. The engineering decision is which component to buy, which to run yourself, and which one you are tuning when the turn feels slow. The answer to the last question is almost never the recognizer.

## The latency of a turn, by component

The Pipecat maintainers publish a round trip for a typical cascaded stack, measured from the user's microphone to the agent's audio coming out of the speaker. It totals 1,293 ms. Grouping its rows by component:

| Component | Rows in the table | ms | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| LLM | time to first byte | 650 | 50% |
| Recognizer and turn detector | transcription and endpointing | 300 | 23% |
| Transport | mic, codec, network, jitter buffer, speaker, both ways | 203 | 16% |
| Synthesizer | time to first byte | 120 | 9% |
| Glue | sentence aggregation | 20 | 2% |

The shares are 650/1,293, 300/1,293 and so on, rounded. Two components own three quarters of the turn, and neither of them is the one that turns audio into words. The recognizer's own work is mostly done before the user stops talking, because streaming recognizers transcribe as audio arrives. What is left after the last word is the decision that the user has finished, and that decision is a separate model with its own failure modes.

## Recognition

Whisper set the expectations most teams still carry: the paper reports training on 680,000 hours of multilingual and multitask supervision and zero-shot results often competitive with prior fully supervised systems, without fine-tuning. Two consequences matter in a voice agent. First, accuracy on clean, general speech is a solved purchase; spend evaluation effort on your domain's proper nouns, account numbers and accents, where a recognizer that is fine on a benchmark still mangles the one token your tool call needs. Second, a voice agent needs interim results while the user is still speaking, not a transcript of a finished recording. Streaming recognizers emit interim transcripts that can change and a final transcript that does not. The interim stream is what makes speculative LLM starts possible, and its stability is a number you measure per recognizer, not a property you assume.

Recognizer pricing is per audio minute and small next to the rest of a voice bill. OpenAI's transcription models, for example, are listed per minute, and `gpt-realtime-whisper` and `gpt-live-transcribe` can run without turn detection, with the application committing each turn itself. That mode is the escape hatch when your application knows the turn ended (a push-to-talk button, a DTMF key) and should not wait for any detector.

## Turn detection is its own model

Turn detection answers one question: has this person finished their turn, or are they pausing? There are three generations of answer.

**Silence threshold (plain VAD).** A voice activity detector marks speech and silence frames, and the turn ends after a fixed run of silence. Pipecat's defaults are `VAD_STOP_SECS = 0.8` and `VAD_START_SECS = 0.2`. OpenAI's `server_vad` exposes the same knobs as `threshold`, `prefix_padding_ms` and `silence_duration_ms`; the docs show 0.5, 300 and 500 as example values. ElevenLabs puts the cost plainly: waiting 700 ms of silence adds 700 ms to every turn. Shortening the window trades latency for cut-offs, because people pause mid-sentence to recall an account number, and a silence detector cannot tell that pause from the end of a turn.

**Semantic turn detection.** A model judges whether the words or audio so far form a complete turn. OpenAI's `semantic_vad` takes an `eagerness` of `low`, `medium`, `high` or `auto` (which equals `medium`); `low` lets the user take their time, `high` ends turns as soon as it can. The knob moves the same trade-off as the silence window, but along a better curve, because "my account number is" no longer looks finished.

**Audio-native turn models.** Pipecat's Smart Turn v3.2 is about 8M parameters (8 MB quantized for CPU), takes up to 8 seconds of 16 kHz mono audio, covers 23 languages, and runs only after a VAD has detected silence, so it does not run continuously. The README reports inference in as little as 10 ms on some CPUs and under 100 ms on most cloud instances. Because it hears audio, it can use intonation that a transcript-based detector never sees. The primer describes the combined approach: a VAD with a short 200 ms trigger, then the turn model, for a decision about 250 ms after the user pauses.

In a speech-to-speech session the turn detector also starts the response: OpenAI documents `create_response` and `interrupt_response` as applying only to speech-to-speech conversations, while in a transcription session the detector only decides how audio is chunked. Turning detection off in a speech-to-speech session moves the decision, and its latency, into your client.

## The LLM sets the floor

The primer measured time to first token for several hosted models, median and P95 in ms: GPT-4.1 536 and 1,771, Gemini 2.5 Flash 597 and 1,137, Claude 4.5 Haiku 637 and 1,615, GPT-5.1 739 and 1,492, and a self-hosted Nemotron 3 Ultra 541 and 712. Its rule of thumb is that 600 ms or less is fast enough for most voice use. The medians cluster within about 200 ms of each other; the P95s span 712 to 1,771 ms. A model with a 1,771 ms P95 cannot meet a 1,500 ms turn target at the 95th percentile no matter how good the rest of the pipeline is, so for a voice agent the LLM's tail is a selection criterion before its quality is.

Tool calls make this worse: the primer lists several reasons a function call adds latency, including the second inference after the tool returns. A voice turn with one tool call pays the LLM's time to first token twice plus the tool, which is why the next lesson treats filler and watchdog timers as part of the design.

## Synthesis is judged by time to first byte

TTS quality comparisons are about voice; TTS engineering comparisons are about time to first audio and its tail. The primer's measurements, median and P95 in ms: Cartesia Sonic 3.5 195 and 240, Gradium 235 and 320, Deepgram Aura-2 310 and 600, ElevenLabs Turbo v2.5 330 and 670, Inworld TTS 1.5 Max 337 and 560. As with the LLM, the medians differ by less than the tails do. TTS only helps latency if it streams: it must accept text a clause at a time and return audio before the sentence is complete. The 20 ms "sentence aggregation" row in the table is the wait for that first clause.

## Speech-to-speech removes the boundaries

Moshi models the user's speech and its own as parallel streams, so there are no explicit speaker turns at all; overlap, interruptions and interjections are just audio on two channels. It predicts time-aligned text tokens before its audio tokens (the paper calls this Inner Monologue), which the abstract says improves the linguistic quality of the speech. The paper reports 160 ms theoretical and 200 ms practical latency. There is no endpointer to tune and no TTS to stream, so the levers in this lesson disappear, and so do the places you would have inspected text, logged a transcript or run a policy check. The playbook in this module weighs that trade.

## Choosing components

For a cascaded agent, the procedure that follows from the numbers above:

1. Pick the LLM on P95 time to first token under your prompt and tool definitions, measured from your region, then on quality. A median that fits and a tail that does not is a model that fails one turn in twenty.
2. Pick the turn detector next: semantic or audio-native for open conversation, manual commit wherever the interface already knows the turn ended. Evaluate it on your own recordings for cut-off rate and added delay together.
3. Pick a streaming TTS on P95 time to first audio, then voice.
4. Pick the recognizer last, on your domain's hard tokens.

<Callout kind="gotcha">ElevenLabs' latency post says a natural-feeling agent "typically targets" P50 time to first audio under 800 ms and P95 under 1.5 seconds. That is one vendor's target, measured from end of speech rather than from the microphone, and it is not a standard. The Pipecat primer's June 2026 update sets 1,500 ms voice-to-voice as the target and warns against spikes above it. State which target and which start point you are using before comparing numbers.</Callout>

## When this is wrong

The primer's table is one stack, measured with a macOS client and particular providers; its 300 ms for transcription and endpointing assumes a modern turn model, and your network legs depend on where your users and servers are. The per-model latency figures are the maintainers' measurements at one point in time, and hosted model tails change with provider load from week to week, so they are a reason to measure, not a ranking to cite. The component ordering above also assumes a cascaded design with text in the middle. If you adopt a speech-to-speech model, latency becomes a property of one vendor's model and serving stack, and your tuning work moves to turn-taking behaviour and cost. Finally, latency is not the whole of conversation quality: a fast agent that interrupts callers is worse than a slower one that does not, and only recordings of real calls show which you have.
