## What it is and who runs it

Every open-weight model ships a `config.json` that a serving engine reads to build the model: layer count, heads, which layers attend how far, which layers are recurrent. This teardown reads seven of them side by side, from OpenAI (gpt-oss), Google (Gemma 3), AI21 (Jamba), Moonshot AI (Kimi Linear), MiniMax (MiniMax-Text-01), Meta (Llama 4 Scout) and DeepSeek (DeepSeek-V3.2), with each lab's paper, model card or launch post for the parts the config does not say. Each design answers the same question, how to keep a long context without a KV cache that grows on every layer, and each answer changes what a serving engine has to implement.

Two of the configs (Gemma 3 and Llama 4) sit in gated repositories; the values here come from public mirrors of those files and agree with what the papers and posts state.

gpt-oss-120b. 36 layers alternate a banded window of 128 tokens with full attention, 8 KV heads of dimension 64, and each head has a learned bias in its softmax denominator. That bias is an attention sink: the card says it lets a head pay no attention to any token, and it costs one scalar per head, nothing per token. YaRN extends the dense layers to 131,072 tokens from a 4,096-token original.

Gemma 3 27B. Five local layers with a 1,024-token window for every global layer, 62 layers in all, so 10 global and 52 local, with 16 KV heads of dimension 128. The report says the ratio barely moved perplexity even at 7 to 1, and that the window could shrink substantially without hurting it. The global layers carry the long-range work and most of the cache.

Llama 4 Scout. 48 layers: every fourth layer (12 in all) is global attention with no positional embedding, and the other 36 use RoPE inside 8,192-token chunks. Meta's post adds inference-time temperature scaling of attention for length generalisation, and the config switches it on with a floor of 8,192 tokens and a scale of 0.1.

Jamba-v0.1. Blocks of eight layers with one attention layer and seven Mamba layers, MoE on every other layer, 32 layers in all: 4 attention layers with 8 KV heads of dimension 128, and 28 Mamba layers whose state is fixed per sequence. No explicit positional encoding, so order reaches the model only through the Mamba layers' recurrence.

Kimi Linear 48B. 27 layers: 20 Kimi Delta Attention layers (a gated delta-rule recurrence with 32 heads of 128 by 128 state) and 7 MLA layers with no positional encoding. The paper describes the ratio as 3 to 1; the config's last full-attention layer at position 27 makes it 20 to 7.

MiniMax-Text-01. 80 layers: one softmax attention layer after every seven lightning attention layers, so 10 softmax layers with 8 KV heads of dimension 128, and 70 lightning layers each keeping a 128 by 128 state per head across 64 heads.

DeepSeek-V3.2. All 61 layers cache MLA latents (512 + 64 values), as DeepSeek-V3 does. DeepSeek Sparse Attention adds a lightning indexer that scores every past token cheaply and picks the top 2,048 for the main attention to read. It cuts the attention compute per query from the full context to 2,048 entries; it does not shrink what is stored.

The spread at 128K is about tenfold, from Kimi Linear's 1.00 GiB to Gemma 3's 10.41 GiB, and it tracks one number: how many layers keep every token, times how wide each keeps it. Parameter count barely enters. MiniMax-Text-01, at 456B total, caches less per sequence than Gemma 3 27B, because 70 of its 80 layers are recurrent and its softmax layers use 8 KV heads where Gemma's global layers use 16.

## Context strategy

Position handling differs as much as the layer mix:

- gpt-oss applies RoPE everywhere and stretches the dense layers with YaRN (factor 32 from 4,096). The banded layers never see a distance beyond 128, so the extension only has to hold on half the layers.
- Gemma 3 gives global layers a RoPE base of 1,000,000 and local layers 10,000, and the config adds linear scaling with factor 8. Local layers never see long distances, so their positional setup can stay at short-context values.
- Llama 4 Scout uses RoPE only inside 8,192-token chunks and no position at all in the global layers, plus inference-time attention temperature scaling. The post presents the interleaving and the temperature scaling as what lets Scout generalise in length, and publishes no ablation.
- Jamba uses no explicit positions, and Kimi Linear removes them from its MLA layers; in both, the recurrent layers carry order.
- MiniMax-01 applies RoPE to half of each softmax head's dimension at base 10,000 (the config's rope_theta reads 10,000,000; the paper and the file disagree, and the file is what an engine loads).
- DeepSeek-V3.2 keeps MLA's decoupled 64-wide rotary key.

For a long-document product the consequence is that "supports 128K" or "supports 10M" is a statement about positional extrapolation on a few layers, not a measured ability to use every token. A needle-test score is not evidence of usable long context; use retrieval and reasoning tasks drawn from your own documents.

## Cost and latency

Memory is the table above. Compute follows a different ranking. Recurrent and windowed layers cost a constant amount per generated token; full-attention layers cost in proportion to the context. MiniMax's paper reports that at 1,024,000 tokens its 10 softmax layers take about 95% of inference latency and its 70 lightning layers under 12%. Kimi Linear reports up to 6 times the decoding throughput at 1M context against its full-attention baseline. Both are the vendors' measurements on their own stacks.

DeepSeek-V3.2 moves in the opposite direction from the hybrids: more bytes stored per token than DeepSeek-V3 (the indexer keys), less attention compute per query (2,048 entries instead of the whole context). The indexer still scans every past token, at O(L²) over a sequence; the paper's argument is that this scan is much cheaper than MLA attention over the same tokens.

gpt-oss-120b fits a single 80 GB GPU because of MXFP4 expert weights, per the model card. Jamba fits a single 80 GB GPU at 140K tokens, per its paper. Neither statement says anything about how many sequences fit alongside the weights; the widget's per-sequence numbers do.

## Failure modes

An engine that does not implement a layer type correctly either wastes memory or silently changes the model:

- Windowed layers allocated at full length. Charging gpt-oss's 18 banded layers for every token doubles its cache to 9.00 GiB at 128K; for Llama 4 Scout the 36 chunked layers become 18.00 GiB on top of the global 6.00 GiB. Tick the checkbox in the widget to see each case.
- Windowed or chunked layers computed as full attention. That is not a memory bug; it changes outputs, because the model was trained to see 128 or 8,192 tokens in those layers.
- Missing sinks or missing temperature scaling. Dropping gpt-oss's softmax bias or Llama 4's attention temperature tuning leaves a model that loads and runs, and degrades at long context.
- Recurrent layers without state snapshots. Prefix caching and speculative decoding need per-layer state copies at boundaries; an engine without them recomputes from the start.
- Precision assumptions. Recurrent state held in 32 bits doubles every state figure; KV in 8 bits halves the growing part. Read the engine's allocation, not the config.

## What is not public

The training data mix for all seven models, and most of the ablations behind each layer ratio. The published ablations are partial: Gemma 3 reports ratio and window sweeps on perplexity; Jamba reports a positional-encoding ablation; the gpt-oss card, the Llama 4 post and the MiniMax and Kimi papers state their layouts with less or no public evidence for why that ratio and not another. Llama 4 has a launch post and a config but no technical report, so iRoPE's length-generalisation claim has no published measurement behind it. For DeepSeek-V3.2, the paper defines the indexer key's dimension but the text read for this teardown does not describe how serving stores indexer keys per layer; the trace's 7,808 bytes per token is an assumption, not a published figure. MiniMax's softmax RoPE base differs between paper and config, and neither source explains why.

<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Group the work by cache behaviour, not by model. Per-layer allocation with a window cap comes first: it serves gpt-oss and Gemma 3 correctly, and with chunk semantics added it also serves Llama 4 Scout, three models from one allocator change. MLA latent caching with an absorbed decode kernel is second and is needed by Kimi Linear and DeepSeek-V3.2. Recurrent state with snapshots is the most expensive, needed by Jamba, Kimi Linear and MiniMax, each with a different layer type. With four engineers I would ship gpt-oss, Gemma 3, Llama 4 Scout, then DeepSeek-V3.2 without sparse attention, and one recurrent hybrid, deferring the other two.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>Correctness requirements first: window and chunk semantics, sinks, and Llama 4's temperature scaling must be exact, or the model is silently different. Per-layer allocation is then the memory win: without it gpt-oss costs twice its true cache and Scout about three times.</p>
<p>MLA second. DeepSeek-V3.2 can be served with dense MLA attention over the full context, losing DSA's compute saving at long context but producing outputs that differ from the trained sparse model only where the top-2,048 selection would have excluded tokens. That is a quality question to evaluate, not to assume; the alternative is building the indexer and sparse kernel now.</p>
<p>Recurrent layers last, because each type (Mamba, KDA, lightning) needs its own kernel and its own state layout, plus snapshots for prefix caching. Pick the one product needs most. Kimi Linear also needs MLA, so if MLA is already built it is the cheapest hybrid to add.</p>
<p>What we give up: two of the seven models wait a quarter, and DeepSeek-V3.2 ships with higher attention compute than its authors intended. The written artefact is a support matrix stating each model's status (exact, approximate, unsupported) with the evaluation that justified "approximate".</p>
<p>Failure mode of this plan: shipping DeepSeek-V3.2 with dense attention may pass short-context evals and differ at long context, which is where customers choose it; the evaluation has to use long production prompts.</p>
