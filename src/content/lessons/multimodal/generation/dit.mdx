## The problem the paper solves

By 2022 the denoiser in almost every strong diffusion model was a U-Net: convolutional down and up paths with attention at some resolutions. Transformers had taken over language and recognition, with well-understood scaling behaviour; diffusion had no comparable account of how quality grows with model size. Peebles and Xie asked whether the U-Net's inductive bias was needed at all, and whether a transformer denoiser would scale the way transformers do elsewhere.

## The mechanism in engineering terms

DiT keeps latent diffusion's pipeline and swaps one stage. A 256 × 256 image goes through the frozen autoencoder to a 32 × 32 × 4 latent. DiT cuts that latent into p × p patches, projects each patch to a token, adds positional embeddings, and runs a stack of transformer blocks. A linear decoder turns each output token back into a patch of predicted noise (and a predicted variance).

The patch size is the main compute dial. Tokens are (32 / p)²: 256 at p = 2, 64 at p = 4, 16 at p = 8. Parameters barely change with p, but compute does, because every layer runs over every token.

<Predict id="mml-gen-dit-tokens-512" question="At 512 × 512 the latent is 64 × 64 × 4. How many tokens does DiT-XL/2 (patch size 2) process per forward pass?" answer={1024} tolerance={0} unit="tokens">
<p>(64 / 2)² = 32² = <b>1,024</b> tokens, four times the 256 at 256 × 256. The paper reports 524.6 Gflops at 512 against 118.6 at 256, a ratio of 4.4: more than 4 because attention grows with the square of the token count.</p>
</Predict>

Conditioning is the part that took experiments. The timestep and class label have to reach every block. The paper tried four designs at 400K training steps:

| Block design | Gflops | FID-50K |
|---|---|---|
| In-context (condition as extra tokens) | 119.37 | 35.24 |
| Cross-attention | 137.62 | 26.14 |
| Adaptive layer norm (adaLN) | 118.56 | 25.21 |
| adaLN-Zero | 118.64 | 19.47 |

adaLN regresses the layer norm's scale and shift from the condition; adaLN-Zero also regresses a gating scale per residual branch, initialised to zero so each block starts as the identity. It is the cheapest design and the best. Cross-attention costs about 16% more compute for a worse score. For text conditioning, where the condition is a sequence rather than one vector, later systems went back to attention in some form; Stable Diffusion 3's answer is below.

## The key numbers

The model family, at p = 4: DiT-S, B, L and XL have 12, 12, 24 and 28 layers, hidden sizes 384, 768, 1,024 and 1,152, and 1.4, 5.6, 19.7 and 29.1 Gflops. Across the grid of sizes and patch sizes, FID falls as forward-pass Gflops rise. Two models with similar Gflops reach similar FID even when one gets there through depth and the other through smaller patches.

The headline: DiT-XL/2, trained for 7M steps, reaches FID 2.27 on class-conditional ImageNet 256 × 256 with guidance scale 1.5 and 250 DDPM sampling steps, and 9.62 without guidance. The U-Net latent diffusion baseline LDM-4 reports 3.60 guided and 10.56 unguided at about 104 Gflops against DiT-XL/2's 118.6; the pixel-space ADM U-Net used 1,120 Gflops.

<Predict id="mml-gen-dit-sample-tflops" question="DiT-XL/2 costs 118.6 Gflops per forward pass. The FID 2.27 result used 250 sampling steps with classifier-free guidance, which needs two passes per step. Denoiser compute per image, in Tflops?" answer={59.3} tolerance={0.5} unit="Tflops">
<p>250 × 2 × 118.6 Gflops = 59,300 Gflops = <b>59.3 Tflops</b> per image. The paper's own sampling-compute figures (15.2 Tflops for XL/2 at 128 steps) are 128 × 118.6 without the second guidance pass. When you compare published compute per image, check whether guidance passes are counted.</p>
</Predict>

The result that matters most for serving is the comparison of sampling compute against model compute. DiT-L/2 sampled for 1,000 steps spends about 80.7 Tflops per image; DiT-XL/2 sampled for 128 steps spends about 15.2 Tflops, roughly five times less, and has the better FID-10K, 23.7 against 25.9. The paper's conclusion is that more sampling steps cannot make up for less model compute. The budget goes into the forward pass, not into the step count.

## What changed in practice

**The denoiser became a transformer, and so did the infrastructure.** Attention kernels, fused MLPs, quantisation recipes, tensor and sequence parallelism, and profilers built for language models apply with little change. Movie Gen's 30B video backbone, trained with tensor, sequence and context parallelism and FSDP on up to 6,144 H100s, is that infrastructure applied to generation.

**Text conditioning moved into the sequence.** Stable Diffusion 3's MM-DiT gives text tokens and image tokens separate weights and joins the two sequences for attention, so information flows both ways instead of image tokens reading a fixed text embedding through cross-attention. The paper reports the architecture improves text comprehension, typography and human preference ratings, and that lower validation loss tracks better image quality across its model sizes up to 8B parameters at depth 38. It also trained with rectified flow rather than DDPM-style diffusion, which is the step-count lever from the first lesson. On GenEval its depth-38 model at 1024 px with DPO scores 0.74 against DALL-E 3's 0.67.

**Serving changed shape.** A U-Net's cost depended on the resolution through convolutions at several scales; a DiT's cost depends on the token count, so resolution, aspect ratio and frame count map directly onto sequence length. That makes batching a sequence-length problem (bucket by tokens) and makes long sequences, as in video, an attention problem: at Movie Gen's 73,728 tokens, attention is the larger share of each forward pass.

**Scaling became predictable enough to plan.** With a Gflops-to-quality relationship that holds across sizes, a team can choose model size and patch size for a quality target and price the serving cost before training, which was guesswork with U-Nets.

## What to say about it in an interview

The paper in six claims: a transformer over latent patches replaces the U-Net; adaLN-Zero is the cheapest and best conditioning block; quality tracks forward-pass Gflops; FID 2.27 at guidance scale 1.5 and 250 steps on ImageNet 256; sampling steps cannot substitute for model compute; and generation moves onto language-model infrastructure.

Its limits: The headline is class-conditional ImageNet, not text-to-image. FID at 256 × 256 says little about prompt adherence or text rendering. The paper's compute figures leave out the guidance pass.

## When this is wrong

- **The scaling result is on one dataset and one task.** Class-conditional ImageNet at 256 and 512; text-to-image and video scaling are later papers' claims, measured differently.
- **Gflops are not serving cost.** Two models with equal Gflops can differ in latency through attention kernel efficiency, memory traffic and how well their shapes fit the hardware.
- **FID is not product quality.** It measures distribution match on ImageNet's Inception features; it is blind to prompt following and text rendering, which drive most product complaints.
- **The U-Net is not dead everywhere.** For small, fixed-resolution models on constrained hardware, a well-tuned convolutional denoiser can still be the cheaper one to serve; the DiT argument is strongest at scale.
