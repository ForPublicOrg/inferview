## Where the wrong answer is caught

In medical, legal and financial products the error rate matters less than the path a wrong answer takes. A wrong answer can be caught in three places: before it is shown (system-side checks), before it acts (a gate held by a person or a deterministic rule), or after it acted (audit and remediation). Payment systems make the same split between holding a transfer for review and reversing it later. The difference with a model is that the wrong answer looks the same as a right one, so every gate has to be built on a check that does not trust the model's own account.

The public record is mostly the third placement, and it is expensive. In Mata v. Avianca (S.D.N.Y., 22 June 2023), counsel filed opinions that ChatGPT had invented. The court wrote that there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable AI tool, but that Rule 11 gives attorneys a gatekeeping role, and imposed a $5,000 penalty plus letters to each judge falsely named as the author of a fake opinion. One detail is the design lesson: when doubts arose, counsel asked ChatGPT whether one case, Varghese, was real, and it replied that it "does indeed exist". A check run by the generator, in the context that produced the error, is not a check. Charlotin's database lists 2,231 decisions as of 28 October 2026 in which a court or tribunal found or implied reliance on hallucinated material; its party filter counts 1,288 pro se litigants and 880 lawyers.

Moffatt v. Air Canada (British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal, 14 February 2024) shows the operator's side. The airline's chatbot told a customer he could claim a bereavement fare after travel. Its reply hyperlinked the airline's bereavement page, which said the policy does not apply after travel is complete. Air Canada argued it could not be held liable for the chatbot; the tribunal called that a remarkable submission, held the airline responsible for all the information on its website, and ordered $812.02. The answer carried a link to the right source, and the source contradicted it. A citation is a pointer, not support.

## Grounding to approved sources

In a high-risk route the corpus is a controlled artefact, not a crawl. Every document carries an owner, a version, an effective date and its scope (jurisdiction, product, patient population), and retrieval filters on that metadata before ranking, so the leave policy for the wrong country or the formulary from last year never reaches the model. The model's answer is limited to claims with a citation into that corpus, and a claim without one is not shown.

The API details constrain the design. Anthropic's Citations returns valid pointers into the documents you passed, character ranges for text and page ranges for PDFs, and custom content documents let you cite at the granularity you chunk: a contract clause, a dosing row, a policy paragraph. The `title` and `context` fields reach the model but cannot be cited, so version and owner metadata there is visible to the model and never appears as evidence. Citations cannot be combined with structured outputs; the request returns a 400 error. A route that needs both a JSON payload for a downstream system and per-claim citations makes two calls, or puts citation ids in its own schema and gives up the API's pointer guarantee. Either is defensible; the second needs its own check that every id exists.

Then check support. A judge or classifier reads each claim with its cited span and scores whether the span entails the claim. That checker is a model with an error rate, measured on labelled pairs from the route, including pairs like the Air Canada one where the span is on topic and says the opposite.

## Verification that fails independently

The value of a verifier is the share of the generator's errors it catches, and that depends on how correlated their failures are. Chain-of-Verification's factored variant answers each verification question in a fresh context without the draft, because the authors found models repeat hallucinations they can attend to. Mata is the unfactored version run by a user: same model, same conversation, same answer.

The strongest checks do not use a model at all. A case citation is looked up in a court database, a drug and dose against the formulary and the order record, an account number or ticker against the system of record, a date calculation recomputed in code. Where the source of truth is structured, the better design takes the high-risk value from it and lets the model write only the surrounding prose; a dose copied from the order system cannot be hallucinated. Model-based verifiers are for what has no structured source, and their independence is measured, not assumed: on the labelled set, count how often the verifier passes an item the generator got wrong.

## Designing the escalation path

An escalation path is a set of triggers, a receiver with authority, and a user-facing state. The triggers are concrete and logged by name: a claim with no citation, a support score below its threshold, route confidence below the threshold priced in the [previous lesson](/learn/safety/hallucination/why-hallucinate), an intent classified as high-stakes (a dose, a filing deadline, a transfer, an eligibility decision), and a user who disputes or repeats the question. Each trigger is a classifier with its own recall, so the path's miss rate is the share of wrong answers that pass every trigger, and that is the number the design owns.

The receiver is a named role that can act, with a service-level target, and the reviewer sees the claim, the cited span, the source version and the trigger that fired, not the whole conversation. The user sees a pending state that says a person is checking, not a hedged answer that reads as an answer.

Review degrades with volume. Anthropic reports, from its own telemetry, that users approved roughly 93% of Claude Code permission prompts and that attention per prompt falls as the count rises. Rehberger's essay on the normalisation of deviance applies Vaughan's term from the Challenger investigation to the same drift: output that is usually right stops being checked. Two controls keep review a control. Keep the trigger rate low enough that each item gets real attention, and seed the queue with known-wrong items to measure each reviewer's catch rate.

## Logging that supports review

Every answer in a high-risk route writes one record that lets someone reconstruct, months later, why the user saw what they saw: the request, the retrieved document ids with versions, the model id, prompt version and threshold version, each claim with its citation and support score, the triggers that fired, the decision, what was rendered, the reviewer's identity, decision and time, and the id of any downstream action. The same record is the replay input when the model or the prompt changes.

The answers that were not escalated are where the misses are, so the log is also the audit frame. A weekly random sample of non-escalated answers, labelled by a domain expert, estimates the miss rate. With zero misses in n clean audits, the 95% upper bound on the miss rate is the p that solves (1 - p)<sup>n</sup> = 0.05, about 3/n. Bounding misses below 0.1% takes about 3,000 clean audits; below 1%, about 300. That labelling budget is part of the launch cost.

```python
import math
from dataclasses import dataclass, field

HIGH_STAKES = {"dose", "deadline", "filing", "transfer", "eligibility"}

@dataclass
class Claim:
    text: str
    cited_doc: str | None      # document id and version, or None if no citation
    support: float             # support-checker score for (claim, cited span), 0..1

@dataclass
class Decision:
    action: str                # "answer", "escalate" or "refuse"
    triggers: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)

def route(claims: list[Claim], intent: str, confidence: float, threshold: float,
          support_min: float = 0.9) -> Decision:
    if not claims:
        return Decision("refuse", ["no supported answer"])
    triggers = []
    if any(c.cited_doc is None for c in claims):
        triggers.append("uncited claim")
    if any(c.cited_doc and c.support < support_min for c in claims):
        triggers.append("weak support")
    if confidence < threshold:
        triggers.append("low confidence")
    if intent in HIGH_STAKES:
        triggers.append(f"high-stakes intent: {intent}")
    return Decision("escalate" if triggers else "answer", triggers)

def audit_size(max_miss_rate: float, confidence: float = 0.95) -> int:
    """Clean audits needed so that zero misses bounds the miss rate below max_miss_rate."""
    return math.ceil(math.log(1 - confidence) / math.log(1 - max_miss_rate))

claims = [Claim("Leave is 16 weeks.", "handbook-ca@2026-07", 0.97),
          Claim("It can start before the birth.", None, 0.0)]
print(route(claims, "eligibility", 0.88, 0.8))   # escalate: uncited claim, high-stakes intent
print(route(claims[:1], "general", 0.88, 0.8))   # answer
print(audit_size(0.001), audit_size(0.01))       # 2995 299
```

## When this is wrong

The design treats the approved source as correct. Grounding moves the error from the model to the corpus owner, and a stale or wrong source produces a confident, perfectly cited wrong answer that every check in this lesson passes. Source ownership and review dates are part of the control, and the audit sample has to label against the world, not against the corpus.

It also assumes escalation is safe. Where delay is the harm, as in an urgent clinical question, a pending state can be worse than a qualified answer with an explicit instruction to call a person now. The trigger then routes to a faster channel, not to a queue.

The trigger recalls are measured on labelled traffic from people who are not trying to avoid them. A user who wants an answer the system withholds rephrases until no trigger fires, so repeated questions in one session are themselves a trigger, and trigger recall needs re-measuring on production samples.

The audit bound assumes independent samples from a stable distribution. A miss rate below 0.1% across all traffic can hide a much higher rate in a rare slice, such as paediatric doses or a new jurisdiction, so the slices that carry the most harm get their own audit sample.
