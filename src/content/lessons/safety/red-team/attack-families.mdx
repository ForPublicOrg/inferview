## What a surface check assumes

A surface check is a predicate over the text at one boundary, in one turn: a keyword list, a regex, a normaliser plus a blocklist, a similarity threshold against known attacks. Each one silently assumes five things about the attack. The payload is literally present in the text. It arrives in one message. It looks like a harmful request. The context around it is short. The check sees the same bytes the model sees.

The model satisfies none of those assumptions for the attacker. It decodes base64, reads low-resource languages, reassembles fragments, follows a story across turns and learns from examples in its context, all without being asked to. Wei, Haghtalab and Steinhardt's 2023 account of why safety training fails gives the two root causes. Competing objectives: the model is trained to help and follow instructions as well as to refuse, so an attack builds a situation where helping looks right. Mismatched generalisation: safety training covers less of the input space than capability training, so the model can understand inputs in places where it never learned to refuse. Attacks they designed from those two ideas succeeded on every prompt in a set of unsafe requests drawn from GPT-4's and Claude v1.3's own red-team evaluation sets.

A WAF matching SQL keywords failed for the same structural reason: the parser downstream accepted far more encodings than the filter upstream. The difference here is that the downstream parser is a model, its grammar is all of language, and there is no escaping function to switch to.

## Families by the assumption they break

<div class="tablewrap">

| Family | Example | Assumption broken | Published result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Encoding and obfuscation | base64, hex, homoglyphs, zero-width and tag characters, scrambled word interiors | the payload is literal | OWASP lists Base64, hex, Unicode smuggling and typoglycemia among current attacks |
| Translation | the request in a low-resource language | the payload is literal | 79% actionable answers from GPT-4 (Yong et al., 2023) |
| Best-of-N | random case flips, scrambling and noise, resampled | the payload is literal, and one attempt | 89% on GPT-4o, 78% on Claude 3.5 Sonnet at 10,000 samples |
| Payload splitting and reconstruction | fragments assembled by the model, or an answer emitted in pieces | one message, one output | OWASP LLM01 scenario; reconstruction attacks got through Anthropic's first-generation classifiers |
| Multi-turn escalation | Crescendo: benign questions that build on the model's replies | one message, and looks harmful | most tasks jailbroken in under five turns, capped at ten |
| Role-play and persona | DAN, the grandmother story, a debugging persona | looks harmful | competing objectives (Wei et al.) |
| Many-shot | hundreds of faux compliant dialogues in context | short context | power law in the number of shots, up to 256 tested |
| Hidden channel | tag characters or white-on-white text the reviewer cannot see | the check sees what the model sees | OWASP: screening a cleaned copy while forwarding the original lets hidden text through |

</div>

The last row is the one most often built by accident. A pipeline that normalises a copy for the filter and forwards the original to the model has a filter that reviews a different document from the one executed. OWASP's cheat sheet spells this out for tag characters, the block from U+E0000 to U+E007F, which render as nothing and which models can read.

NFKC folds fullwidth letters back to ASCII, so that variant is caught; it is the only obfuscation that is. NFKC keeps the zero-width space and the tag characters, the filter never decodes base64, scrambled interiors do not match, and splitting the phrase defeats substring matching. ## The layer that can stop each family

A layer either reads meaning or removes a capability. Surface checks do neither, which is why the right place for them is logging and cheap triage, never the boundary. OWASP's own wording is that input screening is not a security boundary.

**Encoding, translation, Best-of-N.** The layer that can lower the rate reads meaning: a model-based classifier, or the target model's own training. Both lower p and neither bounds it; Best-of-N reached its numbers against safety-trained frontier models, and a guardrail model is itself an LLM and itself injectable, as the cheat sheet notes. The cheat sheet recommends a purpose-trained classifier with a different attack surface from the primary model. What bounds the damage is upstream of all of it: the secret or the capability is not reachable from that session.

**Payload splitting and output obfuscation.** A check on input alone or output alone misses the attack by construction, because neither half is harmful. Anthropic's January 2026 classifier generation replaced separate input and output classifiers with an exchange classifier that judges each output together with its input; in their human red-teaming that cut successful jailbreaks by more than half, for about 50% more compute. These are vendor-measured numbers. The structural control is on the output path: if the output cannot carry data anywhere (no external URLs rendered, no outbound tool), a reassembled secret has nowhere to go.

**Multi-turn escalation.** Crescendo's prompts are benign and human-readable; the authors say input filters suit single-turn attacks with identifiable malicious content, and their automated version beat prior methods by 29 to 61% on GPT-4. Any per-message check misses it. A rate-lowering layer has to see the conversation; a structural layer limits what the conversation can reach, for example by scoping the session's tools to its task.

**Role-play and persona.** The payload is the frame, not a string. The layers are the same as for encoding: a meaning-reading classifier lowers the rate, and keeping the capability out of the session bounds it.

**Many-shot.** This is the one family with a structural fix on the input side: capping context length removes it, at the price of long inputs. Anthropic's other two results bracket the choice: fine-tuning against it only delayed it, while classifying and modifying prompts before the model saw them cut success from 61% to 2% in one case. Where the product needs long untrusted inputs, cap the share of context any single untrusted source can fill and classify the rest.

**Hidden channels.** This one has a by-construction fix at the surface, and it is not a filter. Decide what the model receives, make the check and the model see the same bytes, and strip or reject the character classes the product has no use for (tag characters, bidirectional controls, zero-width characters outside scripts that need them). It works because it removes a channel instead of judging content.

## Each family's regression test

The family decides how a found attack becomes a test that keeps it stopped:

- **Encoding and Best-of-N**: the pinned original plus generated variants on every run. garak's `encoding` and `badchars` probes and promptfoo's `base64`, `hex`, `rot13`, `homoglyph` and `best-of-n` strategies are generators for this.
- **Translation**: the same case in a fixed list of languages chosen from your users and from low-resource languages; promptfoo sets this with its `language` field.
- **Payload splitting**: an oracle that looks for the protected value in pieces and encodings, not only as a whole string. [Problem sfe-010](/problems/sfe-010) builds that detector.
- **Multi-turn**: the full transcript stored as the case, plus a multi-turn generator (promptfoo's `crescendo` or `goat` strategies, or garak's `atkgen`) for variants, because promptfoo does not transform authored multi-step sequences.
- **Role-play**: hand-written or model-written variants of the frame; garak's `dan` and `grandma` probes are seed material.
- **Many-shot**: a case at the maximum context the product accepts, so a context-limit change re-runs it.
- **Hidden channel**: a deterministic test with no model in the loop that asserts the stripped classes never reach the model, run on every pull request.

## When this is wrong

The families overlap and compose. A Crescendo conversation can be translated, a many-shot prompt can be encoded, and Best-of-N combined with a prefix attack did better than either alone. The table is for coverage, so that a red team or a suite does not forget a family; it is not a claim that one layer per family suffices.

The classification by assumption is about surface checks. A model-based classifier breaks the "payload is literal" assumption for the defender as well and catches many encoded and translated attacks; it then fails the way the target model fails, through competing objectives and mismatched generalisation of its own. Moving from keywords to a classifier changes the failure mode, not the category: a rate, not a boundary.

Several numbers here are from 2023 and 2024 models. The 79% translation result was GPT-4 in 2023 and has not been re-measured here on current models; Crescendo's comparisons were against the methods of its time. Re-measure the families on your own model before quoting any rate as current.

Hidden-channel stripping assumes the product has no use for the stripped classes. Zero-width joiners are required in some scripts and emoji sequences, and bidirectional controls in mixed right-to-left text. A strip list written for an English product will break other languages, so build it from the scripts your users write.
