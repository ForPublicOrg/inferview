## A catch rate is a per-attempt number

Every rate-lowering defence ships with one number: the share of attacks on an evaluation set that it stopped. That number describes one attempt. The attacker's number is different: the probability that at least one attempt inside their budget succeeds. With a per-attempt success rate p and n independent attempts, that is 1 - (1 - p)^n, and the expected number of attempts to the first success is 1/p.

A login form is judged by how many guesses the attacker gets and what one correct guess unlocks, never by the share of wrong guesses it rejects. Prompt-level defences are routinely judged the second way.

Independence is the wrong model in both directions, and the published attacks show each direction.

## Best-of-N: retrying is the whole attack

Best-of-N (Hughes et al., December 2024) needs no insight about the target. It takes one harmful request and samples augmented copies: each word longer than three characters scrambled with probability 0.6, each character's case flipped with probability 0.6, and characters nudged by one ASCII position with probability 0.06. It sends copies until one gets a harmful answer. With 10,000 samples per request the paper reports 89% attack success on GPT-4o and 78% on Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and 52% against a Llama 3 8B model trained with circuit breakers. The cheap end matters more: at N = 100 it already reached 50% on GPT-4o and 41% on Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and 53 to 71% of the Claude and GPT jailbreaks found within 10,000 samples were found in the first 100. The authors put N = 100 on GPT-4o at about $9.

Run the independence model against those numbers. If 41% at N = 100 came from one constant per-sample rate, that rate would be 1 - 0.59<sup>1/100</sup> = 0.53%, and 10,000 samples would succeed with probability indistinguishable from 1. The measured figure is 78%. The success rate is an average over requests, and requests differ: many fall in the first hundred samples, and a residual resists far longer. The paper fits this as -log(ASR) = a N<sup>-b</sup>, a power law, and fitting at N = 1,000 forecast the N = 10,000 result with 4.4% average error. For a defender the forecast is the useful part. Measure a defence at a budget you can afford, fit the curve, and read off what an attacker with a hundred times your budget gets.

Three more results from the paper change engineering decisions:

- Temperature 0 is not a fix. It lowered attack success by between 0.7% (Claude Opus) and 27.7% (Gemini Pro), because the attack varies the input, not the sampling. OWASP's cheat sheet reaches the same conclusion.
- A found jailbreak is not a key. Successful text attacks produced a harmful answer again on about 30% of resamples at temperature 1. A red-team finding replayed once will usually look fixed when it is not; the next lesson builds tests around that.
- Composition multiplies. Adding an optimised prefix cut the samples needed on Claude 3.5 Sonnet text from 6,000 to 274 for 74% success. Attack families stack, so a defence measured against each family alone overstates its strength.

## Many-shot: the context window is the budget

Anthropic's April 2024 many-shot result moves the retries inside one prompt. The attacker fills the context with faux dialogues, up to 256 in the study, in which an assistant answers harmful questions, then asks the real one. Effectiveness rose with the number of shots following a power law, the same shape benign in-context learning follows as demonstrations are added, and larger models were often more susceptible because they learn in context better. The mechanism is the capability; it cannot be trained away without training away in-context learning.

The mitigations Anthropic reported read like a cache-sizing argument. Capping context length prevents the attack completely and throws away long inputs. Fine-tuning the model to refuse such prompts only delayed it: more shots were needed, then harmful answers came anyway. That is a lower p, not a bound. Classifying and modifying prompts before the model sees them cut attack success from 61% to 2% in one case. The post presents that as one case, not a general figure.

## Adaptive attacks broke what static evaluations passed

*The Attacker Moves Second* (Nasr, Carlini, Tramèr and eleven co-authors, October 2025) took 12 published defences against jailbreaks and prompt injection, most of which had reported near-zero attack success, and attacked each with methods tuned to that defence: reinforcement learning with an attacker model updated by GRPO, an evolutionary search with an LLM mutator and a critic, gradient search where it applied, and human red-teamers.

The human half is the part to remember when budgeting a red team. Over 500 participants competed for a $20,000 prize pool across 40 challenges in an AgentDojo-based interface and defeated every challenge; on a selected subset humans succeeded 100% of the time against 69% for the automated search. The authors' own limit is the right framing for any red-team result: an empirical evaluation can show a defence is broken, never that it is robust.

## Vendor numbers, read as rates

Vendors that publish numbers for their own products now measure against adaptive attackers, and their numbers are useful once converted to the attacker's view. These are vendor-measured.

Anthropic's November 2025 browser-use post reports Claude Opus 4.5 at 1.4% attack success with its improved safeguards, down from 8.0% for the model alone, against an internal adaptive Best-of-N attacker given 100 attempts per environment. Under independence, 100 attempts at 1.4% give 1 - 0.986<sup>100</sup> = 75.6%, and the expected wait is 1 / 0.014 = 71 attempts. The post says itself that a 1% rate still represents meaningful risk.

Constitutional Classifiers show the price of pushing p down. The February 2025 version cut success on 10,000 synthetic jailbreak prompts from 86% to 4.4% for 23.7% more compute and 0.38% more refusals of harmless requests. The January 2026 generation reports about 1% added compute, 0.05% refusals on harmless traffic, and no universal jailbreak found in over 1,700 hours of red-teaming across 198,000 attempts. "Universal" is the important word. A universal jailbreak is a reusable key across requests; a Best-of-N success is found per request. If the harm you care about is one secret leaking once, the per-request rate is the number to use, and it is not zero for any published system.

## What the arithmetic does to the architecture

The procedure that follows from the numbers:

1. Measure p against an adaptive attacker at a budget you can afford, and fit the curve, not one point. A static battery result is an upper bound on robustness that may be off by an order of magnitude.
2. Estimate the attacker's budget n from the product, not from hope: who can send inputs, at what cost, under which rate limits and account controls. OWASP's wording is exact: rate limiting restricts attempt budgets and does not establish robustness.
3. Compute P(at least one success) over that budget and the attacker's expected cost, cost per attempt / p. Using Best-of-N's $9 per 100 samples on GPT-4o as a scale, a 1.4% rate costs the attacker about 0.09 / 0.014 = $6.43 per success, and a tenfold better defence costs them $64.
4. Where P(at least one success) is high over a realistic budget, the impact of one success has to be bounded by something that does not depend on p: the secret is not in the context, the session that reads untrusted content has no outbound channel, the action needs an approval the model cannot produce. Willison's lethal trifecta (private data, untrusted content, external communication) names the three things to keep apart.
5. Use the rate-lowering layer as a detector as well as a filter. Every caught attempt is a signal about an account.

The fifth point converts a filter into a budget limit. Suppose every failed attempt is flagged and an account is banned on its third flag. The attacker succeeds on an account only if a success arrives before the third flag:

```python
import math

def attempts_for(p, target):
    """Attempts for P(at least one success) >= target, attempts independent."""
    return math.ceil(math.log(1 - target) / math.log(1 - p))

def success_before_ban(p, flags_to_ban):
    """Every failed attempt is flagged; the account dies at the given flag count."""
    return 1 - (1 - p) ** flags_to_ban

print(attempts_for(0.05, 0.5), attempts_for(0.014, 0.5))   # 14 50
q = success_before_ban(0.05, 3)
print(round(q, 3), round(1 / q, 1))                         # 0.143 7.0
```

At a 5% per-attempt rate, 1 - 0.95<sup>3</sup> = 14.3% of accounts get a success before the ban, so the attacker needs about 7 accounts per success. The security property is now the cost of an account (verification, payment, reputation), a control the product team owns and can measure. The same filter, unmonitored, gave the attacker even odds by attempt 14.

## When this is wrong

Independence understates the attacker who adapts and overstates the attacker whose goal is hard. The Best-of-N curve shows both: success comes quickly for most requests and slowly for a residual. Use 1 - (1 - p)^n to argue that a rate is not a boundary, then measure the curve for your own targets instead of quoting the formula's output as a forecast.

Budgets are not always unlimited. An indirect injection that must sit in a document a particular user opens, an attack that needs an authenticated and paid account, or a harm that needs thousands of separate successes (extracting a table one row per success) all cap n or make each success small. In those cases a well-measured rate-lowering layer can be the right primary control, and saying so with the budget written down is a defensible design.

Attempts are visible. The arithmetic treats the defender as static, but the system sees every failed attempt, and detection that responds before the budget is spent changes the outcome more than a better filter does. That only holds if someone owns the response.

Vendor and paper numbers here are dated, model-specific and measured on the authors' own targets and harnesses; the paper's results are not directly comparable across defences. Use them for the shape of the argument and measure your own system for the numbers.
