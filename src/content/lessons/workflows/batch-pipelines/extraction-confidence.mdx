## The threshold is a headcount

An extraction pipeline that routes low-confidence values to people has one number that decides how many people it needs: the confidence threshold. In the labelled sample below, moving it from 0.95 to 0.99 raises the share of items sent to people from 26% to 40%, and nothing in the model changed. That makes the threshold a staffing decision first and a quality decision second, and it should be chosen like one: on a labelled sample, with the cost of a review and the cost of an escaped error written next to it, and revisited when either changes.

This lesson covers where the score comes from, how to check that it means anything, how to read the review-rate against error-rate curve, and how to read published accuracy figures without turning them into a per-document promise they do not make.

## Where the score comes from

Instacart's PARSE platform, described in August 2025, uses self-verification. After the extraction call, a second "scoring" prompt asks the model whether the extracted value is correct given the product data and the attribute definition, framed as an entailment question, and instructs it to answer yes or no first. The probability of "yes" on that first generated token is the confidence score. Low-confidence values go to human auditors, who correct them before the values reach the catalog. The post cites AutoMix for the method and publishes neither the threshold nor how it was chosen.

The method has three properties worth knowing before you copy it.

It costs a second call per value, though a short one: the scoring prompt repeats the inputs but generates one token. With a shared prefix it caches well.

It depends on the API returning token log probabilities. Not every API or model does, and support can differ by endpoint and by output mode, so check the reference for the exact model and endpoint you deploy. Without log probabilities the substitutes are coarser: sampling the verifier several times and using the agreement rate (granular to one over the sample count), or a verbal score in the output, which is a number the model wrote rather than a probability it assigned.

And it saturates. In OpenAI's cookbook recipe on log probabilities, clear cases score 100.0%, and two questions the article only partly covered scored 99.14% for one answer and 99.59% for the opposite one. The recipe itself says confidence measures how sure the model is, not whether it is right. Kadavath et al. (2022) found larger models well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false questions "in the right format", and found calibration harder to keep on new tasks. AutoMix treats self-verification as noisy enough that it puts a separate router on top of it. A score that clusters at the top leaves a threshold with very few useful positions.

## Check calibration before choosing anything

A score is usable for routing if, on a labelled sample, higher scores mean fewer errors, and it is usable for promises if the score in each range matches the observed accuracy in that range. Bin the labelled sample by score and count errors per bin. Here is a toy sample of 1,000 labelled extractions, built to look like a saturating verifier:

The score ranks well: error rates fall bin by bin. It is overconfident at the top: the 0.99 bin is 98.0% accurate, and with 12 errors in 600 the 95% Wilson interval on its error rate is 1.1% to 3.5%, wholly above the 1% the score implies. The 0.95 bin looks overconfident too, but 8 errors in 140 gives an interval of 2.9% to 10.9%, which still touches the claimed range; that bin needs more labels before it supports a conclusion. Sixty percent of items sit in the top bin, and its 12 errors can be caught only by reviewing everything. No threshold reaches them.

Two consequences. Ranking is enough to choose a threshold; calibration is needed only if you quote the score to anyone as a probability. And the labelling budget belongs where the decision is: around the candidate thresholds and in the top bin, where errors are rare and an estimate needs hundreds of labels. With zero errors in 300 labelled items, the 95% upper bound on the error rate is still about 1% (3 / 300 by the rule of three; 1.2% exact).

## The curve, and the price of a caught error

Each position of the threshold buys a set of caught errors with a number of reviews. The useful number per bin is **reviews per caught error**: the 0.95 to 0.99 bin costs 140 reviews for 8 errors, 17.5 reviews each, which at 2 minutes is 35 minutes of reviewer time per error caught. The top bin costs 600 reviews for 12 errors, 50 each. Whether 35 minutes is worth one error depends on what an error costs, which is a business number, so put it in the calculation explicitly.

With the defaults (an escaped error costs $25, a reviewer $40 an hour), the cheapest position reviews everything below 0.99 at $16,667 a day, only $266 cheaper than reviewing below 0.95, with 93 more reviewer hours. The curve is flat near its minimum, so the cost model does not pick the threshold on its own; reviewer capacity does. Set the error cost to $10 and the cheapest position moves down to 0.90, at $10,400 a day. The threshold follows the cost of an error, which is why one threshold per pipeline is usually wrong: an invoice amount and an invoice description do not cost the same when wrong, and each field gets its own threshold, chosen on its own labelled sample.

The reviewer is not ground truth either. The widget assumes every routed error is caught; in practice reviewers miss some, and a side-by-side UI that pre-fills the model's value invites accepting it. Measure the review step the same way as the model: seed known errors into the queue and count how many come back corrected.

## What a published accuracy figure means per document

Uber's April 2025 post reports TextSense at 90% overall accuracy, with 35% of submitted invoices at 99.5% accuracy and 65% above 80%. Accuracy is computed per field, at header and line level, with exact matching for fields such as invoice numbers and fuzzy string matching for fields such as descriptions. The post does not say how fields are weighted into the overall figure, and it does not state the share of invoices with every field correct, which is the number accounts payable cares about.

<Predict id="wfl-conf-perdoc" question="An invoice has 18 extracted fields, each correct 97% of the time, independently. What percentage of invoices have every field correct?" answer={58} tolerance={2} unit="%">
<p>0.97<sup>18</sup> = <b>0.578</b>, so 58%. At 90% per field the same arithmetic gives 0.90<sup>18</sup> = 15%. Field accuracy and document accuracy are different metrics that differ by a power of the field count.</p>
<p>Uber's figures do not fit the independence model: 15% fully correct invoices would be incompatible with 35% of invoices at 99.5%. Errors cluster in some invoices (a hard template, a poor scan, many line items) and spare others. That clustering is useful: document-level signals such as the vendor, the page count or a failed line-item sum predict where the errors are, and they route whole documents better than per-field scores do.</p>
</Predict>

So "90% accuracy" is not a per-invoice success rate, and it supports no promise about how many invoices can skip review. The number to report for a pipeline that writes documents to a system of record is the share of documents auto-accepted and the error rate among them, with an interval, per field class.

## In the room

<Callout kind="interview" label="Staff question">
A catalog team extracts a gluten-free attribute for about 2 million product values a week and launches the filter in three weeks. Legal treats a wrong "gluten-free: yes" as a safety issue; a missing badge is only a lost search result. Eight auditors have about six productive hours a day each. On a pilot, the verifier gives 70% of values a score of 0.99 or higher. Choose the routing and say what ships.
</Callout>

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The errors are asymmetric, so the threshold should be too: route only positive values, and only those without an explicit claim the pipeline can point to. Eight auditors at six hours give 240 hours a week, about 14,400 one-minute reviews, which is under 1% of 2 million, so reviewing by score alone cannot work. Ship the badge only where an extracted "yes" is backed by a quoted certification or label claim, show no badge otherwise, and put the audit hours on a labelled sample of shipped positives to measure the error rate.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>A false "no" costs a missing badge, so negatives auto-accept at any score. A false "yes" is the harm, so the routing applies to positives. The score saturates at the top, and a 0.99 bin can still hold errors at a rate that matters for a safety claim, so the score cannot be the only gate on positives. Requiring evidence (the extraction must quote the text or packaging claim, and a deterministic check confirms the quote exists in the source) gives a second, uncorrelated signal.</p>
<p>Capacity sets the rest: 8 × 6 × 5 = 240 hours a week. Some of those go to a stratified sample of shipped positives, enough to put an upper bound on their error rate before launch; zero errors in 300 labels bounds it near 1%, and legal may need a tighter bound, which costs proportionally more labels. The remainder reviews positives that lack evidence, if the team wants them badged at all.</p>
<p>What I give up: coverage. Products that are gluten-free but do not say so on the listing get no badge at launch. I would write that down with the measured share of evidence-backed positives, and with the reversal condition: if the sampled error rate on shipped positives exceeds the bound legal signs, the filter goes back to evidence-only and verified-only values.</p>

## When this is wrong

A self-verification score shares the extractor's blind spots. When the same model reads the same image twice, an unreadable digit is misread twice and verified with confidence. Errors that come from the input rather than from reasoning are the ones the score misses; a different model, a different input representation or a deterministic rule is what decorrelates the check.

Calibration does not transfer. A threshold chosen on one attribute, one category mix or one model version says little about the next. Kadavath et al. report calibration trouble on new tasks; in a pipeline, the new task arrives as a new product category or a supplier with a new template. Re-label a sample after each model change and each change in input mix, and treat a sudden drop in review rate after an upgrade as a calibration question before a quality win.

The toy cost model is linear: every escaped error costs the same and every review takes the same time. Neither holds. Some errors are worth a thousand times others, and hard items take reviewers longer, so the bins that route the most errors also cost the most minutes per review. Where the error cost has a long tail, route by field and by stakes (amount over a limit, a safety attribute) before routing by score.

And a review queue is only a control if it is drained. A threshold that sends more work than the team clears in the latency budget turns into a backlog, and backlogs get bulk-approved. Size the threshold to the team, and alert on queue age, not only on queue length.
