## What it is and who runs it

TextSense is Uber's internal document-processing platform. The Uber engineering post that describes it (April 2025, by Rohit Subudhi, Rakesh Vagvala and Sushil Kumar Jain Devichand) covers its first large use: extracting data from supplier invoices so that accounts payable no longer keys them in. Uber works with thousands of suppliers; invoices arrive in more than 25 languages, in varied templates, sometimes handwritten or scanned, sometimes spanning several pages, and each carries 15 to 20 header attributes plus line items. The previous tools, rule-based systems and robotic process automation, needed a person to write rules for every new format.

Everything below comes from that one post. Where the post is silent, this teardown says so rather than filling the gap.

**Intake.** Invoices arrive two ways. A user can upload a PDF in Uber's invoicing web app, whose back end forwards it to TextSense. Or an ingestion service reads open tickets in the ticketing system, pulls supplier emails and their PDF attachments, sends the email text to TextSense for parsing and sends the PDFs with the email details to the same back-end endpoint. Files land in object storage. The post does not say how often the ingestion service reads tickets or whether anything is event-driven.

**Cadence.** Uber's workflow platform orchestrates the steps, which the post describes as nonlinear and verbose. Cadence is a durable-execution engine, so a step that fails under it, an OCR call or a model call, can be retried on its own instead of rerunning the invoice from intake. The post does not describe the workflow definition, retry policies or timeouts.

**Pre-processing.** Image augmentation for low-resolution scans and handwriting, format conversion, and handling of multi-page documents, before any text is read.

**OCR.** Uber's Vision Gateway, its computer-vision platform, reads the document images.

**Model extraction.** A model extracts fields such as invoice number, date and amount, and the line items. GPT-4 was the production choice after a comparison described below. The post says the models are periodically retrained, a sentence that fits the fine-tuned candidates better than GPT-4; it does not say what is retrained in production.

**Post-processing rules.** Business logic validates, enriches and refines the extracted data, and cross-checks it against existing databases or predefined rules, before human review. The post gives no example rules.

**Side-by-side review UI.** Reviewers see the PDF beside the extracted values, with multiple alerts and soft warnings. They correct and approve.

**ERP write-back.** Approved documents are processed as invoices and sent to the ERP for approval and vendor payment. Monitoring tracks speed, accuracy and cost across the pipeline.

## Context strategy and model choice

The post describes model selection in more detail than the production prompt. The team trained and tested on the last year of invoice data, split 90% for training and 10% for testing, pairing text extracted from the PDFs with the structured fields entered for those invoices. It evaluated a seq2seq model, Llama 2, a fine-tuned Flan T5 and GPT-4. The fine-tuned Flan T5 exceeded 90% accuracy on header fields, but its accuracy dropped on line items after the first line, and fine-tuning produced hallucinated line data. The post's summary of the comparison is that the "LLM model had slightly higher header accuracy" while "GenAI was the winner for line prediction"; read with the surrounding text, the fine-tuned model led narrowly on headers and GPT-4 won on line items, so the team chose GPT-4 out of the box and added the post-processing layer. The comparison table is an image in the post, so its values are not reproduced here.

The engineering lesson in that choice is about where the errors were. Headers are a fixed set of short fields that a small fine-tuned model learns from a year of examples. Line items are variable-length tables, and a model that degrades after the first row fails on exactly the part of an invoice that carries the amounts. The selection was made on the hard half of the document.

## Tools and permission model

The model has no tools and no write access. It produces fields; rules check them; a person approves them; only then does anything reach the ERP. The permission boundary is the review step, and it sits before the write that moves money. In the terms of the document-pipeline lesson, TextSense gates before the write, which is the right side for a system of record that pays vendors.

## Cost and latency

The post reports a 2x reduction in manual invoicing, a 70% reduction in average handling time and a 25 to 30% cost saving compared with the manual process. It gives no cost per invoice, no model latency, no invoice volume and no token counts, so none of these can be derived. The cost saving is relative to manual processing, which means it includes reviewer time; it is not a model cost figure.

## Failure modes

The post names the hard inputs: diverse templates, more than 25 languages, handwriting, scans and multi-page invoices, plus brittle RPA rules in the previous system. It names one model failure: line-item accuracy after the first row, with hallucinated line data from fine-tuned models.

Three further risks follow from the architecture as published; they are questions for the design, not reported incidents. Two intake channels (upload and ticket) can deliver the same invoice twice, and the post does not describe deduplication. Field-level alerts help a reviewer only if the reviewer reads them, and a side-by-side UI with values pre-filled makes accepting the model's value the fastest action. And a pipeline measured by field accuracy can improve its headline number while the share of invoices needing any correction stays flat, because errors cluster in some invoices.

## What is not public

- How the 90% overall accuracy is computed beyond exact and fuzzy matching per field: the field weighting, how header and line accuracy are combined, and the fuzzy-match threshold.
- What the per-invoice figures mean: whether "65% above 80%" includes the 35% at 99.5%, and what share of invoices had every field correct. The 90% is not a per-invoice success rate.
- Review thresholds and routing: whether every invoice is reviewed, what raises an alert or a soft warning, and whether any score routes fields.
- Volumes: invoices per day, pages per invoice, review queue sizes.
- The production prompt, whether the model reads OCR text or page images, and what is periodically retrained.
- Cost per invoice and model latency.
- How the Cadence workflow handles retries, timeouts and duplicate submissions.

The post's future work lists a document classification layer, ensemble or chained models, more document types and integrations, and moving toward fully automated processing for historically perfect cases.

## Interview questions

<Callout kind="interview" label="Staff question">
You own TextSense. Finance wants straight-through processing (no human touch) for invoices from suppliers whose past invoices were always extracted perfectly, which the post lists as future work. The ERP pays automatically on approval. Design the rule for which invoices skip review, and what would take a supplier off the list.
</Callout>

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>Skip review only per supplier and template, never per model score alone: a supplier qualifies after a run of reviewed invoices with zero corrections large enough to bound its error rate, every rule passes on the current invoice, and the amount is under a limit. A supplier leaves the list on any correction found by a sampled audit, a template change or a model change. Keep auditing a random share of skipped invoices, because without it the skip list can only grow.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>"Historically perfect" needs a number. Zero corrections in 300 reviewed invoices bounds the per-invoice error rate near 1% at 95% confidence; zero in 30 bounds it near 10%. The qualifying run is a business choice on that curve, and it decides how many suppliers ever qualify, so I would show finance the curve instead of picking alone.</p>
<p>Per invoice, the skip also requires every post-processing rule to pass, no soft warning, and an amount under a limit set with the controller, since the cost of a wrong payment scales with the amount. The skip list keys on supplier and template, because a supplier that changes invoice software is a new distribution.</p>
<p>Removal triggers: a correction on any audited invoice, a template fingerprint change, and every model or prompt change, which re-qualifies everyone on a shadow run. What I give up: the audit sample costs reviewer time forever, and the qualifying run delays savings for new suppliers. The failure mode of this design is a slow drift (a supplier adds a field the schema ignores) that no rule catches; the audit sample is the only control for it.</p>
</Fragment>
