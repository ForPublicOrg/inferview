export const solution = String.raw`def evaluate(chunks, retrieved, relevant):
    want = set()
    for s, e in relevant:
        want.update(range(s, e))
    got, total = set(), 0
    for i in retrieved:
        s, e = chunks[i]
        got.update(range(s, e))
        total += e - s
    hit = len(want & got)
    recall = hit / len(want) if want else 0.0
    precision = hit / total if total else 0.0
    union = len(want) + total - hit
    return recall, precision, (hit / union if union else 0.0)
`;

## The unit of retrieval

The chunk is the unit that gets embedded, matched, ranked, placed in the prompt and paid for. Whatever the chunker separates, retrieval cannot reunite: a table row cut from its header, a clause cut from the defined term it uses, a step cut from the condition that guards it. Chunking is a schema decision about the retrieval index, made before any query arrives, and like a schema it is expensive to change, because every change re-embeds the corpus.

Three properties of a chunk matter. Its boundaries decide which facts travel together. Its size decides how many topics one vector has to represent (too many and it matches none of them well) and how many tokens each retrieved hit costs in the prompt. Its context, whatever text or embedding signal about the rest of the document it carries, decides whether "the Supplier shall give 30 days notice" can be matched by a query that names the supplier.

## Splitters, from blind to structural

**Fixed size** cuts every N tokens with an optional overlap. OpenAI's vector stores do this by default: the `auto` strategy uses 800-token chunks with 400 tokens of overlap, and a `static` strategy accepts 100 to 4,096 tokens with overlap at most half the chunk (checked 2026-10-28).

**Recursive** splitting tries separators in order and descends to the next only for pieces still too long. LangChain's `RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter` defaults to paragraph break, line break, space, then single characters.

**Structure-aware** splitting cuts on the document's own structure. The same LangChain class takes per-language separator lists: for Markdown it tries headings, code fences and horizontal rules before paragraphs; for Python it tries `class` and `def` first. It only works if the parser kept the structure, which is why the parsing route comes first.

**Semantic** splitting embeds sentences and cuts where neighbours stop being similar; **parent-child** retrieval matches on small chunks and returns their enclosing section, separating the unit of matching from the unit of reading.

<Callout kind="gotcha" label="Characters, not tokens">
LangChain's base `TextSplitter` defaults to `chunk_size=4000`, `chunk_overlap=200` and `length_function=len`, which counts characters. Code that says "1,000-token chunks" while using the default length function is off by the characters-per-token ratio of the corpus. Switching to `from_tiktoken_encoder` fixes the unit but not the vocabulary unless you pass one: its `encoding_name` defaults to `gpt2`.
</Callout>

## What the published comparison shows

The most careful public comparison is Chroma's July 2024 report. It labels relevant excerpts for 472 generated queries over five corpora, retrieves 5 chunks with text-embedding-3-large, and scores at the token level: recall is the share of relevant tokens retrieved, and IoU divides the relevant tokens retrieved by the union of relevant and retrieved tokens, counting text that overlapping chunks share once per chunk.

Read the ± column before the means. The report says it is a standard deviation and that means are over all queries and corpora. If that spread is across the 472 queries, the standard error of each recall mean is about 30 / √472 ≈ 1.4 points, and of an unpaired difference between two rows about 2.0. The 1.4-point gap between 400 tokens with and without overlap is inside that, and so is most of the recall column; the report does not publish the per-query pairs that would tighten it. The IoU differences are another matter: the standard error of a mean with a spread of 5.6 is 5.6 / √472 ≈ 0.26, so 6.9 against 1.4 is not noise. What this benchmark supports is narrow: on these corpora with this embedding model, the chunkers are hard to separate on recall, and the 800/400 default retrieves the most text that is not needed. The authors also report that with a small embedding model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) overlap did help recall, 82.4 against 77.1 at 250 tokens, so even the overlap conclusion depends on the embedder.

The queries were generated by an LLM and filtered, and the authors name the limits themselves: a small evaluation, limited diversity and a recognisable question style. It is the method to copy, not the numbers.

## Putting context back into the chunk

Small chunks match precisely and lose their surroundings. Two published techniques restore the surroundings without growing the chunk.

**Contextual retrieval** (Anthropic, September 2024) has a model write 50 to 100 tokens situating each chunk in its document, prepends that text, and indexes the result for both embeddings and BM25. On Anthropic's datasets the top-20 retrieval failure rate, one minus recall@20, fell from 5.7% to 3.7% with contextual embeddings, to 2.9% adding contextual BM25, and to 1.9% adding a reranker over the top 150. Those are reductions of a 5.7% failure rate, measured by the vendor on its own data. The reported one-time cost is $1.02 per million document tokens, and it depends on prompt caching.

With caching, the 8,000-token document is written to the cache once and read nine times at the cache-read rate, which is why the published cost per million document tokens is about a dollar. </Predict>

**Late chunking** (Jina, 2024, revised July 2025) runs a long-context embedding model over the whole document, then mean-pools the token embeddings inside each chunk's span, so each chunk vector has seen its neighbours. There are no extra model calls and no training. In the paper's Table 2, averaged over four BEIR datasets and three models, nDCG@10 rose from 52.2 to 54.0 with 256-token chunks, from 52.4 to 54.3 with five-sentence chunks and from 52.4 to 53.8 with semantic boundaries; the largest single gain was NFCorpus with jina-embeddings-v2-small, 23.5 to 30.0, and in a few cells late chunking scored lower than naive. It needs token-level outputs from the embedding model, which an API that returns one pooled vector per input does not provide, and documents longer than the model's context go through the paper's long variant, which encodes overlapping macro chunks.

The cheapest context is often deterministic. A parsed contract already knows its title, parties, effective date and the section path of every clause; prefixing "Acme supply agreement, 2025, section 14.2 Termination" to each chunk costs no model call and fixes the most common missing reference.

## Choosing defaults for two corpora

Two corpora, both scenarios. Corpus A is an internal policy wiki in Markdown: short pages, headings every few paragraphs, small tables. Corpus B is the contract set from the parsing playbook: 200-page agreements parsed to HTML with section numbers, defined terms and fee tables.

The eval is the same for both. Sample real questions (or generate them and keep only those a reviewer accepts), and for each mark the relevant spans as token offsets in the parsed text, not as chunk ids, so the labels survive re-chunking. Score every candidate configuration on token-level recall at the k the generator will actually receive and on IoU, which stands in for prompt cost. Compare configurations per query, paired, because the same hard questions are hard for every chunker and pairing removes that variance. The decision rule: among configurations whose recall is within two paired standard errors of the best, take the one with the highest IoU.

For Corpus A the candidates are recursive splitting at 200 and 400 tokens with and without overlap, and the Markdown separators at a 400-token cap with each table kept whole and the heading path prefixed. The default to test first is the structural one, because the pages are short enough that a heading section is usually one topic. It stands if the eval finds no recall loss against the best fixed-size row.

For Corpus B, size is the wrong knob. The candidates are clause-level chunks on section numbers, each with the deterministic prefix above, matched small and returned with their parent section; the same with contextual retrieval added for clauses that refer to other sections; and a fixed 400-token baseline. Contextual retrieval is worth its model calls only if the eval shows cross-reference questions failing on the deterministic prefix, so the labelled set has to contain those questions on purpose.

## When this is wrong

Everything here treats the chunk as the unit of meaning. For code, the unit is a function or class, and call relationships matter more than size. For conversations it is a speaker turn and its reply. For documents dense with cross-references ("as defined in section 2.1") no chunker suffices; the referenced text has to be resolved and attached, which is a parsing job.

Chunking can also be the wrong lever. Anthropic's own post says a knowledge base under about 200,000 tokens can go whole into the prompt. A reranker or hybrid retrieval may recover more than any chunker change, and is cheaper to roll back.

The evidence is thin in a specific way. Chroma's comparison is one benchmark with one embedder and generated questions; Anthropic's contextual retrieval figures come from Anthropic's datasets in 2024; the late chunking gains are averages that hide cells where it lost. None of them is a replication of the others, and none uses your documents.
