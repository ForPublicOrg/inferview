## The three choices inside every score

An nDCG@10 is computed from a query sample, a set of judged documents and a grade scale. All three are fixed before any system runs, and a different choice of any one changes which system wins. A public leaderboard has made all three choices on someone else's corpus and someone else's users. For a new search product with no labels, the evaluation is a build: you choose queries, you choose which documents get judged, you write the scale, and you pay for the judgments. This lesson covers each choice and what it costs. The next lesson covers putting a model in the judge's seat.

## Why other people's numbers do not transfer

BEIR was built to test exactly this. Across 18 datasets and 10 systems, BM25 underperformed the neural retrievers by 7 to 18 points on MS MARCO, the in-domain set they were trained on, yet proved one of the strongest baselines once the data changed. The in-domain ranking did not predict the out-of-domain one.

BRIGHT pushes the same point onto queries whose relevant documents share neither words nor surface meaning with the query: a StackExchange post whose answer cites a page on soil salinity, or a coding question whose relevant document is the syntax reference for a different function. SFR-Embedding-Mistral led the MTEB retrieval leaderboard at 59.0 nDCG@10 when the authors checked it on 28 May 2024; on BRIGHT it scored 18.3. BM25 averaged 14.5. Two more BRIGHT results bear on design choices you will make later. Replacing the query with LLM-written reasoning steps improved retrieval by up to 12.2 points. And a cross-encoder trained on MS MARCO made results worse: reranking BM25's top 10 moved the average from 14.3 to 13.1, and reranking the top 100 dropped it to 8.3, while GPT-4 reranking the top 10 raised it to 17.4.

A leaderboard is still useful as a shortlist. It cannot tell you which of three shortlisted models wins on your queries, because the gap between them on the leaderboard is smaller than the gap between the leaderboard's queries and yours.

## Choice one: the query sample

The queries define what "good" means, so they come from the people who will search. A product with logs samples from them. A product without logs builds a sample from the closest records of real need: support tickets, questions posted in internal chat, the tasks a design partner wrote down, searches run against the system it replaces. Write each one as the searcher typed it, not as a cleaned-up question, because cleaning removes the identifiers, typos and fragments that separate lexical from dense retrieval.

Stratify before you sample, and report every metric per stratum. Three strata cover most enterprise search: identifier lookups (an error code, a ticket number, a SKU), natural-language questions, and reasoning-heavy queries where the relevant document never restates the question, which is BRIGHT's category. A sample drawn uniformly from early logs under-represents the third stratum, which is where the expensive retrieval changes earn or lose their cost.

Breadth beats depth when the budget is fixed. Thomas and colleagues report that Bing prefers small judgment sets for many queries over TREC's practice of judging many documents for fewer queries. The variance you are fighting is across queries, and a system comparison over 50 deeply judged queries says more about those 50 than about the product.

## Choice two: which documents get judged

Nobody judges the corpus. Pooling judges the union of the top results from several systems and treats every other document as not relevant. That rule produces two distortions, and the widget below shows both on one query.

With the default pool (BM25 and the hybrid run, judged to depth 10), annotators judge 13 documents. The new embedding model has 5 unjudged documents in its top 10 and measures 0.674, last of the four. With every document judged it scores 0.944, first. The pooled systems are wrong in the other direction: the ideal ordering is built only from documents someone judged, so it is missing the relevant documents only the new model found, and the hybrid run measures 0.990 against a true 0.815. Now pool all four systems at depth 5: 11 judgments, fewer than before, and every system's score is exact because the shallow, diverse pool found all 8 relevant documents.

BEIR measured the first distortion on a real collection. TREC-COVID's pool came from many teams' systems, yet the share of each system's top 10 that annotators had never seen was 6.4% for BM25 and 31.8% for TAS-B. After the authors judged 980 missing pairs, ANCE moved from 0.654 to 0.735, from slightly below BM25 to 6.7 points above it. The [metric-choice lesson](/learn/evals/golden-sets/metric-choice) covers the hole rate as a metric; here the point is the design rule that follows. The pool needs at least one contributor from every retrieval family you might ship: lexical, dense, hybrid, and a run with an LLM-rewritten query, because on BRIGHT that rewrite improved retrieval by up to 12.2 points, which means it ranks relevant documents that the plain-query runs leave below the cut. Every later system gets its unjudged top-k documents judged before its comparison is accepted.

## Choice three: the grade scale

Binary labels cannot separate "answers the question" from "mentions the topic", and that difference is what users notice. The TREC Deep Learning tracks use four grades: 0 irrelevant, 1 related but does not answer, 2 has some answer that may be unclear or buried, 3 dedicated to the query and contains the exact answer. With gains of 2<sup>g</sup> - 1, a grade-3 document is worth seven grade-1 documents, so the scale is also a statement about what you value. A product whose users act on one answer can put most of the weight on grade 3; a research tool where users read several documents might flatten the gains.

The scale is only as reliable as the people applying it, and the published ceiling is lower than most teams assume. Thomas and colleagues cite two groups of trained TREC assessors agreeing at a Cohen's kappa of 0.52, and assessors shown the same document a second time agreeing with their own first label 52% of the time. Google, they note, gives its search quality raters over 170 pages of guidelines. Your guideline will be shorter, so make every page count: for each grade, three examples from your corpus with the reason, and explicit rules for the cases your domain makes ambiguous (an outdated version of the right document, a correct answer behind a permission the searcher lacks). Re-judge a fixed slice each week and track agreement, because drift in the judges looks exactly like drift in the system.

Two collection details from published sets show the trade-offs in labelling rules. Near-duplicate passages in DL 2022 and 2023 had labels propagated from one judged copy, which saves judgments but means the label counts overstate the human work. And BRIGHT kept a StackExchange positive only when a student annotator and two PhD reviewers agreed unanimously; that buys precision in the labels at the cost of dropping hard cases, which is the wrong trade for a product whose hard cases are the point.

## Building it

The pool, the hole rate and graded nDCG fit in a few lines, and running them on the widget's data shows the numbers trec_eval would report:

```python
import math

def build_pool(runs, depth):
    """Union of each contributing run's top `depth` documents, per query."""
    pool = {}
    for run in runs:
        for qid, docs in run.items():
            pool.setdefault(qid, set()).update(docs[:depth])
    return pool

def ndcg10(docs, qrels):
    """Graded nDCG@10 with gain 2^g - 1; a document missing from qrels scores 0."""
    gain = lambda g: 2 ** g - 1
    dcg = sum(gain(qrels.get(d, 0)) / math.log2(i + 2) for i, d in enumerate(docs[:10]))
    best = sorted(qrels.values(), reverse=True)[:10]
    idcg = sum(gain(g) / math.log2(i + 2) for i, g in enumerate(best))
    return dcg / idcg if idcg else 0.0

def holes10(docs, judged):
    return sum(d not in judged for d in docs[:10]) / 10

# One query from the widget above; in practice each run covers every sampled query.
bm25   = {"q1": ["d03", "d01", "d07", "d02", "d14", "d04", "d05", "d11", "d06", "d08"]}
hybrid = {"q1": ["d03", "d11", "d07", "d21", "d01", "d14", "d18", "d02", "d09", "d04"]}
new    = {"q1": ["d27", "d11", "d21", "d25", "d03", "d18", "d15", "d16", "d07", "d17"]}
truth  = {"d03": 3, "d07": 2, "d11": 3, "d14": 1, "d18": 2, "d21": 2, "d25": 1, "d27": 3}

pool = build_pool([bm25, hybrid], depth=10)["q1"]
qrels = {d: truth.get(d, 0) for d in pool}          # what annotators produce: grades for pooled documents only
print("judgments:", len(pool))
print("new model holes@10:", holes10(new["q1"], qrels))
print("new model nDCG@10 on the pool: %.3f" % ndcg10(new["q1"], qrels))
print("new model nDCG@10, all judged: %.3f" % ndcg10(new["q1"], truth))
print("BM25 nDCG@10 on the pool:      %.3f" % ndcg10(bm25["q1"], qrels))
# judgments: 13
# new model holes@10: 0.5
# new model nDCG@10 on the pool: 0.674
# new model nDCG@10, all judged: 0.944
# BM25 nDCG@10 on the pool:      0.706
```

The release rule that follows: no comparison is reported while any compared system has a hole rate above the threshold you set, and the fix for a hole is a judgment, not a footnote.

## When this is wrong

Pooling assumes the contributing systems between them find nearly every relevant document. For queries with many relevant documents, or a corpus with heavy duplication, even a diverse pool misses some, and every system's score is then relative to the pool rather than absolute. Comparisons between systems that all contributed stay fair; absolute numbers do not.

Graded relevance judges a document against a query in isolation. It does not see that the right document is stale, that the searcher lacks permission to open it, or that two top results contradict each other; those need their own checks, and the [permissions and freshness lesson](/learn/rag/retrieval-foundations/permissions-freshness) covers the first two.

Offline judgments measure whether relevant documents rank high. They do not measure whether people find what they need, which can diverge when the interface, snippets or answer generation change. Once logs exist, offline judgments become the instrument for diagnosing a change, and online behaviour becomes the instrument for deciding whether it shipped well.

The BEIR and BRIGHT results are measurements of 2021 and 2024 models on public corpora. The specific gaps will change with every model release; the structural finding, that a ranking on one set of queries does not predict a ranking on another, is the part to carry.
