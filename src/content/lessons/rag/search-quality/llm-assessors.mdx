## The assessor is a labeller you can rerun

A relevance assessor built on an LLM takes a query, a document and a guideline and returns a grade. It runs on every change and a judgment costs a model call; the risk is an instrument wrong in a consistent direction, at scale. Two published studies define what is known. Thomas and colleagues at Bing measured an LLM labeller against trained assessors and against real searchers, and report running LLM labels, with expert labellers alongside, for most of Bing's offline metrics since late 2022. UMBRELA reproduced the method openly with GPT-4o on five years of TREC Deep Learning judgments.

## What the Bing study measured

The TREC experiments used TREC-Robust 2004: 250 topics, labels from trained assessors, and a stratified sample of 1,000 judgments at each of three grades. The model was an in-house GPT-4 at temperature 0, asked for a grade from 0 to 2. The prompt had optional parts: a role ("you are a search quality rater"), the topic's description, its narrative, a request to score two aspects (how well the page matches the intent, how trustworthy it is) before the overall grade, and a request to simulate several judges. Turning those five features on and off gives 32 templates.

Across those templates, Cohen's kappa against TREC assessors ranged from 0.20 to 0.64. The feature effects were not what a prompt engineer would guess. Asking for aspects first added 0.21 to kappa. The narrative added 0.06 and the description 0.01. The role statement cost 0.04, and simulating multiple judges cost 0.13. The best template used description, narrative and aspects.

For calibration, the same paper cites what humans achieve on similar tasks: two groups of trained assessors agreeing at kappa 0.52 on TREC ad hoc documents, and crowd workers between 0.24 and 0.52 on a TREC-Robust re-judging study. On this collection, the best prompts are at or above the human level.

## Paraphrase sensitivity, and the selection rule that handles it

The finding with the most operational weight is the paraphrase study. The authors took the best template and wrote 42 paraphrases of its instructions, keeping the structure fixed. Mean kappa across the paraphrases ranged from 0.50 to 0.72. One of the examples printed in the paper scores 0.64 and its paraphrase 0.72, and nothing in the wording says which will be better. The paper found no consistent or practical effect from prompt or document length.

They then checked whether picking the best variant is safe. Over 1,000 random splits of the 3,000 judgments into halves, the paraphrase that scored best on the first half beat the original template on the second half 989 times. The rule this supports is a train and test split for prompts: generate variants, select on one half of the human-labelled slice, and report agreement on the other half. Selecting and reporting on the same labels overstates what you will see in production.

The variance also tells you what a prompt edit is. A one-sentence change to the guideline is a change to the measuring instrument, and every score computed before and after it is on a different scale until you re-run the agreement check.

## What Bing reports about cost and validity

The web results are summarised in one table and come with the caveat that they depend on Bing's own contracts and training. Relative to typical crowd workers, the LLM labels scored 28% higher on Bing's accuracy measure, against 24% for full-time employees and 19% for the best trained crowd. Relative cost was about 1/20 of the typical crowd's, against about 8 times for employees and 5 times for the best crowd, and throughput about 10 times. Latency fell from hours or days to minutes or hours.

The gold behind "more accurate" is what makes the claim strong. It came from searchers labelling results for their own queries, in context: about 2.5 million preference pairs, across about ten languages and fifty countries. Third-party labels can only show that two labellers disagree; first-party gold can say which one was right. A team with no first-party gold can show agreement with its experts, which is a weaker claim, and should describe it that way.

Bing also runs the assessor as a monitored system. Each week a stratified sample of recent LLM labels is re-labelled by trained assessors and the disagreement rate is tracked; a fixed query set is re-labelled every day and is expected not to change. The authors add two limits worth keeping: labelling web pages with a model trained on web text is, in their words, an easy case, and they have had to build harder gold sets as models improved, because agreement on an easy set saturates.

## What UMBRELA reproduced, and what it did not claim

UMBRELA ran GPT-4o with the same style of prompt on TREC Deep Learning 2019 to 2023, on the four-grade TREC scale. Its code is open (Apache-2.0) and ships judges for several model providers and local models, plus a majority-vote ensemble, which makes it a reasonable starting point for your own assessor rather than a prompt copied from a paper. Label agreement was lower than the Bing paper's TREC-Robust figure: kappa between 0.31 and 0.37 on four grades and between 0.42 and 0.50 binarised, which the authors call fair agreement. Per grade, the model matched humans on roughly 75% of grade-0 documents and on roughly 50%, 30% and 45% of grades 1, 2 and 3.

The headline claim is about system rankings. Scoring the submitted runs by nDCG@10 under human labels and under UMBRELA labels gave Kendall tau between 0.87 and 0.94 and Spearman between 0.97 and 0.99 across the five years. Thomas and colleagues found the same pattern on TREC-Robust, tau from 0.77 to 0.86 over 110 runs, and cite a tau of 0.94 between two sets of human assessors on another collection as a rough ceiling.

These are different properties, and the difference decides where the assessor is safe to use:

| Use | Property it needs | Published evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing among systems that differ substantially | Ranking correlation | Strong: tau 0.77 to 0.94 |
| Deciding between two close systems | Correct order of one pair | Not covered by an aggregate tau |
| Debugging a single query's results | Per-label agreement | Moderate: kappa 0.31 to 0.64 depending on scale and setup |
| Training data for a ranker | Per-label agreement, and errors not aligned with the model being trained | Bing reports better rankers from LLM labels than from crowd labels; no public replication on your domain |

A tau of 0.80 over five systems means one of ten pairs is swapped. If the swapped pair is the two systems you are deciding between, the aggregate is high and the decision is wrong.

## Calibrating your own

The procedure follows directly from the evidence: a human-labelled slice drawn from the same strata as the evaluation, a prompt chosen on half of it and measured on the other half, the confusion matrix read per grade, and a ranking check on the systems you are actually comparing.

```python
from itertools import combinations

def kappa(pairs):
    """Cohen's kappa for (human, model) label pairs."""
    n = len(pairs)
    labels = sorted({h for h, _ in pairs} | {m for _, m in pairs})
    po = sum(h == m for h, m in pairs) / n
    pe = sum((sum(h == c for h, _ in pairs) / n) * (sum(m == c for _, m in pairs) / n) for c in labels)
    return (po - pe) / (1 - pe)

def agreement_when_model_says(pairs, label):
    """How often the human agrees, given the model's label: the number that tells you which labels to trust."""
    sub = [h for h, m in pairs if m == label]
    return sum(h == label for h in sub) / len(sub), len(sub)

def kendall_tau(a, b):
    """Rank correlation of two score lists over the same systems (no ties assumed)."""
    s = sum((1 if (a[i] - a[j]) * (b[i] - b[j]) > 0 else -1) for i, j in combinations(range(len(a)), 2))
    return s / (len(a) * (len(a) - 1) / 2)

# Thomas et al., Table 5, binarised: (TREC label, LLM label), 0 = not relevant, 1 = relevant.
pairs = [(0, 0)] * 866 + [(1, 0)] * 405 + [(0, 1)] * 95 + [(1, 1)] * 1585
print("kappa: %.3f" % kappa(pairs))
print("human agrees when LLM says 0: %.2f of %d" % agreement_when_model_says(pairs, 0))
print("human agrees when LLM says 1: %.2f of %d" % agreement_when_model_says(pairs, 1))

# Scenario: nDCG@10 of five candidate systems under human and LLM labels on the same queries.
human = [0.412, 0.455, 0.471, 0.503, 0.509]
llm   = [0.398, 0.447, 0.469, 0.527, 0.512]
print("kendall tau: %.2f" % kendall_tau(human, llm))
# kappa: 0.644
# human agrees when LLM says 0: 0.68 of 1271
# human agrees when LLM says 1: 0.94 of 1680
# kendall tau: 0.80
```

In the scenario the tau is 0.80 and the one discordant pair is the top two systems. The ranking check that matters is on the pairs you will decide between, with the human labels on enough queries to resolve them.

Three practices follow from what the papers found. Use the assessor to fill holes and to label new systems' unjudged documents, where its volume pays, and keep humans on the slice that sets the scale. Report its agreement per stratum, because a domain the model knows poorly can sit inside a good aggregate. And re-run the agreement check on every model version and every prompt edit, since the paraphrase study shows either can move kappa by more than the effect you are trying to measure.

## When this is wrong

Both studies label general web or web-like passages with models trained on web text. On a corpus of internal engineering documents, contracts or clinical notes, nothing published says the agreement holds, and the only evidence is your own held-out slice.

Agreement with human labels is the measure here, and human labels are themselves noisy: UMBRELA's authors examined DL 2019 documents humans graded 3 and judged several of those grades unwarranted. When the assessor and the humans disagree, the disagreement is a finding to resolve, not automatically a model error. Without first-party gold, which most teams lack, you cannot say which side is right in general.

The models here are 2023 and 2024 GPT-4 variants. The kappas and taus will move with each model generation, and the paraphrase result says they move within one model too; the method is what carries over.
