## The mechanism

Every vector quantisation scheme in production is a lossy index followed by a recheck, the same shape as a Postgres GiST index on bounding boxes that returns candidates and lets the executor recheck the real geometry. The compressed codes decide which candidates are worth looking at; a second pass over better vectors decides the order. The difference from the GiST case is that the recheck is optional and approximate in both stages, so recall depends on two numbers you choose: the code size, which sets how good the shortlist is, and the shortlist length, which sets how much of the first stage's error the second can repair.

The Faiss paper states the rule for the second stage plainly: the first-level index should be judged by recall at the shortlist size, not at k. A binary index whose recall@10 is poor can still have excellent recall@100, and then a verification pass over 100 full vectors returns a correct top 10.

## Four codes

**Scalar int8.** Each dimension is mapped to 256 levels using a per-dimension minimum and maximum taken from a calibration set, one byte per dimension, four times smaller than float32. In Hugging Face's measurement with mixedbread's 1,024-dimension model on MTEB retrieval, int8 kept about 97% of float32 NDCG@10 without rescoring and about 99.3% with a rescore multiplier of 4 to 5. The calibration set is the failure point: a shift in the embedding distribution after calibration clips values silently.

**Binary.** Each dimension becomes one bit, its sign after normalisation, 32 times smaller; 1,024 dimensions pack into 128 bytes, and distance is Hamming, an XOR and a popcount. The same measurement kept about 92.53% of NDCG@10 without rescoring and 96.45% with it, and the authors report that multipliers from 1 to 10 gave no further gain for that model. Their rescoring step scores the shortlist with the float32 query against the binary document codes, an asymmetric pass that needs no full document vectors at all. In exact CPU search they measured binary at 24.76 times float32's speed on average and int8 at 3.66 times. Hugging Face sized 250M such embeddings at 953.67 GB as float32, 238.41 GB as int8 and 29.80 GB as binary (the figures are GiB).

**Product quantisation.** Split each vector into m sub-vectors and replace each with the id of the nearest of k* centroids learned for that slice; the code is m x log2(k*) bits. The PQ paper calls k* = 256 with m = 8 (64-bit codes) often a reasonable choice. Search uses asymmetric distance computation: before scanning, compute the squared distance from each of the query's m sub-vectors to each of the k* centroids of its slice, a table of m x k* numbers; the distance to any database code is then m table lookups and adds. The paper's verdict on the symmetric variant, which also encodes the query, is that its only advantage is saving query memory, and that the asymmetric version has lower distortion at similar cost. Faiss uses asymmetric distances in most of its indexes.

**RaBitQ.** A 2024 SIGMOD paper that keeps the bit budget of binary codes and adds a guarantee. Each vector is normalised against a centroid (in IVF-RaBitQ, its cluster's centroid), rotated by a random orthogonal matrix, and stored as D bits for D dimensions. The paper proves an unbiased estimator of the inner product whose error is O(1/sqrt(D)) with high probability, quantises the query to 4 bits so its error is negligible, and computes distances with AND and popcount. The bound changes the verification pass: instead of a tuned shortlist length, a candidate is dropped when its distance lower bound already exceeds the exact distance of the best result so far, and every other candidate is checked. The authors' case against PQ is that it has no such bound and fails badly on some data: on the MSong dataset they report PQ with over 50% average relative distance error and no more than 60% recall even with re-ranking.

## In Postgres

pgvector exposes two of these as expression indexes on an ordinary `vector` column, since 0.7.0 (April 2024). The binary form indexes `binary_quantize(embedding)::bit(n)` with `bit_hamming_ops` and verifies with a subquery that takes a Hamming shortlist and re-orders it by full-vector distance; the README's example takes 20 by Hamming and returns 5:

```sql
CREATE INDEX ON items USING hnsw ((binary_quantize(embedding)::bit(1024)) bit_hamming_ops);

SELECT * FROM (
    SELECT * FROM items
    ORDER BY binary_quantize(embedding)::bit(1024) <~> binary_quantize($1)
    LIMIT 40
) ORDER BY embedding <=> $1 LIMIT 10;
```

The full vectors stay in the table, so the verification pass is 40 heap fetches, each carrying a 4,104-byte value (4 x 1,024 + 8) for a 1,024-dimension `vector`. That is the cost the RAM budget moved: off the index, onto random reads of the table. The half-precision form indexes `embedding::halfvec(n)`, which halves the index and needs no recheck beyond what HNSW already does.

## Choosing a format

The decision procedure, in order:

1. **Budget.** RAM per vector, as in the prediction. It rules formats out before recall is measured.
2. **Recall target and where the verification vectors live.** If full or int8 vectors fit in RAM, the verification pass is cheap and a long shortlist costs little. If they live on SSD, each shortlist entry is a random read, and the shortlist length times queries per second is an IOPS budget. Hugging Face's 41M-document pipeline is the worked example: a 5.2 GB binary index in memory, the top 40 rescored from a 47.5 GB int8 index on disk with no memory reserved for it, against roughly 200 GB of memory for float32.
3. **The model.** Retention figures are per model. Measure recall@10 against float32 exact search on your labelled queries for each candidate format, at shortlist lengths of 2, 4 and 10 times k, and choose the smallest code and shortest shortlist that meet the target.
4. **Guarantee.** If the data includes outlier distributions where a codebook can be badly wrong (the MSong case), prefer a scheme with an error bound, or a longer verification pass.

The toy below shows why step 3 is a measurement. It is 2,000 normalised 256-dimension vectors in 20 tight clusters, sign-quantised, with and without a verification pass.

```python
import math
import random

random.seed(3)
D, N, K = 256, 2000, 10


def unit(v):
    n = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in v))
    return [x / n for x in v]


centres = [[random.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(D)] for _ in range(20)]
docs = [unit([c + random.gauss(0, 0.6) for c in random.choice(centres)]) for _ in range(N)]
queries = [unit([c + random.gauss(0, 0.6) for c in random.choice(centres)]) for _ in range(20)]
codes = [sum(1 << i for i, x in enumerate(d) if x > 0) for d in docs]  # D floats become D bits


def dot(a, b):
    return sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))


def search(q, multiplier):
    qb = sum(1 << i for i, x in enumerate(q) if x > 0)
    shortlist = sorted(range(N), key=lambda i: (qb ^ codes[i]).bit_count())[: K * multiplier]
    if multiplier == 1:
        return shortlist
    return sorted(shortlist, key=lambda i: -dot(q, docs[i]))[:K]  # verification on full vectors


exact = [set(sorted(range(N), key=lambda i: -dot(q, docs[i]))[:K]) for q in queries]
for multiplier in (1, 4, 10):
    hits = sum(len(e & set(search(q, multiplier))) for q, e in zip(queries, exact))
    read = 0 if multiplier == 1 else K * multiplier
    print(f"multiplier {multiplier:>2}: recall@10 {hits / (K * len(queries)):.2f}, full vectors read {read}")
# multiplier  1: recall@10 0.32, full vectors read 0
# multiplier  4: recall@10 0.72, full vectors read 40
# multiplier 10: recall@10 1.00, full vectors read 100
```

On this data the multiplier is everything: 0.32 without verification, 0.72 at 4 and 1.00 at 10. Hugging Face found no gain beyond a multiplier of 1 for their model. Both are true for their data; the clusters here are tight enough that sign bits cannot order points inside a cluster, and a real model's embeddings may or may not look like that. The only way to know is the sweep in step 3.

## When this is wrong

Recall against float32 exact search is the metric here, and it is not relevance. A quantised index can lose 3% of NDCG on a benchmark and nothing a user notices, or the reverse on a corpus of near-duplicates where the top 10 are separated by tiny distances.

The retention figures are Hugging Face's, for one model on public retrieval tasks, and their rescoring used binary document codes rather than full vectors, so it is not the same pass as pgvector's re-rank. RaBitQ's bound is on the distance estimate, not on recall; recall still depends on the index structure the codes sit in. PQ codebooks and int8 ranges are trained on data, so they degrade when the data drifts, while sign bits do not need training but lose information when the embeddings are not centred on the origin; RaBitQ normalises against a centroid before taking bits. The decision also moves with hardware: when vectors are on SSD, the verification pass is an I/O budget, and the disk-resident designs in the next lesson and the teardown are built around keeping it to a few reads.
