## The first stage that lost the evidence

A RAG answer passes through at least five transformations: documents become chunks, the user's turn becomes a query, the query becomes a ranked list, the ranked list becomes a prompt, and the prompt becomes an answer. A wrong answer is evidence that some stage dropped or corrupted the facts the answer needed. The useful question is which stage did it first, because every later stage was working from bad input and any fix applied there treats a symptom.

That is a bisection over the pipeline, the same move as `git bisect` over commits or walking a distributed trace hop by hop. The difference is that RAG stages are not independent. A strong generator can answer correctly from its own weights after retrieval missed, which hides a retrieval defect until a question arrives about a document the model never saw in training. A reranker can recover a chunk that first-stage retrieval ranked at 40, or bury one ranked at 2. So the bisection works on the evidence, the gold chunk ids for the question, and asks at each stage whether they are still present, rather than asking whether the final answer looks right.

## The taxonomy

Barnett et al. (2024) named seven failure points from three systems: a research assistant over uploaded papers, a course tutor over video transcripts, HTML and PDF, and a biomedical question-answering run over 4,017 open-access documents with 1,000 expert-written questions from BioASQ. The names are the closest thing the field has to a shared vocabulary, so use them in design reviews and incident notes.

Two stages from this lesson's title are not rows in the table. Chunking failures land in FP1 or FP2 depending on what went wrong: a parser that dropped a table makes the answer missing from the index even though it was in the source, and a chunk boundary that separated a value from its header makes the right chunk unretrievable for any query that names the header. Check the index, not the source documents, before calling something Missing Content. Query failures land in FP2: in a multi-turn assistant the query sent to retrieval is often a rewrite of the user's turn, and a rewrite that dropped the entity name produces a clean, confident miss. Log the query as sent, not the user's text.

Read the paper's evidence base for what it is. The authors inspected 40 issues by hand plus every item their automated evaluator flagged, and they report that the automated evaluation was more pessimistic than a human rater. The introduction gives the BioASQ corpus as 15,000 documents while the case-study table gives 4,017, and the paper does not reconcile them. Its lessons-learned table is experience from three systems, including that an 8K context beat 4K in the tutor and that adding the file name and chunk number to each chunk helped. Its central claim is the operational one: robustness is found at runtime, because the inputs that break a RAG system are the ones nobody wrote a test for.

## What the trace has to hold

Bisection needs a recorded value at every stage boundary. Per request, log:

- the index version or snapshot id, so "was the gold chunk indexed" has an answer for the state the request saw rather than today's index;
- the query as sent to each retriever, after any rewriting;
- the ranked ids and scores from each first-stage retriever and from fusion;
- the ids after reranking, deduplication and any score threshold;
- the ids actually rendered into the prompt, with their positions and token counts, after the token budget cut;
- the answer, its citations, and the model and prompt version.

The gap between "retrieved" and "in context" is where most of the hidden defects sit. Rerankers with an input token limit, deduplication keyed on the wrong field, a token budget that truncates the last chunk, and prompt templates that sort chunks by document date all live there. A dashboard that reports recall@k on the retriever's output says nothing about them. Recall measured on the rendered prompt does.

## The triage function

Given gold ids for each eval question and the trace above, the first failing stage is a four-line function. It labels the first upstream loss even when the answer was correct: a correct answer with no gold chunk in the prompt came from the model's weights, and that path fails on private documents.

Run it over every failed question and count the labels, and the counts are a ranked bug list. The "not extracted" bucket then splits further by hand into FP4 to FP7, which is the part a function cannot do. With the trace in place, a reviewer has about three minutes per item to classify twenty failures in an hour (60 / 20 = 3), which is enough to read the question, the gold chunk, the rendered context ids and the answer. Without the trace it is not: reconstructing what the prompt held means rerunning the request, and the index may have changed since. The [RAG pipeline lab](/labs/rag-pipeline) builds a pipeline whose stages log these boundaries and ends with a broken stage that the trace has to name.

## Faithfulness tells you which side of the prompt went wrong

Answer correctness alone cannot separate a generator that invented a claim from a generator that faithfully repeated a wrong chunk. Faithfulness can. Ragas defines it as the number of claims in the answer supported by the retrieved context divided by the total number of claims, from 0 to 1; its documentation works an example in which an answer gives a correct birthplace and a wrong date, one of two claims is supported, and the score is 0.5. Claim extraction and verification are model calls by default, and the `FaithfulnesswithHHEM` variant replaces the verifier with Vectara's HHEM-2.1-Open, a T5 classifier. Crossing correctness with faithfulness gives four cells, each pointing at a different stage:

<tbody>
<tr><td>Correct</td><td>Working</td><td>Answered from weights; retrieval or assembly lost the evidence and the model covered for it</td></tr>
<tr><td>Wrong</td><td>The context was wrong: a stale, superseded or conflicting chunk won retrieval; look at ingestion and freshness</td><td>Generation added or distorted claims; FP4 territory</td></tr>

The bottom-left cell is the expensive one to miss. A faithful wrong answer looks like a model problem in a review of transcripts and is a data problem: last year's policy is still indexed, and the generator did its job. Raising the faithfulness score cannot fix it. Two cautions apply to the metric itself. The verifier is a model with its own error rate, so hand-label a sample of its verdicts and report agreement beside the score. And pin the library version in the harness: the Ragas page states that the legacy metrics API will be deprecated in 0.4 and removed in 1.0, so an unpinned upgrade changes what the number means.

The "worse after raising k" row has a measurement behind it. Chroma's 2025 context-rot report tested 18 models and found that a single distractor reduced performance relative to the needle-only baseline, and four distractors compounded the drop. Raising k trades FP2 failures for FP4 failures, and only the per-stage counts say whether the trade paid. The "right documents, wrong answer" row is where the [oracle-context ablation](/learn/rag/debugging-rag/oracle-context) starts: replace the context with the gold chunks and see whether generation succeeds at all.

## When this is wrong

The model assumes a linear pipeline with one retrieval per answer. Agentic systems retrieve several times, rewrite their own queries and decide when to stop, so the first failing stage can be a planning decision; trace each retrieval call as its own span and triage per call.

It assumes a small gold set exists. Questions whose answer is an aggregation across many documents ("which teams changed on-call policy this year") have no handful of gold chunks, and global questions may have no single right answer. There, coverage and faithfulness carry more weight than recall, and the triage function returns noise.

It assumes the labels are current. Gold chunk ids are tied to a chunking and an index version; change the chunker and every label needs remapping, and documents that changed since labelling turn correct answers into apparent failures. Version the eval set with the index.

It assumes stages fail one at a time. Fixing the first failing stage can expose the next: raising recall puts more distractors in the prompt, and the "not extracted" count rises. Rerun the full triage after each fix rather than tracking one bucket.

And the taxonomy itself comes from three systems and one paper, with a hand inspection of 40 issues. It names failure modes well; it says nothing about how often each occurs in your system, which only your own counts can.
