## The brief and what it leaves open

A techinterview.org article from July 2026 reports a take-home in which the candidate gets 50 Markdown documents and is asked for a Q&A system, an eval set of 20 questions, and reported recall@5 and faithfulness. The article names no company for it and states no time limit. It describes a second variant in the same piece, in which the candidate calibrates an automated faithfulness and relevance judge against provided human labels; the lesson below covers the first, and its judge section applies to both.

The build is small. What the brief leaves open is larger: what unit recall counts (chunks or documents), whether "recall" means recall or hit rate, which judge produces faithfulness and how anyone knows it is right, what the 20 questions cover, and whether 50 documents need retrieval at all. Each of those is a decision the submission has to state and defend, and the README and eval report are where a reviewer finds them. This lesson works the take-home under a three-hour budget, which is a scope choice, not something the brief imposes.

The blocks sum to 180 minutes. The eval set gets the largest block because every number in the report inherits its flaws.

## Does this corpus need retrieval?

Count the tokens before building anything. Anthropic's contextual-retrieval post suggests that a knowledge base under 200,000 tokens, about 500 pages, can go straight into the prompt. Fifty documents fit under that if they average under 200,000 / 50 = 4,000 tokens each. The brief asks for recall@5, so the submission builds retrieval regardless, but a corpus that fits makes the long-context run the honest baseline: answer the 20 questions with the whole corpus in the prompt and report its correctness next to the RAG pipeline's. If the long-context run wins, the README says so and says what RAG buys instead (cost per query, latency, a corpus that will grow). A submission that never mentions the comparison leaves the most obvious reviewer question unanswered.

## The eval set is the deliverable

Twenty questions is enough to find failures and not enough to rank close variants, so compose the set for coverage rather than for a representative score. One defensible split, and the stage each slice exercises:

- 8 single-passage factual questions, the baseline;
- 4 exact-identifier questions (a config key, an error code, a version string), which test whether lexical matching is in the pipeline;
- 4 multi-document questions with two gold documents each, the only questions where recall and hit rate differ;
- 2 questions where two documents disagree or one supersedes the other, which test whether the faithful answer is also the correct one;
- 2 questions the corpus cannot answer, which test Missing Content behaviour (Barnett et al.'s FP1): the right output is a refusal.

Write each question in your own words after reading the document, not by lifting a sentence from it. A question that copies the passage's wording is answered by lexical overlap, and an eval set generated from the documents by a model has the same flaw at scale. Record gold at two levels: document ids, which survive a change of chunker, and chunk ids, which the triage function needs. Unanswerable questions have no gold set, so recall is undefined for them; exclude them from the recall mean, say so, and report refusal accuracy on its own.

## recall@5, defined in the report

Per answerable question, recall@5 is the share of its gold documents that appear in the top 5, and the reported number is the mean over those questions. Hit rate@5 counts a question as found if any gold document appears. The two are identical on single-gold questions, so an eval set without multi-document questions cannot tell them apart, and a submission that reports "recall@5" computed as hit rate is wrong only where it matters. A question with two gold documents and one retrieved scores 0.5 recall and 1.0 hit.

State the unit. If the retriever returns chunks, either take the top 5 chunks and map them to documents (two chunks from one document then use two of the five slots) or deduplicate to the top 5 distinct documents (which shows the generator more than five chunks). Both are defensible; the report names one. State the floor as well: at document level a random ranking puts a given document in the top 5 with probability 5 / 50 = 0.10, which is the baseline any retriever's number is read against.

## Faithfulness, with its judge calibrated

Faithfulness is supported claims over total claims, judged against the context the generator actually received, which is the top 5 rendered into the prompt, not everything retrieved. The Ragas paper introduced the metric and reported, on its WikiEval set of 50 questions from 50 Wikipedia pages, agreement with human judgments of 0.95 for faithfulness against 0.72 and 0.54 for two simpler judge prompts, with gpt-3.5-turbo-16k as the judge. The same table shows 0.78 for answer relevance and 0.70 for context relevance. Those numbers describe one judge model from 2023 on 50 questions; they do not certify whichever judge the submission uses.

With 20 questions the calibration is cheap enough to do completely: label every extracted claim by hand as supported or not, run the judge, and report how many claims the two agree on and which ones they do not. Report correctness against the reference answers separately, because a faithful answer to the superseded document is faithfully wrong. Pin the library and judge model versions; the Ragas documentation states that its legacy metrics API will be deprecated in 0.4 and removed in 1.0, so an unpinned install changes the metric under the report.

The [Score the take-home](/problems/rag-015) problem builds this scoring harness against a fixed run, including the edge cases (empty gold sets, answers with no claims) that the report has to decide.

## A pipeline small enough to defend

Chunk along Markdown headings and prefix each chunk with its document title and heading path before indexing. That is the cheap form of contextual retrieval, and Barnett et al. report that adding the file name and chunk number to chunks helped in one of their systems. Index with BM25 and with embeddings, and fuse the two rankings with reciprocal rank fusion, summing 1 / (k + rank) across rankings. Cormack et al. fixed k = 60 in a pilot and found the choice near-optimal but not critical: their pilot MAP moved from 0.2072 at k = 0 to 0.2147 at k = 80. Put the top 5 in a prompt that requires a chunk id for every claim and permits "the documents do not say".

Leave out the reranker, query rewriting and anything agentic unless the per-question results show a failure one of them fixes. At 8.9 points of standard error, no added component can be shown to help on 20 questions unless it flips several of them, and a component without evidence is a liability in the review. Run three retrieval variants (BM25 only, embeddings only, fused) because they cost minutes and the per-question differences show which questions need which.

## The eval report

The report is one page and leads with the numbers and their size:

```text
recall@5 (document level, mean over 18 answerable): 0.83   hit rate@5: 0.89
faithfulness (judge, against top-5 context): 0.91   judge agreed with hand labels on 37 of 40 claims
correctness vs reference: 15 of 20   refusals correct: 2 of 2
one question = 5.6 points of recall; differences under two questions are noise

per question: id | slice | recall@5 | faithful | correct | first failing stage
variants: BM25 / dense / fused, per-question flips
oracle context on the 5 incorrect: 3 pass (retrieval or assembly), 2 fail (generation)
cut: reranker, query rewriting, incremental indexing and deletes, permissions, conversation
next: the one change with the most failed questions behind it, and the rerun that confirms it
```

The figures in the block are an illustration of the format, not results. The per-question table and the triage column carry the review: they show the candidate knows where each failure starts, using the [failure taxonomy](/learn/rag/debugging-rag/failure-taxonomy) and the [oracle ablation](/learn/rag/debugging-rag/oracle-context) from earlier in this module.

## Five submissions, critiqued

These are composites built to show the reviewing logic, not reports of real candidates.

**The generated eval set.** A model wrote 20 questions from the documents; recall@5 is 1.0 and faithfulness 0.97. The questions paraphrase the passages they came from, none spans two documents and none is unanswerable, so the numbers measure lexical overlap between a question and its source. Nothing in the submission is wrong, and nothing in it was tested.

**Hit rate reported as recall.** Every question has one gold document, so the two metrics agree and the submission never had to choose. The reviewer's note is that the eval set cannot detect the multi-document failures a real corpus produces.

**Faithfulness from defaults.** An unpinned library, its default judge, scored against all 20 retrieved chunks rather than the 5 in the prompt, with no hand check. The score is higher than it should be, because claims supported by chunks the generator never saw count as supported, and nobody can say how often the judge is wrong.

**The full stack.** Hybrid search, a reranker, query rewriting, hypothetical-document expansion and an agent loop, reported as one aggregate number. With 20 questions, no component's contribution can be separated from noise, and the README cannot say which part to remove if latency matters. The reviewer reads this as scope chosen for appearance.

**BM25 only, done carefully.** One retriever, a well-composed eval set, the triage table, and a README that lists which failed questions dense retrieval would likely fix and how the candidate would confirm it. Acceptable if the triage shows the BM25 failures by stage and the cut is stated as a decision; weak if dense retrieval was dropped for time and the README does not say so.

## What was cut, in writing

The README lists each cut with the measurement that would justify adding it back: a reranker when the oracle runs show distraction loss; query rewriting when conversational questions enter the set; incremental indexing and deletes when documents change; permission filtering when documents have owners; a second judge when the hand-label agreement is low; more questions when two variants differ by fewer than a few flips. The [enterprise wiki design problem](/problems/rag-016) is what several of those cuts turn into at scale.

## When this is wrong

The take-home here comes from one article that names no company and no time limit. Briefs vary: some provide the eval set, some ask for a deployed application, some give a judge to calibrate. The brief named two metrics, so this lesson treats the evaluation as the product; a brief that asks for a working interface changes what gets cut.

The noise arithmetic assumes questions are independent trials with one difficulty. They are not: the composition was chosen for coverage, so the mean over 20 is not an estimate of anything in production. The per-question table is the result; the means are summaries of it.

The judge agreement figures are from a 2023 paper with a 2023 judge on 50 questions. A current judge may agree with humans more or less, and on Markdown technical documents with code and configuration the claim extraction step behaves differently from Wikipedia prose. The hand calibration on your own 20 questions is the only figure that applies to your submission.

And the long-context comparison can decide the scope. If 50 documents fit in a prompt with room to spare, retrieval is a requirement of the brief rather than of the problem, and the README should say which of the two it is answering.
