## The decision

Retrieval underperforms on a domain, and three levers compete for the same quarter: fine-tune the embedder, add or fine-tune a reranker, or change how the corpus is chunked. They fix different failures. A reranker reorders candidates the retriever already found and cannot recover a passage outside them. A fine-tuned embedder changes which passages are found, and forces a re-embed of the whole index. Re-chunking changes what a passage is, and also forces a re-embed, without any training. Picking by habit spends the budget on a lever that cannot touch the failure.

## The inputs that change it

**The failure signature.** On a held-out set of labelled queries, compute recall@10 and recall@100 for the current retriever. A large gap between them says the right passage is usually found and ranked too low. Low recall@100 says it is not found. Then read 50 failures and tag each: domain vocabulary the model does not know, an answer split across two chunks, an answer buried in a long chunk, a query that needs reasoning before it matches anything, a negation or exclusion.

**Labelled pairs.** Rerankers and embedders both train on pairs; chunking needs none. The reranker write-up trained on 99,000 pairs, a small slice of a 3-million-pair dataset. A few thousand pairs is enough to start either, provided the mined negatives are audited.

**The latency budget.** A cross-encoder reranker scores every candidate per query, so its cost grows linearly in the candidate count. A fine-tuned embedder costs nothing extra per query if its size does not change.

**How often the corpus changes.** Every lever except the reranker means re-embedding the corpus. That is a one-time migration, plus the same work on every future model change. A reranker is the only lever whose deployment does not touch the index.

## The options and when each wins

| Failure signature | Lever | What it costs | Why it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Right passage in the top 100, not the top 10 | Reranker, off the shelf or fine-tuned | One cross-encoder pass per candidate per query; pairs to fine-tune | It reorders candidates and reads query and passage together |
| Right passage absent; failures on in-house vocabulary | Fine-tune the embedder (or add lexical search) | Pairs, a mining audit, a full re-embed and migration | Only the retriever decides what enters the candidate set |
| Answers split across chunks or buried in long ones | Change the chunks | Re-chunk and re-embed; no training | No model can match a passage that does not contain the answer |
| Queries need reasoning before they match | Rewrite the query before retrieval | An LLM call per query | BRIGHT reports up to 12.2 points from reasoning about the query |
| Negation and exclusion queries | Cross-encoder reranker | Same as the reranker row | NevIR ranks cross-encoders best on negation and bi-encoders worst |

The reranker row has the best published evidence for a small domain model. In the Sentence Transformers write-up, a 150M-parameter ModernBERT reranker, fine-tuned on 99,000 pairs in about 30 minutes on one RTX 3090, reranked the top 30 of a retriever on its own domain (GooAQ) to 77.14 NDCG@10. The retriever alone scored 59.12; bge-reranker-v2-m3 at 568M parameters scored 73.56, and mxbai-rerank-large-v2 at 1.54B scored 75.40. The author notes the result holds for that domain, not all domains.

The same table measures what retrieval misses cost. When all positives were added to the top 30, the fine-tuned reranker reached 83.51. The gap, 83.51 minus 77.14 = 6.37 points, is the quality the reranker could not reach because the retriever never handed it the passage. That gap is the case for working on recall as well.

## A worked decision

The case, with scenario numbers: an internal engineering-docs search over 1.2 million chunks. 2,500 labelled query and passage pairs exist from a past annotation project. The search endpoint has a 250 ms p95 budget, and retrieval uses 60 ms of it today. About 2% of documents change each day. Peak traffic is 40 queries per second.

Held-out measurement on 500 queries: recall@10 is 0.61, recall@100 is 0.90. Of 50 read failures, 31 have the right passage between ranks 11 and 100, 12 are answers split across two chunks of a long runbook, and 7 use internal service names the model treats as noise.

The signature points at ordering first: 0.90 at 100 against 0.61 at 10. A reranker over the top 50 is the first lever. The cost is a cross-encoder pass per candidate.

Second, the 12 split answers are a chunking defect. No reranker can fix them, because neither chunk contains the whole answer. Re-chunking the runbooks along their section structure is a re-embed of part of the corpus, with no training. Third, the 7 vocabulary failures are the only case for a fine-tuned embedder, and 7 of 50 does not justify a full re-embed of 1.2 million chunks, two indexes during migration and the mining audit. Adding lexical search for exact service names is the cheaper fix for that slice.

The decision: an off-the-shelf reranker over the top 50 now, fine-tuned on the 2,500 pairs once it proves out; re-chunk the long runbooks; hybrid lexical search for service names. Revisit the embedder fine-tune if, after both changes, recall@100 is the binding limit.

What this gives up: the reranker adds a model to the serving path with its own latency and failure modes, and the 2% daily churn means the re-chunked runbooks need the new chunker in the ingest path, not as a one-off.

## What to measure after deciding

- **Recall@k on held-out queries,** split by query rather than by passage, and never used for mining or training. Report recall@10 and recall@100 separately, because they answer different questions about the levers.
- **The false-negative rate of mined pairs,** if any lever involves training. Sample 200 mined negatives stratified by rank, two judges, a written rule. If 30 of 200 are false, the rate is 15%, and the 95% interval is 1.96 × √(0.15 × 0.85 / 200) ≈ ±4.9 points. NV-Retriever measured 38.8% and 47% with naive top-k mining on public data, so do not assume yours is low.
- **Per-slice recall,** for the failure tags you started with. A change that lifts the average and leaves the split-answer slice at zero has not fixed that slice.
- **Latency at peak,** for the reranker at the chosen candidate count, measured on the serving hardware.

## When this is wrong

The table assumes the three levers are independent. They interact: a fine-tuned embedder changes the candidates a reranker sees, so a reranker tuned on the old candidates should be re-evaluated after any retriever change.

The reranker evidence is one write-up on one domain, with an in-domain training set far larger than most teams have. With a few hundred pairs, an off-the-shelf reranker may beat a fine-tuned one. Measure both.

The failure tags are a reading of 50 examples by one person. With a small sample, a slice of 7 failures can be 3 or 12 next time; treat the tags as a direction, and re-measure after the first lever ships.

Finally, retrieval may not be the problem. If the generator ignores a correctly retrieved passage, none of these levers helps; check answer quality on queries where retrieval succeeded before spending the quarter on retrieval.
