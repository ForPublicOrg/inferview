## Four settings, one decision

An embedding index has four settings that teams tend to decide in different meetings: the similarity metric, whether vectors are normalised, how many dimensions are kept, and how each value is stored. They are one decision. Each trades retrieval quality against bytes per vector, and the settings interact: truncation breaks normalisation, a broken normalisation changes what the metric ranks, and quantisation compounds whatever truncation lost. This lesson costs an index from published dimension and quantisation results, then covers the operation that most often goes wrong, changing the model.

## Metric and normalisation

If every vector has length 1, cosine equals the dot product and squared L2 distance is 2 minus 2 times the cosine, so all three rank identically. OpenAI states that its embeddings are normalised to length 1, and its guide says cosine and Euclidean distance give identical rankings. The dot product is then the cheap choice, because it skips two norms per comparison.

The condition is what breaks in production. A dot product scales with each document's length, so any writer that stores unnormalised vectors gives those documents a ranking bonus unrelated to relevance. The common way to get there is Matryoshka truncation: the first k values of a unit vector are shorter than 1, by an amount that differs per vector.

```python
import math

<Callout kind="aside" label="Attempt first">
Problem [fnd-023](/problems/fnd-023), recall that fell after a fine-tune got harder negatives, is this module's opening problem. Try it before reading on; the lesson explains how metric, normalisation and model version decide which vectors can share an index.
</Callout>

def norm(a): return math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in a))
def normalize(a):
    n = norm(a)
    return [x / n for x in a]

full = normalize([0.5, -0.2, 0.7, 0.1, 0.05, -0.3, 0.2, 0.1])
cut = full[:4]
print(round(norm(cut), 3), round(norm(normalize(cut)), 3))   # 0.92 1.0
```

Under a dot product, that document now scores 0.92 times what it should against every query, and another document truncated from a different vector may keep 0.98. OpenAI's guide says to re-normalise after shortening by hand, the Matryoshka write-up on Hugging Face says the same, EmbeddingGemma's card says to re-normalise after truncating, gemini-embedding-001 needs manual normalisation below 3,072 dimensions, and gemini-embedding-2 does it for you. Cosine as the index metric hides the bug, which is a reason to choose it when more than one writer feeds the index. An ingest assertion that every stored norm is 1 within a tolerance catches it either way.

## Costing an index

Raw storage is vectors times dimensions times bytes per value. Binary stores one bit per dimension, so a 1,024-dimension vector packs into 1,024 / 8 = 128 bytes.

<p>100,000,000 × 128 bytes = 12,800,000,000 bytes, or <b>12.8 GB</b>. The same vectors in float32 are 100,000,000 × 1,024 × 4 = 409.6 GB, and in int8 102.4 GB. The ratio between float32 and binary is 32, which is the factor the quantisation write-up reports.</p>

## Recall per byte, from published numbers

Three levers reduce bytes per vector. The published evidence for each comes with conditions that decide how far it transfers.

| Lever | Bytes per vector vs float32 | Published quality | Measured on |
|---|---|---|---|
| int8 | 4x smaller | 97% of float32 NDCG@10, about 99% with rescoring | mxbai-embed-large-v1, MTEB retrieval |
| binary | 32x smaller | 92.53% without rescoring, 96.45% with | same |
| Matryoshka truncation, 768 to 64 dims | 12x smaller | 98.37% of the Spearman score (96.46% without Matryoshka training) | mpnet-base NLI model, STSBenchmark |
| `dimensions=256` on text-embedding-3-large | 12x smaller than its 3,072 | still above ada-002 at 1,536 on MTEB | vendor's own claim |

The Matryoshka paper's headline, embeddings up to 14 times smaller at the same accuracy, is ImageNet-1K classification. None of these rows is recall@10 on your corpus, and none measures truncation and quantisation together. The ratios in the first column are exact; the quality column is a prior for the experiment, not a substitute for it.

Rescoring is what makes binary usable. The search runs over packed bits with Hamming distance, takes a shortlist several times larger than k, then rescores the shortlist against a higher-precision copy and keeps the top k. The write-up's main runs used a rescore multiplier of 4, and its demo over 41 million Wikipedia texts kept a 5.2 GB binary index in memory, against about 200 GB for float32, with the int8 rescoring copy (47.5 GB) on disk. On exact CPU search it measured a mean speed-up of 24.76x for binary and 3.66x for int8. The architecture is two tiers: bits in RAM, bytes on disk, and a disk read per shortlisted candidate.

Two operational details decide whether these numbers hold. Binary thresholds each dimension at 0, which assumes the model's values are roughly centred; check the sign balance per dimension on a sample. int8 maps each dimension to 256 levels using ranges from a calibration set, and the write-up notes that the calibration data strongly affects the result. Calibrate on documents from the corpus, and recalibrate when the corpus shifts.

Instruction-aware models change the query path. EmbeddingGemma's card gives a task prefix for queries, and Qwen3-Embedding's card reports that instructions typically improve results by 1% to 5%, the vendor's own figure. A query path that omits the prefix runs without error and retrieves worse. Benchmark averages are a shortlist: the E5 paper reports a model beating ones with 40 times more parameters on MTEB, and your corpus can reorder any leaderboard.

## Why two models cannot share an index

Two embedding models, even with the same dimension count, learn unrelated coordinate systems; dimension 17 of one has no relationship to dimension 17 of the other. A query embedded with model B and scored against documents embedded with model A returns a ranking with scores in the usual range, and it means nothing. The same holds, less visibly, for one model with a different query prefix, truncation length or normalisation step. Matching dimensions are what let the mistake ship, because the index accepts the writes.

The prevention is metadata: store the model id, version, dimension and preprocessing settings with every vector, and include the model id in any query-embedding cache key. A mixed index then shows up in a query, not as a drop in recall a week later.

## Migration: re-embed everything, or dual-write behind a cut-over

A model change is a migration between two spaces, and the cost is mostly not the embedding calls.

<Predict id="emb-ops-reembed-cost" question="Re-embedding 50 million chunks of 400 tokens each at $0.02 per million tokens costs how many dollars?" answer={400} tolerance={20} unit="$">
<p>50,000,000 × 400 = 20 billion tokens, which is 20,000 million tokens × $0.02 = <b>$400</b> at list price. A second float32 copy of 50 million 1,536-dimension vectors is 50,000,000 × 1,536 × 4 = 307.2 GB of RAM for as long as both indexes live, which usually costs more than the embedding calls.</p>
</Predict>

Two plans are defensible.

**Rebuild and swap.** Embed the whole corpus with the new model into a new index from a snapshot, replay changes made since the snapshot, evaluate, and switch reads. It is the simpler system: one write path at a time and a short window with two indexes. The cost is the catch-up step, which needs a reliable change log, and a rollback that means switching back to an index that stopped receiving writes at the swap.

**Dual-write behind a cut-over.** From the start of the backfill, every new or changed document is written to both indexes; the backfill fills the new one; reads move by flag, a share of traffic at a time; the old index keeps receiving writes until the rollback window closes. Rollback is a flag flip to a current index. The cost is two write paths and two indexes for the whole window, and one race: a document updated during the backfill can be overwritten in the new index by the backfill's older copy, so the backfill must skip documents whose new-index copy is newer.

Rebuild and swap fits a corpus that re-embeds in hours and changes slowly. Dual-write fits a corpus that takes days, changes constantly, or serves traffic that cannot tolerate a rollback to stale data. A third option, querying both indexes and merging, needs two query embeddings per request and a rank-based merge, because scores from two spaces are not comparable. It is a stopgap during a backfill, not a design.

## When this is wrong

The recall-per-byte table is a prior. Each row was measured on one model and one benchmark, and none of them is recall@10 on a production corpus with its own query mix. A model whose values are not centred loses more under binary quantisation; a model trained without a Matryoshka loss loses more under truncation, as the comparison in the Matryoshka write-up shows. Measure every combination you intend to ship.

The storage arithmetic counts raw vectors. In graph indexes the graph can be a large share of memory at low dimensions, so cutting bytes per vector from 128 to 64 may move the total far less than the vector term suggests.

The metric advice assumes retrieval. For clustering or deduplication by threshold, the absolute score matters, not only the ranking, and a threshold tuned on one model, dimension or format does not carry to another.

The migration plans assume the corpus is yours to re-embed. If documents are embedded by customers or partners on their side, the migration becomes a compatibility problem with a long tail, and the index needs to serve two model versions by design.
