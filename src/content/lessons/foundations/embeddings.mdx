## The search that got worse after the upgrade

Scenario, not a real company: a team swaps its embedding model for a newer one with a better benchmark score. They embed new documents with the new model and leave the old ten million vectors where they are, because re-embedding costs money. Search quality drops the same afternoon. Nothing errors. Every query still returns ten results with confident scores.

The bug is not in the new model. It is in the idea that a vector means something on its own. A vector only means something relative to the other vectors produced by the same model, with the same settings, normalised the same way. This lesson is about that space: how it is built, how you measure distance in it, what it costs to store, and what breaks when you mix two of them.

## A coordinate, not a fingerprint

An embedding model takes a piece of text and returns a fixed-length list of floats. OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small returns 1,536 of them by default and text-embedding-3-large returns 3,072. Whatever the input length, up to the 8,192-token limit, the output length is the same.

The software analogy I use is a geocoder. You hand it an address and it hands back a latitude and longitude. The coordinates are useful only because every address went through the same geocoder: two points that are close on the map are close in the world. Feed half your addresses through a geocoder that uses a different projection and the distances become nonsense, even though every individual coordinate looks plausible.

Toy numbers make it concrete. Pretend the model has three dimensions and you embed three sentences:

| Text | Vector |
|---|---|
| "refund my order" | [0.9, 0.1, 0.3] |
| "I want my money back" | [0.8, 0.2, 0.4] |
| "the weather in Pune" | [0.1, 0.9, 0.2] |

The first two point in nearly the same direction. The third points somewhere else. Nobody labelled dimension one as "money" and nothing in the real model is that tidy; the dimensions are whatever helped the training objective. What you can rely on is the geometry: related texts end up close together.

## How the space gets built

Embedding models are trained contrastively. You feed the model pairs that should match, such as a question and the passage that answers it, or a title and its article, and pairs that should not. The loss pulls matching pairs together and pushes non-matching pairs apart. E5, for example, was trained this way on a large curated set of text pairs. Newer models such as the Qwen3 Embedding series train in stages: large-scale pre-training on weakly labelled pairs first, then fine-tuning on higher-quality data, with an LLM synthesising part of that data.

The consequence for an engineer is that "similar" means "similar in the way the training pairs were similar". If the training pairs were questions and answers, a question lands near its answer, which is what retrieval wants. It does not mean the model understands negation, units or your product's internal jargon. Whether "the deploy succeeded" and "the deploy did not succeed" land close together is something to test on your own data, not something to assume either way.

## Cosine, dot product, L2

Three ways to compare two vectors:

- **Dot product** multiplies matching coordinates and sums them. It grows with both the angle and the lengths of the vectors.
- **Cosine similarity** is the dot product divided by both lengths. It measures the angle only.
- **L2 (Euclidean) distance** is the straight-line distance between the two points.

The case that matters: if every vector has length 1, the three agree on the ranking. Cosine equals the dot product, and squared L2 distance equals 2 minus 2 times the cosine, so sorting by any of them gives the same order. OpenAI states that its embeddings are normalised to length 1, which is why its docs say you can use a dot product and that cosine and Euclidean distance give identical rankings.

When vectors are not normalised, they disagree. Take a query [0.9, 0.1, 0.3, 0.2], a document that is the same vector, and a "long" document that is the same vector multiplied by 3. Cosine scores both documents 1.0. The dot product scores the long one higher, 2.85 against 0.95. L2 distance scores the long one as far away, 1.949 against 0.0. Same direction, three different stories. That is the whole reason you check whether your model normalises before you pick a metric for the index.

<Callout kind="gotcha">
"Cosine and dot product are the same" is true only for unit-length vectors. Say the condition out loud in an interview, then say how you would check it: compute the norm of a few stored vectors.
</Callout>

## Dimensions cost memory

Storage is napkin math: number of vectors times dimensions times bytes per value. Guess this one before you look.

<p>10,000,000 × 3,072 × 4 bytes = 122,880,000,000 bytes, or <b>122.88 GB</b> (1 GB = 10^9 bytes). That is the vectors alone, before the approximate-nearest-neighbour index, metadata and replicas. At 768 dimensions the same corpus is 30.72 GB; stored as binary at 3,072 dimensions it is 3.84 GB.</p>

Three levers shrink that number, each with a cost.

**Fewer dimensions.** Matryoshka Representation Learning trains a model so that the first k values of the vector are themselves a usable embedding. The paper reports embeddings up to 14 times smaller at the same accuracy on ImageNet-1K classification. Several current text models are trained this way: OpenAI's third-generation models take a `dimensions` parameter, the Gemini embedding models recommend 768, 1,536 or 3,072, Qwen3-Embedding-8B accepts any size from 32 to 4,096, and EmbeddingGemma's 768 can be cut to 512, 256 or 128.

**Smaller numbers.** Store float16 instead of float32 and you halve memory. Hugging Face measured int8 quantization at 4 times less memory and binary at 32 times less, with binary keeping about 92.5% of retrieval performance without rescoring and up to about 96% with rescoring (re-scoring the binary shortlist with the full vectors).

**Fewer vectors.** Bigger chunks mean fewer vectors, at the cost of coarser retrieval. That trade belongs to the RAG track.

The trap inside the first lever: truncating a normalised vector makes it shorter than length 1. Cut a unit vector of 8 values to its first 4 and in the example in the code below the norm drops to 0.92. If your index uses a dot product, every truncated vector now scores lower than it should, by an amount that differs per vector. OpenAI's docs say to re-normalise after manual truncation, gemini-embedding-001 needs manual normalisation below 3,072 dimensions, EmbeddingGemma's card says to re-normalise after truncating, and gemini-embedding-2 does it for you. Read the card of the model you use.

## Real numbers

| Model | Default dimensions | Smaller sizes | Input limit | Normalisation after truncation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OpenAI text-embedding-3-small | 1,536 | `dimensions` parameter | 8,192 tokens | re-normalise if you truncate by hand |
| OpenAI text-embedding-3-large | 3,072 | `dimensions` parameter | 8,192 tokens | same |
| gemini-embedding-2 | 3,072 | 768 or 1,536 recommended | 8,192 tokens | done by the API |
| gemini-embedding-001 | 3,072 | 768 or 1,536 recommended | 2,048 tokens | manual below 3,072 |
| Qwen3-Embedding-8B (open weights) | 4,096 | any from 32 to 4,096 | 32K tokens | check before using a dot product |
| EmbeddingGemma (about 300M parameters) | 768 | 512, 256, 128 | 2,048 tokens | re-normalise |

<p class="tiny">All rows from the sources at the end, checked 28 Oct 2026. Price for scale: text-embedding-3-small is listed at $0.02 per million tokens, so embedding one billion tokens costs 1,000 × $0.02 = $20 at list price.</p>

Two habits from the 2025 models are worth naming. Instruction-aware embedders expect a prefix that says what the text is for. EmbeddingGemma's card gives a query prompt of the form `task: search result | query: ` followed by the text, and Qwen3-Embedding's card reports that instructions typically improve results by 1% to 5%. Leave the prefix off the queries and nothing errors; quality just drops. And benchmark averages are a starting shortlist, not an answer: the MMTEB authors found a 560M-parameter model, multilingual-e5-large-instruct, at the top of their public ranking ahead of models with billions of parameters. Your corpus decides.

## The comparison in code

```python
import math

def dot(a, b): return sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))
def norm(a): return math.sqrt(dot(a, a))
def cosine(a, b): return dot(a, b) / (norm(a) * norm(b))
def l2(a, b): return math.sqrt(sum((x - y) ** 2 for x, y in zip(a, b)))
def normalize(a):
    n = norm(a)
    return [x / n for x in a]

q = [0.9, 0.1, 0.3, 0.2]
docs = {"short": [0.9, 0.1, 0.3, 0.2], "long": [2.7, 0.3, 0.9, 0.6], "other": [0.1, 0.9, 0.2, 0.4]}
for name, d in docs.items():
    print(name, round(cosine(q, d), 3), round(dot(q, d), 3), round(l2(q, d), 3))
# short 1.0 0.95 0.0 / long 1.0 2.85 1.949 / other 0.325 0.32 1.153

full = normalize([0.5, -0.2, 0.7, 0.1, 0.05, -0.3, 0.2, 0.1])
cut = full[:4]
print(round(norm(cut), 3), round(norm(normalize(cut)), 3))   # 0.92 1.0
```

## Why you cannot mix models in one index

Back to the hook. Two embedding models, even two with the same number of dimensions, build different spaces. Dimension 17 in one has no relationship to dimension 17 in the other. A query embedded with the new model and compared against documents embedded with the old one is comparing coordinates from different projections. The cosine scores still fall between -1 and 1, the ranking is still a ranking, and none of it means anything. The same applies, more quietly, to the same model with a different prefix, a different truncation length or a different normalisation step.

The fix is boring and expensive: re-embed the corpus with the new model into a new index, run both side by side, compare on a labelled query set, then switch reads over. Store the model name, version, dimension and preprocessing settings with every vector, so a mixed index is detectable by a query rather than by a drop in quality.

## When this is wrong

The "nearby means related" picture is a good default and wrong in specific places. Similarity is whatever the training pairs taught, so negation, numbers, dates and exact identifiers (order ids, error codes, SKUs) are weak spots. That is why production retrieval usually pairs embeddings with keyword search and a reranker, which the RAG track covers. I have no reliable rate for how often negation fails, so I test it on the corpus I ship rather than quote one.

The storage math here counts raw vectors only. Approximate-nearest-neighbour indexes add overhead that depends on the index type and its parameters, so measure the real footprint in your vector store before sizing hardware. And the quantization figures are from one 2024 benchmark run; re-check recall on your own data before shipping binary vectors.
