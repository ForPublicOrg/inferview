## The decision

A log-probability is the one number the model gives you about its own output that costs nothing extra to compute: the engine already has the distribution when it samples. Exposed per token, it does two jobs. It locates the step where a generation could have gone another way, which turns "the model is flaky" into a specific position and a specific gap. And on the first token of a constrained answer it gives a confidence score for a label or an extracted value, which can route low-confidence outputs to a person. The decision is whether you can get it on the model you use, whether it is calibrated on your data, and what to do when the answer to either is no.

## What the engine returns

For each generated token, the engine can return the log-probability of the token it chose and of the N most likely alternatives at that step. In vLLM, `logprobs=N` returns the N most likely tokens and always includes the sampled token, so up to N + 1 entries per step; `-1` returns the whole vocabulary; the server's `max_logprobs` setting caps N. `prompt_logprobs` does the same for the prompt tokens, which is how you score a fixed text under the model without generating anything.

Three quantities come out of that. The gap between the top two log-probabilities at a step measures how close the choice was. The sum of the chosen tokens' log-probabilities is the sequence log-probability, and exp of minus its mean is the sequence perplexity. And for a response whose first token is the answer, such as `yes` or `no`, or a label from a fixed set, the probabilities of those first tokens are a distribution over answers.

Before comparing numbers across settings, check whether your engine returns log-probabilities from the raw distribution or from the one after temperature and truncation: the same token has a different probability at temperature 0.7 than at 1.

## Near-ties: debugging flips

The nondeterminism lesson showed one prompt run 1,000 times at temperature 0 splitting at token 103 between "Queens, New York" and "New York City". Batch-dependent numerics can only flip a choice when the top two candidates are already close. So when a production output flips between runs, the first question is where the two outputs diverge, and the second is the log-probability gap at that position.

A gap of a few thousandths at a token that carries meaning (approved or denied, a digit in an amount, the first word of a label) is a model that is undecided, and no change to serving will make that decision robust; it needs a better prompt, more context or a different model. A tiny gap at a token that carries no meaning (a synonym, a comma) explains the flip and needs nothing except an eval that does not compare strings. Without logprobs you can only see that outputs differ; with them you can rank divergence points by gap and fix the ones that matter.

The same reading works on a single run. Tokens whose chosen probability falls below a threshold are where the model was guessing, and in extraction they cluster on the values, not the boilerplate: the digits of an amount, the characters of an ID. Highlighting those spans in a review tool sends a reviewer to the part of the output that most needs a check.

## Confidence for labels and values

Instacart's catalog-attribute system, PARSE, uses a second call to score each extracted value. A scoring prompt, framed as an entailment task, gives the model the product's features, the attribute's definition and the extracted value and asks whether the value is correct, with yes or no as the first token. The system converts the first token's logit to the probability of yes and uses it as the confidence score; values from low-scoring products are flagged as potentially wrong and sent to human auditors, who correct them. The post does not publish the threshold or the verifier's own precision, which is the part every team has to measure for itself.

The pattern generalises. Ask the question so the answer is a single token from a small set, constrain or instruct the first token to be the answer, and read the distribution over the set from that token's top-N log-probabilities. Renormalise over the set, because some mass goes to tokens outside it, such as a leading space variant or a different casing.

A self-verification call doubles the calls per value. Reading the label's own first-token probability from the extraction call costs nothing extra, and is what to try first on classification. The second call earns its cost when the extraction is free text, where no single token carries the answer, or when the extracting prompt is long and the verifier can be a smaller, cheaper model.

## Calibration is a measurement, not a property

A score of 0.9 is useful only if values scored 0.9 are right about nine times in ten on your data. Nothing guarantees that. Instruction tuning, the prompt, the domain and the verifier model all move it, and a score that ranks well can still be systematically high. The measurement needs audited labels: bucket the scores, compare the mean score with the observed accuracy per bucket, then choose the threshold from the trade-off between how much you send to review and how many errors that catches.

The code below runs that measurement on scenario data: 2,000 values whose verifier is overconfident by construction.

```python
import math, random

rng = random.Random(3)
rows = []
for _ in range(2000):
    lp_yes = -rng.expovariate(8.0)
    p_yes = math.exp(lp_yes)
    correct = rng.random() < p_yes ** 2
    rows.append((p_yes, correct))

def reliability(rows, edges=(0.0, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0)):
    out = []
    for a, z in zip(edges, edges[1:]):
        sel = [(p, c) for p, c in rows if a <= p < z or (z == 1.0 and p == 1.0)]
        if sel:
            mean_p = sum(p for p, _ in sel) / len(sel)
            acc = sum(c for _, c in sel) / len(sel)
            out.append((a, z, len(sel), round(mean_p, 3), round(acc, 3)))
    return out

def at_threshold(rows, t):
    review = [r for r in rows if r[0] < t]
    errors = [r for r in rows if not r[1]]
    caught = [r for r in review if not r[1]]
    return len(review) / len(rows), len(caught) / len(errors)

for row in reliability(rows):
    print(row)        # (bin low, bin high, n, mean p_yes, observed accuracy)
for t in (0.80, 0.90, 0.95):
    rate, recall = at_threshold(rows, t)
    print(t, f"review {rate:.1%}", f"errors caught {recall:.1%}")
```

The bins show the shape: values scored 0.9 to 1.0 average 0.955 and are right 91.9% of the time; values scored 0.8 to 0.9 average 0.856 and are right 75.5% of the time. The ranking is good, the levels are high. The thresholds show the trade-off you actually choose: sending everything below 0.8 to review sends 16.4% of values and catches 41.2% of errors; below 0.9, 42.5% and 75.3%; below 0.95, 66.0% and 91.2%. With an auditing team sized for a fixed number of reviews a day, the threshold is set by capacity, and the errors you accept are the ones above it. Recalibrating the scores (fitting a map from score to observed accuracy) fixes the levels and changes nothing in this trade-off, because it keeps the order.

## When you cannot get them

Availability is narrowing on hosted frontier models. OpenAI's guide for its latest models says to remove `top_logprobs`, and `logprobs` on Chat Completions, whenever reasoning effort is not none, and two of the listed models do not support effort none at all. OWASP's 2025 entry on unbounded consumption lists model extraction and functional replication among the risks and, as a mitigation, recommends restricting or obfuscating the exposure of `logit_bias` and `logprobs` in API responses. Providers have a reason to withhold them that has nothing to do with your use case.

The fallbacks each cost something. Self-consistency, sampling the answer several times and using the agreement rate as confidence, costs one call per sample and needs a temperature above 0, which some hosted models will not let you set. A verifier that answers with a number or a label in text gives you a score you then have to calibrate exactly as above, with more variance. A small classifier trained on the audited labels gives a calibrated score cheaply once you have enough labels, and becomes a second model to maintain. Self-hosting an open-weight model returns the logprobs and adds a serving system.

## When this is wrong

The lesson treats the first-token probability as the model's belief about the answer. On reasoning models the visible first token follows hidden reasoning, and where logprobs are returned at all they describe the answer conditioned on that reasoning, which can be confident and wrong. Constraining the first token also interacts with the previous lesson: a grammar mask renormalises over allowed tokens, so the probability you read may be of a forced choice rather than a preferred one.

Calibration drifts. A score calibrated on last quarter's products or tickets can be off on this quarter's, and a model update can shift every score at once. The audit sample above the threshold is what detects that, and it is the part teams cut first. Finally, a high sequence log-probability means the text is typical for the model, not that it is true; perplexity is a debugging signal for where generation was uncertain, not a quality metric.
