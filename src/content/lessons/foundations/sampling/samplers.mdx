## The decision

Every sampler answers one question: which tokens in the tail of the next-token distribution may be drawn. Temperature reshapes the distribution; top-k, top-p and min-p each draw a line through it and renormalise what is left. They are admission policies over one ranked list, the way a fixed pool size, a load-based cap and a ratio to the healthiest backend are three policies over the same health scores. What separates them is how the line moves when the distribution changes shape, and the shape changes at every token. A sampler that suits one shape is often wrong for the other, and one generation passes through both shapes many times.

## The four rules, stated exactly

Engines apply these rules in sequence, and the sequence changes which tokens survive: min-p applied before temperature keeps one token on the peaked example below where applied after it keeps three. Two engines given the same four numbers can return different kept sets, so check the order before comparing settings across engines.

**Temperature** truncates nothing: at any finite T every token keeps non-zero probability, so temperature alone does not prevent junk tokens, it only makes them rarer or more common. vLLM accepts T in [0, 2] and treats 0 as greedy, and when it does, it forces top_p to 1, top_k to 0 and min_p to 0, so the other knobs are ignored at T = 0.

**Top-k** cuts at a rank, so it ignores shape entirely. On a peaked distribution k = 40 keeps 39 tokens the model barely considered; on a flat one it can cut tokens as likely as the ones it kept. In vLLM, top_k of 0 or -1 disables it.

**Top-p** cuts at a running sum, so its cutoff depends on every token above the line. Holtzman et al. proposed it to truncate "the less reliable tail" while keeping diversity, after showing that decoding by maximum likelihood produces text that is bland and repetitive. The kept set grows when the distribution is flat and shrinks when it is peaked. That is the behaviour you want until temperature is high: raising T moves mass into the tail, and a mass-based rule then admits more tail tokens to reach the same p.

**Min-p** keeps every token whose probability is at least p_base times the top token's probability. The cutoff scales with the model's confidence. The paper applies it after temperature: compute probabilities at temperature T, set the threshold to p_base x p_max, drop everything below, renormalise. It recommends p_base between 0.05 and 0.1 for general use and reports that 0.5 to 0.7 helps coherence at very high temperatures at some cost in diversity. Its experiments cover GPQA, GSM8K and AlpacaEval creative writing on Mistral and Llama 3 models from 1B to 123B parameters; the abstract reports the gains as largest at higher temperatures and says the method is adopted by Hugging Face Transformers and vLLM.

The structural difference fits in one line each. Top-k's line is a count. Top-p's line is a running sum, so it depends on every token above the line. Min-p's line is a ratio to one token, so it depends only on the top probability.

Temperature is applied first, then each rule cuts independently, so the three cuts are on the same probabilities.

Switch the widget to the flat distribution at temperature 1.0. Top-p 0.9 keeps six of eight: the six verbs sum to 0.949, and the first five reach only 0.844. Min-p 0.1 keeps seven, because its cutoff is 0.1 x 0.211 = 0.021 and `fell` at 0.047 passes. Here min-p is the more permissive rule. On a flat distribution it keeps plausible alternatives that a mass cutoff may drop, and on a peaked one at high temperature it cuts the tail that a mass cutoff admits; neither rule is uniformly tighter. Top-k with k = 4 cuts two verbs within a few hundredths of the ones it kept on the flat distribution, and admits the quote character on the peaked one at temperature 2.0.

The order of operations changes the answer too. Apply min-p 0.1 to the peaked distribution before temperature 2.0 rather than after: at temperature 1 the top token is 0.879, the cutoff is 0.088, the second token at 0.072 fails, and only one token survives, so raising the temperature afterwards cannot add diversity back. Applied after temperature, as the paper does, three tokens survive. Two engines given the same four numbers can keep different sets if they apply the steps in a different order, so the order belongs in any comparison of sampler settings across engines.

## The defaults that follow

For extraction, classification and tool arguments, the content is a fact the model either has or does not, and diversity is a defect. Greedy decoding is the natural choice where you can set it, and where the engine forces sampling, a low temperature with a min-p cutoff keeps the draw on the head of the distribution. Greedy does not guarantee identical output across calls; the next lesson covers why.

For open-ended generation the argument from Holtzman et al. applies: maximum-likelihood decoding degenerates into repetition, so you sample, and the question is how much tail to admit. Publishers state their own settings, and they differ. The DeepSeek-R1 repository recommends a temperature between 0.5 and 0.7, with 0.6 preferred, "to prevent endless repetitions or incoherent outputs". Settings published for one model family do not transfer to another, because the shape of the distribution is a property of the model's training.

On hosted frontier APIs the decision is increasingly made for you. Claude's documentation says that on its current models a non-default temperature, top_p or top_k returns a 400 error on every request, that top_p defaults to 0.99 on those models, and that from API version 2026-10-21 a request that includes any of the three returns 400 on every model. OpenAI's guide for its latest models tells callers to remove temperature, top_p and top_logprobs whenever reasoning effort is not none. A sampler policy in your code is therefore a self-hosting concern, or a concern for older model versions; on hosted reasoning models the levers are effort settings, output limits and structured outputs.

## A sampler in code

The four rules as filters over a probability dictionary, with temperature applied first and min-p after it. The `filter_*` functions return renormalised dictionaries; `draw` takes the uniform number as an argument so tests can pin it.

```python
import math, random

</Callout>

def softmax(logits, t):
    z = {k: v / t for k, v in logits.items()}
    m = max(z.values())
    e = {k: math.exp(v - m) for k, v in z.items()}
    s = sum(e.values())
    return {k: v / s for k, v in e.items()}

def renorm(d):
    s = sum(d.values())
    return {k: v / s for k, v in d.items()}

def ranked(p):
    return sorted(p.items(), key=lambda kv: (-kv[1], kv[0]))

def filter_top_k(p, k):
    return renorm(dict(ranked(p)[:k]))

def filter_top_p(p, top_p):
    kept, cum = {}, 0.0
    for tok, q in ranked(p):
        kept[tok] = q
        cum += q
        if cum >= top_p:
            break
    return renorm(kept)

def filter_min_p(p, p_base):
    cut = p_base * max(p.values())
    return renorm({k: q for k, q in p.items() if q >= cut})

def draw(p, r):
    cum = 0.0
    items = ranked(p)
    for tok, q in items:
        cum += q
        if r < cum:
            return tok
    return items[-1][0]

peaked = {"4": 9.0, "5": 6.5, "3": 5.8, '"': 4.0, "null": 3.5, "1": 3.0, "-": 2.0, "the": 1.0}
p = softmax(peaked, 2.0)
print(sorted(filter_top_p(p, 0.9)))    # ['"', '3', '4', '5']
print(sorted(filter_min_p(p, 0.1)))    # ['3', '4', '5']
rng = random.Random(7)
print([draw(filter_min_p(p, 0.1), rng.random()) for _ in range(8)])
```

Two choices in this code are policy, not fact. The top-p loop keeps the token that crosses p, so the top token always survives even when its probability alone exceeds p. Ties sort by token string, which makes the kept set reproducible when two tokens have equal probability. A real engine works on a tensor over the full vocabulary for every sequence in a batch and avoids a full sort where it can. The lab in this module builds the same rules with property tests and a seeded draw, and problem fnd-007 adds a draw that has to stay fast over a large vocabulary.

## When this is wrong

The lesson treats the distribution as a fixed input and the sampler as a separate layer. Two things break that. On reasoning models the visible answer follows hidden reasoning tokens that were themselves sampled under a policy you may not see or set, so the distribution at the answer depends on the sampler upstream. And penalties change the distribution before the cut: vLLM's repetition_penalty applies to tokens from both the prompt and the output, so in an extraction task a penalty above 1 pushes the model away from copying values out of the input, which is the task.

The examples have eight tokens. A real vocabulary has tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands, and the tail is long rather than a few named tokens; the rules behave the same way, but the far-tail mass is spread across many tokens that are each negligible, which makes top-p at high temperature worse than an eight-token example shows. And the comparison between samplers is an empirical claim about text quality, measured by the min-p authors on particular models and benchmarks. On your model and task, run the comparison rather than taking the default from a paper or from this page.
