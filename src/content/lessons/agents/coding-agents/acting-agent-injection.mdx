## The residual rate is a design input

A browser agent reads attacker-controlled text with the user's sessions loaded and can act on what it reads. For a SQL injection the fix is structural: parameterised queries separate code from data, and the residual rate is zero. Prompts have no such separation, and both large vendors now say so in writing. Anthropic's November 2025 post states that no browser agent is immune to prompt injection; OpenAI's December 2025 post on ChatGPT Atlas says injection, like scams and social engineering, is unlikely ever to be fully solved. Once the vendor publishes a nonzero rate and no date by which it reaches zero, the rate stops being a bug report and becomes an input to capacity planning, like a disk's annual failure rate. The design question is how many successes to expect and what each one can reach.

## What the published numbers measure

Anthropic's figures come from an internal adaptive Best-of-N attacker that combines known injection techniques, with 100 attempts per environment, reported as the percentage of attacks encountered that succeeded, over roughly 2,200 to 3,200 attempts per bar. For Claude Opus 4.5 in the Claude for Chrome extension, the attack success rate was 8.0% for the model alone, 7.6% with the original safeguards, and 1.4% with the improved safeguards; the post's text speaks of "a 1% attack success rate" that "still represents meaningful risk". The same chart gives 1.9% for Sonnet 4.5, 2.4% for Haiku 4.5 and 2.0% for Sonnet 4 with the improved safeguards, against 12.0% to 13.8% for those models alone. Most of the drop comes from the layers around the model (classifiers that scan all untrusted content, and the intervention after a detection), not from the model.

OpenAI publishes a method rather than a rate. Its Atlas team trained an attacker model with reinforcement learning and gave it a simulator: the attacker proposes an injection, the simulator runs the victim agent against it and returns the full reasoning and action trace, and the attacker revises before committing. The post reports attacks that steer the agent through workflows of tens or hundreds of steps, and a demonstration found this way in which an email planted in the inbox led the agent, asked to draft an out-of-office reply, to send a resignation letter to the user's CEO instead. The findings feed adversarial training (a new checkpoint was rolled out to all Atlas users) and changes to monitoring and system-level safeguards. ChatGPT agent's launch post names where injections hide (invisible page elements and metadata) and what they aim at: sharing connector data with the attacker, or acting on a site the user is logged into.

Three properties of these numbers matter for design. They are vendor-measured against the vendor's attacker in the vendor's environments. They are per attack encountered, not per user or per task. And they describe one model and safeguard version; the Anthropic figures stop at Opus 4.5 in November 2025, so a figure for a later model has to come from a later publication or your own red team.

## From a rate to an expected count

The count of successes is the rate times the number of injection attempts the agent encounters. The rate is published; the encounter count is yours, and part of it is the attacker's choice.

<Predict id="agt-inj-any" question="At a 1.4% success rate per attack encountered, what is the chance that at least one of 50 encountered attacks succeeds, treating attempts as independent?" answer={50.6} tolerance={2} unit="%">
<p><b>About 50.6%.</b> 1 - 0.986<sup>50</sup> = 1 - 0.494 = 0.506. The expected number of successes is 50 x 0.014 = 0.7. A targeted attacker who can make the agent read 200 variants (by sending emails, posting in a shared document, or seeding a page the agent visits) reaches 1 - 0.986<sup>200</sup> = 94%. Real attempts are not independent, since an attacker iterates on what nearly worked, which pushes the number up, not down.</p>
</Predict>

Two kinds of exposure need separate estimates. Untargeted exposure scales with your traffic: tasks per month times the fraction that meet attacker content times the rate. Scenario inputs, not measurements: 20,000 browsing tasks a month with 1% meeting an injection is 200 encounters and 200 x 0.014 = 2.8 expected successes a month; at 0.1% it is 0.28. The fraction is the dominant term and you cannot measure it in advance; assuming it is small is the assumption that flips the conclusion. Targeted exposure scales with the attacker's effort: anyone who can put text in front of a specific user's agent (an email, a calendar invite, a shared document) chooses the number of attempts. For a high-value user, the realistic design assumption is that a success will happen.

That turns the question from "how do we stop injection" into "what can one success do", which is a permissions question with known tools.

## Bounding the blast radius

Simon Willison's lethal trifecta (June 2025) names the exfiltration case: private data, untrusted content, and a way to communicate externally. Meta's Agents Rule of Two (October 2025) generalises it to three properties within a session: [A] processing untrustworthy inputs, [B] access to sensitive systems or private data, and [C] changing state or communicating externally. An agent may hold at most two; a task that needs all three in one context should not run autonomously and needs supervision, by a human or another reliable check. Adding state changes to the list matters for acting agents, because an injected agent that deletes files or buys something has done harm without exfiltrating anything.

Meta's examples are the design patterns. A web research assistant holds [A] and [C]: it browses arbitrary URLs from a sandbox with no preloaded sessions and limited private data. A public travel assistant holds [A] and [B] and gets human confirmation before reservations or deposits and web requests limited to trusted URLs. A high-velocity internal coder holds [B] and [C] and filters its inputs by author lineage so untrusted text does not reach it.

The vendors' own recommendations line up with the same split. OpenAI recommends Atlas's logged-out mode whenever the task does not need logged-in sites, and disabling ChatGPT agent's connectors when a task does not need them, both of which remove [B]. Anthropic's browser tool docs specify the environment for the [A] side: a dedicated VM with a fresh browser profile and no credentials, a domain allowlist enforced at the network layer and re-checked after redirects, loopback, link-local and private ranges blocked, non-HTTP schemes refused, and `javascript_exec` and `file_upload` left off. Each of those removes something an injected agent could use.

Splitting sessions is the main structural move. A research session with [A] and [C] reads the web and returns a structured artefact; an acting session with [B] and [C] takes that artefact and never reads untrusted text. The boundary is where the work is. The artefact was produced under attacker influence, so the acting session must treat it as data: parse it into typed fields, validate each against trusted records (a payee from the accounts-payable system, not from the page; an amount within the order total), and reject free text.

```python
A, B, C = "untrusted_input", "sensitive_access", "state_change_or_egress"


def p_any(asr, encounters):
    """Chance that at least one of n injection encounters succeeds, treating encounters as independent."""
    return 1 - (1 - asr) ** encounters


def session_props(tools):
    return set().union(*(t["props"] for t in tools))


def check(session):
    props = session_props(session["tools"])
    if len(props) <= 2:
        return "autonomous ok"
    return "supervised" if session.get("confirm_every_state_change") else "reject: all three properties"


research = {"tools": [{"name": "browse_web", "props": {A, C}}]}                 # logged out, no private data
acting = {"tools": [{"name": "read_ap_records", "props": {B}},
                    {"name": "pay_known_payee", "props": {B, C}}]}               # reads no untrusted text
merged = {"tools": research["tools"] + acting["tools"]}

print(round(p_any(0.014, 50), 3), round(0.014 * 50, 2))   # 0.506 0.7
for s in (research, acting, merged):
    print(check(s))   # autonomous ok, autonomous ok, reject: all three properties
```

A check like this belongs where sessions are assembled, so that adding a tool to an agent fails review when it completes the set. The tags are a judgement per tool, and the common error is tagging a tool that returns third-party text (an inbox, a ticket queue, a shared drive) as [B] only; anything that returns text an outsider can write is also [A].

Confirmations are the last layer, not the first. An injection's goal is often to get a human to approve an action, so the confirmation must show what makes the action dangerous: the recipient, the amount, the data being shared. OpenAI's Atlas guidance asks users to check that information being shared is appropriate for the context; Meta lists a user approving a warning without reading it as a way a Rule-of-Two design still fails. Detection and response sit beside all of this: log every action with the page or message that preceded it, so a success can be traced to its source, and keep the kill switch from [autonomy controls](/learn/agents/coding-agents/autonomy-controls) able to revoke the sessions an injected agent holds.

One more gap applies to approvers that are models. Anthropic's auto-mode classifier does not see tool results, by design, so content the agent read cannot argue it into approving; the cost is that it cannot tell whether a resource the user never named was found or fabricated. A model approver closes some injection paths and opens a provenance question the harness has to answer with logs. [Injection defences](/learn/safety/injection-defences) covers detection layers; this lesson's concern is the reach of a success.

## When this is wrong

The rates are vendor measurements against internal attackers in internal environments, for specific model and safeguard versions. External attackers differ in skill and patience, a vendor attacker with white-box access may be stronger than most real ones (OpenAI describes that asymmetry as the point of its approach), and a rate measured on browsing tasks may not transfer to email or documents. Treat them as an order of magnitude for planning, and measure your own agent with your own red team before relying on any figure.

The independence model understates correlated risk. One poisoned page read by thousands of users' agents is one attack with thousands of encounters, and a technique that works tends to keep working until the vendor patches it, so successes cluster in time rather than arriving at a steady rate.

The Rule of Two is not sufficient, as its authors say: it does not address agent mistakes without an attacker or excessive privilege inside the two allowed properties. Several agents that each hold two properties can also pass data among themselves until the system as a whole holds all three. And session splitting moves risk to the boundary; an acting session that accepts free text from the research session has merged the sessions in all but name.
