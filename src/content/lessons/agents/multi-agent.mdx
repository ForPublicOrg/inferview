## Three agents, one game

Scenario, not a real company: a team splits "build a small browser game" across three sub-agents: one for the game loop, one for the graphics, one for the sound. Each finishes, each passes its own checks, and the pieces do not fit. The graphics agent drew sprites at a scale the game loop never expected; the sound agent assumed events the game loop never fires. Cognition used a version of this, with a Flappy Bird clone, to argue against multi-agent designs: every action an agent takes carries decisions that never get written down, and an agent that sees only its brief cannot honour decisions it never saw.

The opposite case is real too. Anthropic built its research feature as a lead agent that spawns sub-agents to search in parallel, and reported that it beat a single agent by a wide margin on their internal eval. Both posts came out in the same week of June 2025. Knowing why both are right is the interview answer.

## The patterns

Most multi-agent systems are one of four shapes. If you have built distributed systems, they will look familiar.

Supervisor, or orchestrator-workers: a lead agent breaks the task down, delegates parts to workers, and assembles the results. A coordinator with a work queue.

Handoff: control passes from one agent to another, which then owns the conversation. A call transfer. In the OpenAI Agents SDK a handoff appears to the model as a tool named `transfer_to_<agent name>`, and the receiving agent sees the prior conversation unless an `input_filter` trims it.

Agents as tools: a manager calls a specialist like a function and keeps control. The SDK exposes this as `Agent.as_tool()`. The difference from a handoff is who answers the user.

Pipeline and peer patterns: fixed stages (CrewAI's sequential process, Google ADK's Sequential and Parallel workflow agents), or peers that debate or vote. CrewAI's hierarchical process needs a manager model or agent; ADK adds a Loop agent and talks to remote agents over A2A.

## When more agents help

Anthropic's research system used Claude Opus 4 as the lead and Claude Sonnet 4 as sub-agents, and "outperformed single-agent Claude Opus 4 by 90.2%" on their internal research eval. The same post explains most of it. On the BrowseComp benchmark, three factors explained 95% of the variance in performance, and token usage alone explained 80%. Multi-agent works there largely because it spends more tokens in parallel, each sub-agent with a fresh context. Agents in their data used about 4 times the tokens of chat, and multi-agent systems about 15 times.

So the condition is breadth-first work that splits into independent parts, where each part needs a lot of reading and only a short answer has to come back. They scaled effort to the query: one agent with 3 to 10 tool calls for a simple fact, 2 to 4 sub-agents with 10 to 15 calls each for a comparison, more than 10 sub-agents for complex research. Parallel tool calling cut research time by up to 90% on complex queries. Wall-clock time drops; token spend does not.

## When more agents hurt

Coding is the counter-example: the parts share state and every decision constrains the next. Cognition's rule is to share full traces, not briefs, and to default to a single-threaded agent, compressing history when it gets long rather than splitting the work.

The MAST study is the systematic version. Its authors annotated over 1,600 traces from 7 multi-agent frameworks and found 14 failure modes in three groups: system design issues (bad role specs, ignoring the termination condition), inter-agent misalignment (withholding information, ignoring another agent's input, drifting off task), and task verification (stopping early, checking incompletely or not at all). Inter-annotator agreement was a kappa of 0.88, which is high for this kind of labelling. Most of those failure modes are coordination bugs you could not have in a single agent.

## Errors compound

Chain steps that each succeed independently with probability *p*, and the run succeeds with probability *p* to the power *n*.

<Predict id="multi-agent-compound" question="Ten steps, each succeeding 95% of the time. What percentage of runs succeed end to end?" answer={59.9} tolerance={3} unit="%">
<p>0.95 to the power 10 is 0.599, so <b>59.9%</b>, and 40.1% of runs fail. Adding the step error rates (10 × 5% = 50%) is the common wrong answer; failures overlap, so the true failure rate is lower than the sum but far higher than any one step's.</p>
</Predict>

Reliability across repeated runs has its own trap. pass@k asks whether at least one of k attempts succeeds, which is what you want for a coding benchmark where you can pick the passing patch. pass^k, introduced with tau-bench, asks whether all k attempts succeed, which is what a customer who uses your agent k times experiences. One rises with k and the other falls.

<AgentsCompounding />

At the defaults, one run succeeds 59.9% of the time; with four trials, pass@4 is 97.4% and pass^4 is 12.9%. Move the step success rate to 0.99 and notice how long a chain stays above 50%: 68 steps, against 13 at 0.95. That is the case for verification steps that catch errors before the next step builds on them, and for fewer, more reliable steps over many fragile ones.

## Coordination costs

Every extra agent adds a context to fill, a prompt to maintain and a boundary where information is lost. The cost arithmetic is worth doing out loud. Opus 5.5 lists at $4 per million input tokens and Haiku 5.5 at $0.10 for prompts up to 100,000 tokens, a 40-fold difference, which is why sub-agents that read a lot and return a little are often run on a smaller model while the lead stays large. The reverse also exists: a small, fast model handles the conversation and delegates the hard step to a large one.

Guardrails and policies do not automatically cover every agent. In the OpenAI Agents SDK, input guardrails run only for the first agent in a run, output guardrails only for the agent that produces the final output, and input guardrails run in parallel with the agent by default, so tokens may already be spent and tools already called when a tripwire fires. Set `run_in_parallel=False` when the check must come first.

## Estimating reliability from trials

You rarely know *p*. You run each task *n* times and count *c* successes. tau-bench's estimator for pass^k is C(c, k) / C(n, k): the chance that k trials drawn without replacement from your n are all successes.

```python
from math import comb

def pass_at_k(n, c, k):
    """At least one of k trials succeeds (unbiased, from n trials with c successes)."""
    return 1 - comb(n - c, k) / comb(n, k)

def pass_hat_k(n, c, k):
    """All k trials succeed (tau-bench's pass^k)."""
    return comb(c, k) / comb(n, k)

print(round(pass_hat_k(8, 6, 4), 3))   # 0.214
print(round(pass_at_k(8, 6, 4), 3))    # 1.0
print(round((6 / 8) ** 4, 3))          # 0.316, the plug-in estimate
```

Six successes in eight trials gives a pass^4 of 15 / 70 = 0.214. Plugging the success rate 0.75 into 0.75 to the power 4 gives 0.316, which overstates reliability when n is small. With 8 trials and only 2 failures, any 4 trials include at least one success, so pass@4 is exactly 1.

## When this is wrong

The compounding formula assumes independent steps with a fixed success rate. Real errors are correlated (one wrong assumption breaks every later step) and recoverable (a good agent notices and backtracks), so treat the widget as a lower bound on how fast chains decay without verification, not a prediction.

The headline multi-agent numbers come from one vendor's internal eval on research tasks with 2025 models. They tell you the conditions under which the pattern worked, not a speed-up you can quote for your own system. MAST's taxonomy comes from specific frameworks and benchmarks; use it as a checklist for reading traces.

And the single-agent advice has limits too. When one context cannot hold the work even with compaction, splitting is the only option, and the job becomes designing what crosses the boundary.
