## Resolution rate is the wrong objective

A support assistant that closes conversations is easy to build. A support assistant that closes issues is the product, and the two diverge in ways a dashboard of "resolved by the bot" will not show. The customer who got a confident wrong answer comes back two days later, angrier, to a human who now has to undo it. The customer the bot refused to escalate gives up and churns, which never shows as a contact at all.

So the design starts from the metric, because the metric decides where escalation sits. Scenario numbers, for one week: 10,000 conversations reach the assistant; it closes 6,000 without a human and escalates 4,000; 1,200 of the 6,000 come back within seven days about the same issue, and 800 of those reach a human.

- Bot resolution as usually reported: 6,000 / 10,000 = 60%.
- Resolved without a re-contact: (6,000 − 1,200) / 10,000 = 48%.
- Human contacts: 4,000 escalations + 800 re-contacts = 4,800, and the 800 arrive with a wrong answer to unwind.

The primary metric is issues resolved without a re-contact inside a fixed window, per customer, not per conversation. Escalations are not a cost to minimise; they are a counted outcome with their own quality bar (did the human have to ask the customer to repeat themselves?). Harm is measured separately by audit: a sample of closed conversations is checked for a policy applied wrongly in either direction, a refund granted that policy forbids or one refused that policy allows.

Two consequences for the experiment. Assignment is by stable hash of the customer, not the conversation, because the re-contact is a second conversation and must land in the same arm. And a seven-day window means every readout is a week late, which is the cost of measuring the right thing.

## Policy belongs in the tools

tau-bench's setup is the support case in miniature: a simulated user, an agent with domain-specific API tools, and policy guidelines written for the agent. Success is judged on the final database state against the annotated goal, not on what the agent said. The paper's finding is that state-of-the-art function-calling agents such as gpt-4o completed under half the tasks and were inconsistent across repeats, with pass^8 under 25% in the retail domain, and its authors conclude agents need better ways to follow rules reliably.

The design response is to stop asking the model to enforce policy that a tool can enforce. A refund tool checks the order's age, the amount limit and the customer's refund history and returns a refusal with a reason the model can relay. An address change on a shipped order fails in the tool. What remains for the model is the part only it can do: understanding which policy applies, explaining it, and deciding whether the case is one it should hand off. The prompt still carries the policy text, so the model can explain it, but correctness no longer depends on the model reading it right every time.

Retrieval over policy has a cheaper option than retrieval. Anthropic's guidance is that a knowledge base under 200,000 tokens, about 500 pages, can go in the prompt whole, and prompt caching makes that affordable. A support policy set often fits.

## Escalation is a component

Escalation has three parts, each designed and tested on its own.

**Triggers.** The customer asks for a person, and that request is honoured on the first ask. The topic is on a list that always goes to a human: legal threats, safety, account takeover, bereavement. A tool refused an action and the customer disputes the refusal. The conversation has run a set number of turns without progress on the stated issue. The retrieved policy does not cover the question, which the assistant detects by being asked to cite the clause it relied on and failing to. Model confidence scores alone are a weak trigger; a citation requirement is a checkable one.

**The handoff record.** A structured summary the human reads in seconds: the customer's issue in one line, the account and order identifiers, what was tried, which tools ran and what they returned, and the policy clause in question. The test for it is operational: the human agent does not ask the customer anything the record already holds. Measure the share of handoffs where they do.

**The way back.** Some escalations are approvals: the human authorises the refund the tool refused, and the assistant can complete the conversation. Designing that path keeps humans on judgement rather than typing.

tau2-bench adds the case most support benchmarks skipped: the customer also acts. In its telecom domain the user changes settings on their own device while the agent guides them, a dual-control environment, and the paper reports significant drops when agents move from a passive user to dual control. A support assistant that walks customers through steps on their phone or router is in that regime, and its eval needs a simulated user who sometimes does the step wrong.

## The offline gate

Before launch, the eval runs tasks against a simulated customer and checks state, as tau-bench does: did the order end in the right state, was the right refund issued, was the conversation escalated when the task required it. Escalation-required tasks are part of the set; an assistant that never escalates fails them.

Each task runs several times, because a customer meets the assistant once and does not get the average. pass^k is the probability that all *k* trials of a task succeed, estimated per task from *c* successes in *n* trials as C(c, k) / C(n, k) and averaged over tasks.

<Predict id="sys-support-passk" question="A task succeeded on 9 of 10 trials. Using C(c, k) / C(n, k), what is the estimated pass^4 for this task?" answer={0.6} tolerance={0.02} unit="probability">
<p><b>0.6.</b> C(9, 4) = 126 and C(10, 4) = 210, so 126 / 210 = 0.6. The plug-in estimate 0.9^4 ≈ 0.656 is close but biased upward for small n. A task at 90% single-trial success fails at least once in four tries 40% of the time, which is the number a repeat customer feels.</p>

## When this is wrong

The re-contact metric assumes you can link two conversations to the same issue. With anonymous chat and no account, linking is fuzzy, and the metric becomes "same customer, any issue, within the window", which overcounts. Moving policy into tools assumes the policy is crisp enough to code; goodwill gestures and exceptions remain judgement, and those belong with a human or behind an approval, not in a prompt. tau-bench and tau2-bench use simulated users written by the authors; real customers are less cooperative and less coherent, so the offline gate is a floor on failure, not a forecast of production.
