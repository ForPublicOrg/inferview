## A workflow that writes decisions

A claims or compliance system reads heterogeneous documents and writes a decision someone can be held to: pay this claim, flag this contract clause, reject this invoice line. That second half decides the architecture. The process has known steps, known approvers and a regulator who will ask why, so the right shape is a workflow with model steps inside it, not an agent choosing its own path. The model extracts, classifies and drafts; deterministic rules check hard limits; a person signs; and every step leaves a record keyed to the exact document version it read.

The two published systems closest to this case are built that way. Uber's TextSense invoice pipeline runs ingestion, pre-processing, OCR, LLM extraction, post-processing business rules and validation, then a review screen that shows the PDF beside the extracted fields, all orchestrated on Cadence, with approved invoices flowing to the ERP. Instacart's PARSE extracts product attributes from text and images and sends low-confidence values to human reviewers. Neither lets the model decide alone.

## What a page costs

Every figure here names its model family, because the families count differently.

**Claude.** The PDF docs put each page at typically 1,500 to 3,000 text tokens depending on density, and each page is also converted into an image that is billed under the vision rules. An image costs ceil(width / 28) × ceil(height / 28) visual tokens, capped at 4,784 on Claude 4.7 and later (2,576 px long edge) and 1,568 on other Claude models. So a dense page on a recent model can approach 3,000 + 4,784 = 7,784 tokens. The one measured example in the docs, for Bedrock's Converse API, is about 7,000 tokens for a 3-page PDF with full visual understanding against about 1,000 in text-only mode.

**Gemini.** Google's document page says each page is equivalent to 258 tokens, with text embedded in the PDF extracted and passed to the model, and accepts up to 1,000 pages or 50 MB.

The arithmetic matters because claims are long. Scenario: a claim bundle of 42 pages (form, invoices, a police report, photos, letters).

## The pipeline

**Intake and versioning.** Each file is stored under its content hash with the claim ID. The hash is the document version: if a claimant uploads a corrected invoice, it is a new version, and the old one stays. Everything downstream refers to hashes, never to file names or URLs.

**Classify and route.** A cheap classifier labels each file (form, invoice, medical letter, photo, police report) and whether the PDF has a text layer. Text-native PDFs go to text extraction; scans and photos go to a vision model. This is the first cost lever: only pages that need vision pay for it.

**Extract once.** A model fills a typed claim record (claimant, policy number, loss date, amounts per line, damage codes) and every field carries the document hash, page and region it came from. The record is a few thousand tokens and is what every later step reads. Uber's account is a warning about which model to use here: fine-tuned smaller models did well on header fields and hallucinated line items, which is why it chose GPT-4.

**Validate.** Deterministic checks the model cannot override: line items sum to the total, the loss date falls inside the policy period, the policy number exists and covers the claimed peril. A failed check routes to a person with the reason.

**Route by confidence.** Instacart's PARSE scores each extracted value by the probability of "yes" in a self-verification prompt and sends low-confidence values to reviewers. Field-level routing beats claim-level routing: the reviewer checks the three uncertain fields, not the whole bundle. The threshold is chosen per field from the cost of a wrong value, set on a labelled sample, and revisited when the model changes.

**Route by difficulty.** PARSE found a cheaper model matched stronger ones on simple attributes at 70% lower cost and lost 60% accuracy on difficult ones (Instacart's own measurements). The claims equivalent is per field type: dates and policy numbers on a small model, free-text damage descriptions and line items on a large one, decided by per-field accuracy on the labelled set, not by intuition.

**Retrieve the clauses in force.** Policy wording is chunked by clause with product, version and effective dates as metadata. A claim retrieves only clauses for its product version in force on the loss date. If a product's whole wording fits comfortably in a cached prompt, skip retrieval for it.

**Draft, check, sign.** The model drafts a recommendation that cites claim-record fields and clause IDs. A rules engine applies coverage caps, deductibles and exclusions. The adjuster sees the draft, citations and rule results, and decides.

## The cost per claim

Scenario inputs: Claude Sonnet 5.5 at $2 input and $10 output per million tokens; the 42-page bundle at 98,000 input tokens; a 3,000-token claim record out; drafting with the record, eight 800-token clauses and 1,000 tokens of instructions (10,400 in, 800 out); five review turns of about 12,000 tokens each carrying the record, not the PDF.

- Extraction, interactive: 98,000 × $2 / 1M + 3,000 × $10 / 1M = $0.226. Through the Batch API at 50% off: $0.113. Claims are not answered in seconds, so extraction belongs in batch.
- Drafting: 10,400 × $2 / 1M + 800 × $10 / 1M = $0.029.
- Review turns: 5 × 12,000 × $2 / 1M = $0.12.
- Total: about $0.26 a claim, against $1.96 in input tokens alone for the chat that re-sends the bundle.

The dominant term is the one-time page cost, so the levers in order are: send fewer pages to vision (text-layer routing), send them once (the record), send them at batch price, and only then compare model families.

## The audit trail

A regulator or a court will ask why claim 1234 was denied, possibly years later. The decision record stores: the hashes of every document version read; the extracted fields with page and region; which fields a person corrected and who; the clause IDs and policy version retrieved; the model, prompt and rules-engine versions; the draft; the adjuster's decision and their notes. Because documents are stored by hash and never overwritten, the record can be replayed: the same inputs through the same versions give the same draft, or the difference is itself evidence.

Two retention conflicts appear here. Privacy law can require deleting a claimant's data while insurance regulation requires keeping the decision basis; the design separates the decision record (identifiers, hashes, reasons) from the documents so deletion and retention can follow different rules. And a model retired by its provider cannot be re-run, so replay means stored outputs, not re-execution.

## When this is wrong

Page-token figures are per family and per model generation; the Claude visual-token caps changed with Claude 4.7, and Gemini's 258 is per page regardless of density, so price the actual documents on the actual model before committing to a provider. Uber's and Instacart's numbers are their own, on their own documents, and neither describes claims. The extract-once design assumes later questions can be answered from the record; an adjuster who needs to see a photo of the damage still needs the image, so the review screen shows source pages, which are displayed, not sent to the model.
