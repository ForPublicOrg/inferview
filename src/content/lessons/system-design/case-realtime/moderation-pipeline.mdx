## Thresholds and reviewer hours are one decision

A moderation pipeline has three outcomes per item (allow, block, look closer) and three tiers that can decide: a cheap classifier that sees everything, a heavier model that sees the ambiguous middle, and people who see what the model cannot settle plus every appeal. The two thresholds on the first classifier's score set the width of the middle band. That width sets the load on the heavier model, which sets the residual that reaches people, which must fit the reviewer hours the team actually has.

So the operating point is not chosen from a precision-recall curve alone. It is chosen from the curve, the cost of each error type per policy category, and the size of the review queue, at the same time. A team that tunes thresholds in one meeting and staffs review in another finds out at the first spike which of the two it got wrong.

## What a first-stage flag is worth

Published guard models report their operating points on their own test sets, at their own base rates. Meta's card for Llama Guard 4 reports, for output filtering on an in-house set, recall of 69 percent at an 11 percent false positive rate in English, and 43 percent at 3 percent averaged over seven other languages. Those numbers are the model's default decision, not a threshold you picked.

Put that classifier in front of production traffic where 1 percent of items violate policy (a scenario value; measure yours from a labelled sample). Per million items, 10,000 violate and the model flags 6,900 of them; 990,000 do not, and it flags 108,900 of those. About 6 percent of flags are real.

This is why the first stage escalates rather than blocks. Anthropic's description of its next-generation Constitutional Classifiers says the same thing about its own system: the first-stage probe can tolerate more false positives because a flag sends the exchange to a more expensive classifier instead of refusing it. Two thresholds on the first stage follow from that. The low one is set for recall, because anything below it is never looked at again. The high one, above which the first stage blocks on its own, is set for precision per category, and for many categories it should not exist.

The Anthropic numbers are the clearest published evidence for the cascade shape. The first generation of Constitutional Classifiers added 23.7 percent compute and 0.38 percent refusals on harmless traffic. An exchange classifier that judged outputs in the context of their inputs cut successful jailbreaks in red teaming by more than half but cost 50 percent more compute on its own. Putting a cheap probe in front of it and escalating only flagged traffic brought the overhead to about 1 percent, with 0.05 percent refusals on harmless queries over a month of Claude Sonnet 4.5 traffic. The same model became affordable by changing what it reads.

Two details from OpenAI's moderation guide change the design. The endpoint is upgraded in place, and the guide says custom thresholds on `category_scores` may need recalibration over time, so a threshold is valid for one model version and one labelled set, and both are logged with every decision. And when moderation runs inline on a streamed response, the scores arrive after the full output, so a streamed reply has already been shown by the time it is scored. For user-visible model output that means holding the stream, or accepting that a violating reply can be seen before it is withdrawn and writing that down as a risk.

## Sizing the middle and the queue

The load on each stage follows from four rates. The band rate is the share of items between the two first-stage thresholds. The second-stage resolution rate is the share of those the heavier model decides with enough confidence. The block rate and the appeal rate set the appeals queue, which arrives no matter what the thresholds are.

```python
# Daily load on each stage of a moderation cascade. Every input is a scenario value except
# where noted; replace them with your own measurements.
import math

def cascade(items, band, s2_resolves, block_rate, appeal_rate, per_reviewer_day):
    to_model = items * band                    # stage 1 cannot decide these
    to_humans = to_model * (1 - s2_resolves)   # stage 2 cannot decide these either
    appeals = items * block_rate * appeal_rate
    queue = to_humans + appeals
    return to_model, queue, math.ceil(queue / per_reviewer_day)

for band in (0.02, 0.03, 0.05):
    m, q, r = cascade(2_000_000, band, s2_resolves=0.80, block_rate=0.005,
                      appeal_rate=0.10, per_reviewer_day=300)
    print(f"band {band:.0%}: stage 2 {m:,.0f}/day, human queue {q:,.0f}/day, reviewers {r}")

# Largest band 40 reviewers can absorb, with appeals served first
cap = 40 * 300
print(f"max band for 40 reviewers: {(cap - 2_000_000 * 0.005 * 0.10) / (2_000_000 * 0.20):.2%}")
```

```text
band 2%: stage 2 40,000/day, human queue 9,000/day, reviewers 30
band 3%: stage 2 60,000/day, human queue 13,000/day, reviewers 44
band 5%: stage 2 100,000/day, human queue 21,000/day, reviewers 70
max band for 40 reviewers: 2.75%
```

The last line is the decision procedure in one number. With 40 reviewers at 300 items a day each and appeals served first, the first stage can send at most 2.75 percent of traffic to the middle band. If the error costs for a category call for a wider band than that, there are three ways out, and each has a cost: hire, raise the second stage's resolution rate (a better or more expensive model, or a narrower policy prompt per category), or let the residual wait in a queue with the item held at limited reach. The third is the one teams take without saying so.

Limited reach is the state that makes a synchronous budget achievable. A post in the middle band is visible to its author and not distributed until a later stage decides. That turns the second and third stages into asynchronous work with a deadline, and the deadline is set per category: an item scored in a self-harm category goes to the front of the queue regardless of arrival order.

## Appeals and the audit record

Appeals are the only measurement of false positives the system gets for free, and only for blocked items whose authors bother. Every decision row carries the item ID, the stage that decided, the model and version at each stage, the scores, the thresholds in force and the policy version. Without the thresholds in the row, an appeal overturn cannot be traced to a threshold change, and a recalibration after a model upgrade cannot be audited.

The appeal overturn rate per category is the dashboard metric for the high threshold. The false negative side needs its own sample: a fixed random draw of allowed items, labelled by reviewers each week, because nothing else in the pipeline ever looks at an item below the low threshold again.

## When this is wrong

The cascade assumes the first stage's errors are mostly in the middle band, so that escalation catches them. Adversarial content is designed to score low. A post that clears the low threshold is never seen again, and appeals do not measure it, so the random sample of allowed items is the only check on that side; if it is small, the false negative rate is unknown, not low.

The published numbers are each vendor's own, on its own data. Llama Guard 4's recall figures are for output filtering on Meta's in-house set; Anthropic's 1 percent overhead is for a probe that reads activations of a model it serves, which a team calling a hosted model cannot copy. The resolution rate, appeal rate and reviewer throughput in the code are scenario values; all three move with policy changes and with who is attacking the platform that week.

Where most traffic is not easy, for example a platform whose users mostly post in a language the classifier handles poorly, the cascade's economics invert: the band is wide, the second stage reads most traffic, and a single stronger classifier per language may cost less than the cascade does.
