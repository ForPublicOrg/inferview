## The pause that felt like a dropped call

Scenario, not a real product: a bank's voice assistant answers correctly, in a natural voice, after about two seconds of silence. Callers say "hello?" into the gap, which the system hears as a new turn, which restarts the response. The model was fine. The pipeline waited for the full text reply before starting speech, and every stage ran one after another.

Real-time rounds test whether you can hold a budget across a chain of components, and whether you know which parts of a system should not be an LLM at all. This lesson covers voice, moderation, chat backends with billing, uploads, and two places where classic models stay in charge.

## The idea: a latency budget is a critical path

You have read a build log for the slowest chain of dependent steps. A voice turn has the same structure: detect the end of speech, finish the transcript, reach the model, get the first useful tokens, synthesise the first audio, buffer it, play it. Add the stages in series and you get the worst design. Overlap them and you get a usable one.

A vendor's example budget puts numbers on it: about 680 ms end to end at P50 and 1,560 ms at P95 when every stage streams into the next. For scale, Stivers and colleagues measured turn-taking across ten languages and found the most common gap between speakers is 0 ms and the mean +208 ms. People start planning their reply before the other person finishes. A voice agent cannot, so it has to recover time everywhere else.

## Voice agent: pipeline or speech-to-speech

OpenAI's voice-agent guide frames the choice. A speech-to-speech session handles audio turns, tools and interruptions in one model. A chained pipeline (speech-to-text, a text agent, text-to-speech) is for when each stage needs to be visible or replaceable, and it gives you text you can check against policy before anything is spoken. Neither removes the need for a transcript: you need one for audit, moderation and evals either way.

Four details carry the deep dive. **Turn detection:** silence-based VAD cuts people off mid-thought, and OpenAI also offers semantic VAD with an eagerness setting. **Barge-in:** when the user speaks over the agent, stop playback, truncate what the model believes it said to what was actually heard, and continue. **Transport:** browsers connect over WebRTC with a short-lived client secret minted by your server, never the API key. **Session limits:** Gemini Live caps audio-only sessions at 15 minutes without context compression and recycles connections about every 10 minutes with a warning message, so reconnection is a feature you design.

Cost is the surprise. OpenAI's Realtime API counts user audio at 1 token per 100 ms and assistant audio at 1 token per 50 ms, and sends the entire conversation to the model for each response. So every turn re-bills every earlier turn.

<p><b>$1.81</b>, about 3.6 times the 10-turn cost for twice the turns. Cost per minute rises from $0.20 to $0.36 because the re-sent history grows every turn. A 90 percent cache hit on that history brings the 20-turn call to $0.46. Cap session length, summarize old turns, and keep the history cacheable, which also means not truncating from the start of the conversation on every turn.</p>

```python
# Speech-to-speech session cost when the whole conversation is re-sent each turn.
# OpenAI Realtime counts user audio at 1 token per 100 ms and assistant audio at 1 per 50 ms.
# gpt-realtime-2.1 audio, USD per 1M tokens, checked 2026-10-28: $32 in, $0.40 cached in, $64 out.
P_IN, P_CACHED, P_OUT = 32.0, 0.40, 64.0

def session_cost(turns, user_s, agent_s, cache_hit=0.0):
    history, total = 0.0, 0.0
    for _ in range(turns):
        user_tok, agent_tok = user_s * 10, agent_s * 20
        reused = history * (cache_hit * P_CACHED + (1 - cache_hit) * P_IN)
        total += (reused + user_tok * P_IN + agent_tok * P_OUT) / 1e6
        history += user_tok + agent_tok
    return total

for turns, hit in [(10, 0.0), (20, 0.0), (20, 0.9)]:
    minutes = turns * (5 + 10) / 60
    c = session_cost(turns, user_s=5, agent_s=10, cache_hit=hit)
    print(f"{turns} turns, {minutes:.1f} min, cache hit {hit:.0%}: ${c:.3f} (${c / minutes:.3f}/min)")
```

```text
10 turns, 2.5 min, cache hit 0%: $0.504 ($0.202/min)
20 turns, 5.0 min, cache hit 0%: $1.808 ($0.362/min)
20 turns, 5.0 min, cache hit 90%: $0.457 ($0.091/min)
```

Per-minute products change the comparison: GPT-Live 1 lists voice sessions at $0.05 a minute, with the backend model and tools billed on top. Compare like with like before you pick.

## Content moderation pipeline

Moderation at scale is a cascade, the same shape as a CPU cache hierarchy: a cheap check on everything, an expensive check on what the cheap one cannot decide, and humans on what neither can. Anthropic's next-generation Constitutional Classifiers work this way, with a light probe screening all traffic and escalating to a heavier ensemble, at about 1 percent compute overhead and 0.05 percent refusals on harmless queries over a month of traffic.

The pieces available today differ in what they are good at. OpenAI's `omni-moderation-latest` is free and covers 13 categories across text and images, but OpenAI warns that thresholds on its scores may need recalibration as the model is upgraded, so pin and re-test them. Llama Guard 4 is a 12B classifier you can host, over a fixed hazard taxonomy; Meta found that filtering inputs raises refusals more than filtering outputs. gpt-oss-safeguard takes your written policy as input and reasons about it, which makes new policies quick to try, but its own report says classifiers trained on tens of thousands of labelled examples can still do better, and it is compute-intensive. So: trained classifiers in the hot path, a policy reasoner on escalations or offline, appeals to humans, and every decision logged with the model and threshold that made it.

## Multi-tenant chat backend with state and billing

Three design calls. **Where state lives.** Keep the conversation in your own store, keyed by tenant and conversation, even if the provider offers server-side state. With OpenAI's `previous_response_id`, every earlier input in the chain is billed again, and stored responses expire after 30 days by default, so the provider is not your system of record.

**What you meter.** Bill from the provider's usage fields, all of them. On Anthropic, total input is `cache_read_input_tokens + cache_creation_input_tokens + input_tokens`; code that reads only `input_tokens` sees just the tokens after the last cache breakpoint and under-bills cached traffic.

**How usage reaches billing.** Write a usage event per request with an idempotency key (the request ID), aggregate per tenant, and send to the billing system asynchronously. Stripe processes meter events asynchronously and allows cancellations only within 24 hours, so reconcile before you send, not after.

When the provider fails, the backend needs a circuit breaker per model, a fallback that is labelled as degraded in the response and the logs, and a rule that spend-limit errors are not retried. The fallback path is evaluated like the primary, or it will fail when you need it.

## Multimodal ingestion

A file uploader for an AI chat app is an ingestion pipeline with a user waiting. Upload straight to object storage with a signed URL, scan, then process asynchronously: split pages, extract text where it exists, render images only where needed, and show the user progress. Check limits before you call a model: Claude takes up to 32 MB and 600 pages per request. Estimate tokens per page before sending a document into context, and keep extracted text so follow-up questions do not re-send the file.

## Where the classic model stays in charge

**Fraud detection.** A trained model on transaction features scores every payment in the synchronous path, where latency and calibration matter and labelled outcomes exist. The LLM works next to it: summarizing a case for an analyst, extracting features from free-text notes, drafting the customer message. It does not make the block or allow decision on its own, because it cannot be calibrated against outcomes the way the classifier can.

**Recommendation with LLM re-ranking.** Retrieval and a classic ranker narrow millions of items to a short list; an LLM can re-rank that list or explain it. Cost scales with the length of the list times the requests, so the list stays short, results are cached per segment, and the classic ranker serves alone when the LLM is slow or down.

## When this is wrong

The latency table is one vendor's example, and adding stage P95s gives a pessimistic bound because the tails rarely line up. Measure your own stages. The audio token rates and prices are OpenAI's as of 28 Oct 2026; other providers count audio differently, and per-minute products move the cost into a different shape.

The cascade pattern assumes most traffic is easy. A platform under a coordinated attack sends more items to the expensive stage, so size that stage for the bad day, with a queue and a policy for what is visible while it waits. And the advice to keep classic models in charge of fraud and ranking holds where you have labelled outcomes; where you have none, an LLM may be the only model you can start with, and the job becomes collecting the labels.

What I would say in the room: "I budget the critical path stage by stage at P95, overlap what I can, and make the cheap check run on everything and the expensive one only on what it cannot decide."
