## What this page is for

A component choice in an AI design (which retriever, which model, which cache) usually changes cost or latency by a factor. A scoping answer changes which components exist at all. "The assistant can issue refunds" adds an approval step, idempotency keys and an audit trail; "customer data may not leave the EU" removes most of the model menu. This page lists the questions whose answers do that, and the branch each one opens. It is a reference to return to before a design round or a design review, not a script.

SPIDER's phases add to 43 minutes and give evaluation its own five. Hello Interview's framework has no evaluation phase, because a general system design round has no output that is fluent and wrong. Hello Interview also advises skipping capacity estimates at the start and doing the arithmetic only when it changes a decision, which suits AI designs, where the number that matters early is cost per conversation, not queries per second.

Both frameworks limit the list: Hello Interview asks for the top three functional requirements and three to five non-functional ones, quantified where possible. Eight questions follow; in a round, the ones whose answer the prompt already implies are stated as assumptions instead of asked.

## The questions and the branches they open

**1. Who is the user, and who sees the output first?** An end customer reading an answer directly, an internal agent who edits a draft before sending, or a developer calling an API. If a human edits before anything reaches a customer, that human is the main guardrail and the design spends its effort on draft quality and edit-distance measurement. If the output goes straight to customers, the design needs output checks, a handoff path and per-topic monitoring.

**2. What may the system do, not only say?** Read-only answers, drafts, or actions such as refunds, plan changes and account edits. Actions move the risk from wording to side effects. The model proposes a tool call and deterministic code decides: the executor checks arguments against policy, requires approval above a threshold, attaches an idempotency key and writes an audit row. Read-only systems skip all of that.

**3. What does a wrong answer cost, and who pays it?** A wrong film suggestion costs a click. A wrong refund costs money; a wrong statement about a bank fee may cost a regulatory finding. The answer sets the error budget, the eval set size needed to show the system meets it, and whether a human approves before the output takes effect. The Predict block below makes the arithmetic concrete.

**4. What is the source of truth, and who owns its freshness?** Help-centre articles, a product database, policy documents, the user's own files. Documents point to retrieval; live records point to tool calls; most support designs need both. Freshness ownership decides whether a wrong answer from a stale article is the assistant team's incident or the docs team's, and whether answers cite a document version.

**5. What are the latency and cost targets?** A chat reply can stream while the user reads; a voice turn has to start speaking quickly; a nightly batch job has hours. Ask for cost as a ceiling per conversation or per task, not per token, because the architecture choice (a single call, an agent loop, several agents) changes the number of calls per conversation. A tight ceiling opens model routing, caching of stable prefixes and smaller models for classification steps.

**6. What data may the system read, and where may it be processed?** Per-user permissions on documents mean the retriever filters by the caller's access at query time, not by an index built from everything. Residency limits restrict which providers and regions can serve the model, and decide where conversation logs live and for how long. A requirement that logs keep no personal data conflicts with the debugging and eval loop, and that conflict is better named in minute three than discovered in the deep dive.

**7. How will success be measured, and against what?** One success metric (resolution without a human, task completion), at least one guardrail metric (re-contact on the same issue, escalation after an answer, complaint rate), and a comparison group with stable assignment. A success metric with no guardrail rewards a system that closes conversations by being confidently wrong; a guardrail with no comparison group cannot separate the launch from the season.

**8. What exists that cannot change?** An incumbent ticketing system, an identity provider, a data warehouse schema, a team that owns the help centre. Each fixed system becomes an adapter in the design and a limit on measurement: if the ticketing system cannot store the assistant's conversation ID, re-contacts are matched by email and every guardrail number carries that bias.

## Turning failure cost into a design branch

<Predict id="sys-framework-error-budget" question="Scenario: 50,000 answers a day, each wrong answer costs $40 on average in remediation, and the business accepts at most $2,000 a day of that cost. What is the highest error rate, in percent, the system may have?" answer={0.1} tolerance={0.02} unit="%">
<p>$2,000 / $40 = 50 wrong answers a day; 50 / 50,000 = <b>0.1%</b>. Now ask what it takes to show that offline. If the true error rate were exactly 0.1%, the chance of seeing zero errors in n independent cases is 0.999<sup>n</sup>; for that chance to fall below 5%, n ≥ ln 0.05 / ln 0.999 ≈ 2,995. So a clean run on about 3,000 labelled cases is the smallest offline evidence for the budget at 95% confidence, before any per-topic split. Few teams can label 3,000 cases before launch, which is why a budget this tight usually turns into a design change instead: a human approves the costly class of outputs, or the system refuses that class and hands off.</p>
</Predict>

The same arithmetic with a $1 cost per wrong answer gives a budget of 2,000 wrong answers a day, a 4% error rate, and an offline set of a few hundred cases is enough to see whether the system is near it. One scoping answer moved the design from "ship with monitoring" to "approval or refusal on the costly class".

## The rubric this track self-rates against

No public source names a rubric the large companies share, so this one is the track's own. Every design problem in the track is self-rated against its four parts.

**Problem navigation.** The questions above whose answers change the design were asked or stated as assumptions, and the answers visibly changed the design.

**Solution design.** The request path works end to end (authenticate, load state, gather context, call the model, check the output, act or reply, log), each component has a reason, and the model sits inside deterministic code.

**Technical depth.** At least one component, two at senior level per the Hello Interview write-up, is discussed at the depth of someone who has operated it: the cache key, the eval set's construction, the approval threshold and who sets it.

**Decision and communication.** Trade-offs are stated as costs ("this costs X to buy Y"), the chosen option names what it gives up, and something is written down: a risk note, a rollout plan with rollback triggers or a decision memo.

## In the room

<Callout kind="interview" label="Interview question">
"Design an assistant that answers employees' HR questions. Legal says no conversation may be stored with the employee's name, launch is in six weeks, and HR wants to see which questions it gets wrong."
</Callout>

<Depth>
<Fragment slot="short">
<p>The storage rule and the eval requirement conflict, and I resolve it by storing pseudonymised conversations under a key HR cannot reverse, with names and identifiers stripped before logging and a 30-day retention I would confirm with legal. HR reviews a weekly sample of wrong answers in that form. The assistant is read-only and cites the policy document version; questions about the employee's own case (pay, leave balance, disputes) hand off to an HR partner instead of being answered, which also keeps those conversations out of the model logs. What I give up: debugging a single employee's complaint end to end, and answers about personal cases.</p>
</Fragment>
<Fragment slot="long">
<p>The scoping questions that change the design here are 2, 3, 6 and 7. Read-only, because an HR assistant that edits records needs approvals and audit HR has not asked for. A wrong answer about policy costs a correction; a wrong answer about one employee's case can cost a grievance, so personal cases hand off.</p>
<p>The conflict is between question 6 (no stored names) and question 7 (HR wants to see failures). Three options: store nothing and measure only thumbs, which leaves HR blind; store everything, which legal refuses; or store redacted, pseudonymised conversations with short retention. I pick the third. Its failure mode is redaction that misses identifiers inside free text, so the redaction step itself has an eval set and a sampling audit, and the retention limit bounds the damage when it misses.</p>
<p>The six weeks decide scope: policy questions only at launch, personal-case handoff from day one, and the weekly wrong-answer review as the first artefact HR receives.</p>
</Fragment>

## When this is wrong

The order is a default. Some interviewers open with a component ("how would you cache this?") and want scoping folded into the answer; research-flavoured rounds can spend the whole time on one mechanism, where these questions take a minute. The SPIDER and Hello Interview minutes come from a community guide and a prep site, and the rubric is this track's own; none of them is a company's published rubric.

The list is also incomplete by design. Multi-tenant products add questions about isolation between tenants and noisy neighbours; agent products add the question of how long a task may run and what it may spend. Eight questions are the ones that recur across the design problems in this track, not a closed set.
