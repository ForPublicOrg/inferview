## The unit of a design answer is a decision

A diagram describes a system. A decision says which of two workable systems you would build, what the other one would have given you, and what evidence would make you switch. Design reviews on a team produce decisions, and the AI design round at staff level asks for the same thing under time pressure.

In an AI system the hard trade-off rarely lives in the boxes. It lives between two measurements that disagree, or between a measurement and a constraint the team cannot move: a regulator, a date, a system owned by someone else. The rest of this lesson works one such case three times, changing only the constraint.

## The case: resolution up, re-contacts up

Scenario, constructed for this lesson. A subscription software company runs a support assistant in its web app; conversations it cannot resolve go to a human queue in the ticketing system. A new version launched two weeks ago with a new retrieval index and a new prompt. The weekly numbers, on 10,000 conversations a week:

The success metric rose and the guardrail metric nearly doubled. Both teams have a story. The assistant team says human load fell by 740 contacts a week and resolutions that held rose by the same 740 (the two always move together here, because every conversation ends as either a held resolution or a human contact). The support operations team says the assistant now sends 780 people a week back to support, up from 320.

## What the numbers do not settle

Three facts are missing, and each one can flip the decision.

**What a re-contact costs.** A re-contact is not an ordinary ticket: the user arrives having already been given an answer, and the human has to undo it. If a re-contact takes 1.5 times the handle time of a fresh escalation, the break-even rate drops from 29.2% to 21.5%; at twice the handle time it drops to 17.7%, and the 15% measured is within three points of erasing the workload gain. The handle-time ratio is a number to request from support operations, not to assume.

**What was wrong in the 460 extra re-contacts.** A user who asks again because the answer was incomplete is an annoyance. A user told the wrong cancellation terms, who then cancels, is harm, and harm does not net against resolutions elsewhere. The analysis is a labelled sample of the new re-contacts sorted by failure type and by topic. When the regression concentrates in a few topics, a partial rollback becomes possible.

**Whether the change caused it.** Without a holdout the comparison is before and after, and anything that changed in those two weeks (a price change, a release, a seasonal spike) is mixed into it. If the launch had a stable-hash holdout, the comparison is between arms over the same weeks. If not, a holdout can be started now on a slice of traffic, at the cost of a week or two of waiting.

The options, each defensible:

1. **Roll back everything.** Restores the old re-contact rate at once and costs the 740 held resolutions a week. Right when the sample shows harm spread across topics, or when nobody can say where the regression lives.
2. **Keep it and fix forward.** Keeps the gain, accepts 780 re-contacts a week until the fix lands. Right when the sample shows annoyance and no harm, and the fix has an owner and a date.
3. **Roll back by topic.** Route the topics where re-contacts concentrate to the old version or straight to a human, keep the rest. Keeps most of the gain; costs a routing rule someone has to maintain and a topic classifier that will misroute some conversations.

The decision is written down with its reversal condition: "We route billing and cancellation to the old version from Thursday. We keep the new version elsewhere. If the labelled sample of non-billing re-contacts shows any case of a wrong policy statement, we roll back everything. Owner: the assistant team lead; support operations signs off on the sample."

## The same evidence under three constraints

**A regulator.** Make the company a bank, and make the regression concentrate in questions about fees and disputes. Now a wrong answer is a statement to a customer about their account that a regulator can ask to see. Option 2 is gone: harm cannot be traded against resolutions elsewhere, and a fix-forward plan does not undo statements already made. The decision becomes option 3 for the regulated topics immediately, with option 1 if the topic boundary cannot be drawn reliably, plus a step the unregulated version did not need: finding and contacting the users who received the wrong statement, which requires that each conversation logs the retrieved sources and the answer as sent. The cost is the gain on those topics and a remediation workload that may exceed the 740 contacts saved.

**A deadline.** Make the annual renewal period start in three weeks, when contact volume rises and the support team is already short. Rolling back adds 740 human contacts a week; at a scenario handle time of 12 minutes that is 740 × 12 / 60 = 148 agent-hours a week, about 3.7 full-time agents at 40 hours, which the team does not have. Option 1 now has a cost measured in queue time for every customer during renewals. The decision leans to option 3 with a narrower topic list and a stated risk: the remaining re-contacts are accepted for the renewal period, in writing, by the support director who owns that queue.

**A system that cannot be rewritten.** Make the ticketing system an incumbent the company cannot change this year, with no field for the assistant's conversation ID. Re-contact is then measured by matching the customer's email within seven days, which misses users who come back on a different channel and over-counts users with two unrelated problems. The 8% and 15% are estimates with an unknown bias, and the bias may differ between versions if the new one changed who escalates. The decision changes from "act on the number" to "act on the labelled sample, and add a measurement that does not depend on the ticketing system": the assistant's own log of users who return to the assistant on the same topic, plus a short post-resolution check. The cost is two weeks of acting on weaker evidence.

The diagram is the same in all three. The decision, the written artefact and the person who signs it are different each time.

## The arithmetic, once

```python
# Weekly human load and held resolutions for two assistant versions.
# All inputs are scenario values; recontact_weight is re-contact handle time / escalation handle time.
def weekly(conversations, resolved_rate, recontact_rate, recontact_weight=1.0):
    resolved = conversations * resolved_rate
    escalated = conversations - resolved
    recontacts = resolved * recontact_rate
    human_load = escalated + recontacts * recontact_weight
    durable = resolved - recontacts
    return round(human_load), round(durable)

def break_even_recontact(conversations, before, after_resolved_rate, weight=1.0):
    human_before, _ = weekly(conversations, *before, recontact_weight=weight)
    resolved_after = conversations * after_resolved_rate
    return (human_before - (conversations - resolved_after)) / (resolved_after * weight)

for w in (1.0, 1.5, 2.0):
    print(f"weight {w}: before {weekly(10_000, 0.40, 0.08, w)}, "
          f"after {weekly(10_000, 0.52, 0.15, w)}, "
          f"break-even {break_even_recontact(10_000, (0.40, 0.08), 0.52, w):.1%}")
```

```text
weight 1.0: before (6320, 3680), after (5580, 4420), break-even 29.2%
weight 1.5: before (6480, 3680), after (5970, 4420), break-even 21.5%
weight 2.0: before (6640, 3680), after (6360, 4420), break-even 17.7%
```

The human-load figures are weighted, so they are in units of one escalation's handle time.

## What a decision under conflict contains

Across the three versions the written decision has the same parts: the options considered, each with its cost in the unit the owner cares about (contacts, agent-hours, regulated statements); the evidence the decision rests on and the evidence still missing; what the chosen option gives up; the measurement that would reverse it, with a threshold; and the person who owns the risk, who is not always the person who made the change. A design answer that ends in such a decision covers what a long component walk-through covers, plus the part a team actually argues about.

The techinterview.org write-up describes the applied-AI design round as designing an eval and serving path around a model you do not own, and reports the support-assistant prompt with its second sentence: "How do you know it's helping and not making things worse?" The case above is that second sentence after launch, when the two measurements disagree.

## When this is wrong

Re-contact within seven days is a guardrail with known blind spots. It misses users who give up, users who act on a wrong answer without coming back, and, with a ticketing system that cannot link conversations, users who return on another channel. A guardrail that stayed flat is not proof of no harm; a labelled sample is still needed for any topic where a wrong answer has a cost outside the support queue.

The break-even arithmetic treats every conversation as interchangeable. If the new version resolves the easy conversations it used to escalate and fails on a different, harder set, the counts can look fine while the users with the most at stake are worse served. Splitting every number by topic is the guard against that.

A question that asks for one component in depth, such as a batching scheduler, is answered with mechanism, and a decision memo about a support queue answers a question nobody asked. The 40%, 52%, 8% and 15% above are scenario values chosen to make the trade-off visible.
