## Minute two, and there is already a vector database

Scenario, not a real interview: the prompt is "design a support assistant for our product". The candidate says "sure", draws a box labelled *vector DB*, another labelled *LLM*, an arrow between them, and starts on chunk sizes. At minute 30 the interviewer asks whether the assistant can issue refunds. It can, as it turns out, and the design has no approval step, no audit log and no way to tell whether answers got better or worse after a prompt change. The diagram was fine. The answer was not, because it solved a problem nobody had stated.

AI design rounds punish this more than classic ones. In a URL-shortener round the components are known and the debate is about scale. In an AI round the hard parts are what the model is allowed to do, what it costs per request, and how you would know it is wrong. Those are requirements, so they come first.

## A design round is a design review for a system that does not exist yet

You have sat in design reviews. Someone presents, reviewers ask "what happens when this fails?", "what does it cost?", "how do we know it works?", and a good presenter has answered most of those before the slide that draws the boxes. The interview is the same meeting, compressed to 45 minutes, with one reviewer who already knows the questions.

Compression is the problem. A rough budget makes it concrete. One community framework for AI design rounds, SPIDER, spends 43 of 45 minutes like this:

Hello Interview's general framework has a similar shape for non-AI rounds: about 5 minutes of requirements, 2 on core entities, 5 on the API, 10 to 15 on the high-level design and about 10 on deep dives. The difference worth noticing is that SPIDER gives evaluation its own slot. In a classic round nobody asks how you test that the URL shortener returns the right URL. In an AI round the answer can be fluent and wrong, so "how do you know" is a design question, not a QA question.

## The rubric this track uses

No public source we found states one rubric that the large companies share, so treat this one as ours. It has four parts, and every design problem in this track is self-rated against them.

**Problem navigation.** You asked the questions that change the design, and you said which assumptions you were making when you could not ask. **Solution design.** The request path works end to end, each component has a reason, and the model sits inside deterministic code rather than the other way round. **Technical excellence.** At least one component is discussed at the depth of someone who has run it: cache keys, idempotency, the shape of the eval set, the rate-limit headers. **Communication.** You narrated trade-offs as trade-offs ("this costs X to buy Y"), checked in with the interviewer, and drove the time.

## Clarify requirements and success metrics

Five questions change an AI design more than any traffic number.

1. **What is the source of truth?** Help-centre articles, a product database, a policy PDF, the user's own files. This decides whether you retrieve, call tools, or both, and who owns freshness.
2. **What may the system do, not just say?** Read-only answers, drafts a human sends, or actions such as refunds and account changes. The action boundary decides whether you need approvals, idempotency keys and an audit trail.
3. **Who is wrong when it is wrong?** A wrong movie suggestion costs nothing. A wrong refund costs money, and a wrong dosage answer costs more. Failure cost sets the eval bar and the human fallback.
4. **What are the latency and cost ceilings?** A chat reply can stream while the user reads; a voice turn has to start speaking almost at once. A vendor writing about voice agents targets under 800 ms to first audio at P50. Ask for a cost ceiling per conversation, not per token.
5. **How will we know it helps?** Name one success metric and at least one guardrail metric. For support: resolution rate without a human, with re-contact within seven days and escalation-after-bad-answer as guardrails. A success metric with no guardrail rewards an assistant that closes tickets by being confidently wrong.

Ask these, write the answers on the board, and move on. Five minutes is enough if you do not negotiate each one.

## Back-of-envelope in two minutes

You will do real capacity work in the next lesson. In the framework you need one number early: roughly what one request costs, because it decides between a single prompt, an agent loop and a multi-agent system before you draw anything.

Two published ratios carry most of the weight. Anthropic reports that agents use about 4 times the tokens of chat and multi-agent systems about 15 times. And output tokens cost 5 times input tokens on several current price lists, for example $2 in and $10 out per million on Claude Sonnet 5 (checked 28 Oct 2026). So "let's make it multi-agent" is a request to multiply the bill by about 15 before any caching. Say that out loud when you propose it, then say what you get for it.

```python
# Back-of-envelope: what the architecture choice does to the daily bill.
# Token multipliers are from Anthropic's multi-agent write-up (agents ~4x chat, multi-agent ~15x).
# Prices are USD per million tokens, checked 2026-10-28; pass them in, they change.
def daily_cost(sessions, chat_tokens, multiplier, price_in, price_out, out_share=0.1):
    tokens = sessions * chat_tokens * multiplier
    out = tokens * out_share
    return ((tokens - out) * price_in + out * price_out) / 1e6

for name, mult in [("chat", 1), ("single agent", 4), ("multi-agent", 15)]:
    cost = daily_cost(10_000, 3_000, mult, price_in=2.0, price_out=10.0)
    print(f"{name:13s} ${cost:,.0f}/day")
```

```text
chat          $84/day
single agent  $336/day
multi-agent   $1,260/day
```

The 10,000 sessions, 3,000 tokens per chat and 10 percent output share are assumptions to state, not facts. The ratio between the lines is the point, and it survives any reasonable choice of assumptions.

## Data flow, components and the model in the loop

Draw the request path before the boxes. For most AI products it reads: authenticate, load conversation state, decide what context is needed, fetch it, call the model, check the output, act or reply, log everything. Each verb becomes a component from a short palette: a **router** that picks a model or a path, a **retriever** and **reranker**, a **cache** in front of either, the **model**, a **tool executor** that runs side effects, **guardrails** on input and output, a **judge** that scores samples offline or online, and a **human queue**.

The habit that separates strong answers: the model proposes, deterministic code disposes. The model can decide to call `issue_refund`; the tool executor checks the amount against policy, requires approval above a threshold, attaches an idempotency key, and writes the audit row. Compilers work the same way. The parser accepts what the grammar allows and the type checker refuses the rest, however confident the input looks.

## Eval plan, failure modes and human fallback

State the eval plan as three loops. **Offline:** a small set of real queries with expected outcomes, run on every prompt or model change; start with about twenty, as Anthropic did, and add every production failure you triage. **Pre-release:** the same set run several times, because agents are not deterministic; tau-bench's pass^k asks whether all k runs of the same task succeed, which is the number a customer feels. **Online:** the success and guardrail metrics from scoping, sampled transcripts scored by a judge you have checked against human labels, and an alert on the guardrail.

Then name the failure modes and what the user sees for each one: retrieval finds nothing (say so, offer escalation), the model contradicts the source (output check against cited passages), a tool fails permanently (stop and tell the user, do not retry), the provider is down (degraded answer or queue, labelled as degraded). "Escalate to a human" is a component with a queue, a handoff summary and a staffing assumption, not a sentence.

## Driving the interview

Say the plan in the first minute: "I'll spend five minutes on scope, sketch the path, then go deep on retrieval and the refund flow, and finish with evals and failure modes. Stop me if you'd rather go elsewhere." Interviewers redirect less when they know where you are going. When you choose between options, use the shape "A costs this, buys that; B costs this, buys that; given the requirement we wrote down, A". When you run long on one component, say so and move.

## The 15-minute fast path

Some loops give you one design question inside a longer conversation. Compress, do not skip: two minutes on the five scoping questions, three on the request path, five on the one component that carries the risk (usually the action boundary or retrieval), three on the eval plan, two on failure modes and cost. The first phase to drop is the long tail of components you would add later. Evaluation is never the phase to drop.

## When this is wrong

A framework is a default order, not a script. Some interviewers open with a deep dive ("tell me how you'd cache this") and want the scope implied; follow them and fold the scoping questions into your answer. Research-flavoured rounds at labs can spend the whole time on one component, such as a batching scheduler, where the five scoping questions take thirty seconds.

The minutes in the SPIDER table come from a community guide and the rubric above is ours. Neither is a company's published rubric, and you should not quote them to an interviewer as one. The 4x and 15x token ratios come from one company's research agent on its own tasks; your product's ratios will differ, so measure them once you have traffic.

What I would say in the room: "I'll scope the action boundary and the eval plan before I draw boxes, because those decide the design more than traffic does."
