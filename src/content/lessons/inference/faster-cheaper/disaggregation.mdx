## The interference being removed

In one pool, every engine step mixes work from both phases. Continuous batching with chunked prefill adds a slice of some waiting prompt to the decode batch, so the step that produces the next token for every streaming user also carries a block of prefill compute. Decode is bound by memory reads and prefill by FLOPs, so the prefill slice lengthens a step that would otherwise cost one weight read.

A toy model with the roofline numbers shows the size. Llama 3 70B in FP8 on four H100 SXM with tensor parallelism: the weight read is 70e9 bytes over 4 × 3.35 TB/s = 5.2 ms per step, and each token costs 2 × 70e9 FLOPs over 4 × 1.979e15 dense FP8 FLOP/s = 0.0177 ms. A decode batch of 64 adds 1.1 ms of compute, well under the weight read, so the step costs about 5.2 ms plus its KV reads.

<p>The step now carries 2,048 + 64 = 2,112 tokens of compute: 2,112 × 0.0177 ms = <b>37.4 ms</b>, which exceeds the 5.2 ms weight read, so the step is compute bound. Every user decoding on that replica waits about seven times longer for this token. A 32,000-token document admitted in 2,048-token chunks takes 16 such steps.</p>

That is the interference. Chunked prefill bounds it, because `max_num_batched_tokens` caps the tokens per step; vLLM's tuning guide says smaller values such as 2,048 give better inter-token latency and larger ones better time to first token. It cannot remove it. DistServe's analysis puts it as chunking trading TTFT for TPOT: smaller chunks lengthen every prefill, and each chunk re-reads the KV of the chunks before it, so a chunked prefill does O(N²) KV loads where an unchunked one does O(N). Both latency targets are being paid from the same step budget.

## What splitting buys

Disaggregated serving runs prefill on one pool of GPUs and decode on another, and moves each request's KV from the first to the second when its prefill ends. Decode steps then carry no prefill, so time per output token depends only on decode batch size and context; prefill instances run large compute-bound batches without anyone waiting on their steps. Each pool can also have its own parallelism and its own hardware: Splitwise argues the token phase does not need the newest GPU's compute and can run on lower-power, cheaper parts.

DistServe frames the objective as goodput: the request rate a GPU sustains while meeting both a TTFT and a TPOT target, with targets per application (in its Table 1, for example, a chatbot on OPT-66B at 2.5 s TTFT and 0.15 s TPOT, code completion at 0.125 s TTFT). Against the systems it compared, it reports serving 7.4 times more requests or meeting a 12.6 times tighter SLO, with over 90% of requests inside the targets. Splitwise reports 1.4 times the throughput at 20% lower cost, or 2.35 times the throughput at the same cost and power. NVIDIA reports, for Dynamo, more than double the throughput for Llama 70B on Hopper with vLLM in FP8 at 3K input and 50 output tokens, and up to 30 times the requests for DeepSeek-R1 on GB200 NVL72, the latter labelled a projection.

vLLM's documentation states the other side flatly: disaggregated prefill does not improve throughput, and the reasons to use it are tuning TTFT and inter-token latency separately and controlling tail inter-token latency. Both are true. The papers measure requests served within latency targets; raw tokens per second with no latency target is at best unchanged, because the same FLOPs and bytes are spent and a transfer is added. A split pays when the targets, not the hardware, are the binding constraint.

## What splitting costs

Every request moves its KV between pools. For Llama 3 70B one token of BF16 KV is 2 × 80 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 × 2 bytes = 327,680 bytes, so a 32,000-token document carries 10.5 GB. DistServe's own example is a 512-token OPT-66B request at about 1.13 GB, and at 10 requests per second that is 11.3 GB/s, about 90 Gbps, to keep transfer off the critical path. Placement decides whether that is cheap. DistServe places paired prefill and decode instances on the same node so the transfer uses NVLink (900 GB/s per H100 SXM on NVIDIA's page) and measures transfer at under 0.1% of total latency, with over 95% of requests seeing less than 30 ms. Across nodes, at the 50 GB/s per GPU of InfiniBand that DeepSeek reports for its cluster, four GPUs each sending their tensor-parallel shard move 10.5 GB in 10.5 / 200 = 52 ms, added to that request's time to first token unless the transfer overlaps prefill layer by layer.

The transfer is also a new failure surface. A prefill instance can die after computing KV and before handing it over, a decode instance can be full when the KV arrives, and the connector between them (NIXL, LMCache, Mooncake and others in vLLM's list, where the feature is still marked experimental) is a component to version, monitor and upgrade. And there are now two pools to size. The right ratio of prefill to decode GPUs is a function of the traffic mix, input lengths against output lengths, so a ratio that is right on Monday is wrong when a customer starts sending long documents. Dynamo's planner exists for this reason: it watches capacity against SLOs, decides whether to serve requests with or without disaggregation, and moves GPUs between the phases.

Granularity bites on small fleets. Sixteen GPUs at tensor parallel 4 are four instances, so the only splits are 1:3, 2:2 and 3:1, a 33% step in capacity between options.

## Chunked prefill is the one-pool alternative

Sarathi-Serve is the case for keeping one pool. It splits prefills into roughly equal chunks and schedules them without stalling ongoing decodes, and against vLLM of that time, under tail-latency constraints, reports 2.6 times serving capacity for Mistral-7B on one A100, up to 3.7 times for Yi-34B on two A100s, and up to 5.6 times for Falcon-180B with pipeline parallelism, where uniform batches also shrink pipeline bubbles. Chunked prefill is now on by default in vLLM V1. It keeps every GPU able to do either phase, needs no transfer and no ratio, and degrades smoothly under a shifting mix. Its limit is the one above: every chunk is paid by every decoding user on that replica.

## Deciding for a mixed workload

Scenario: sixteen H100 SXM, Llama 3 70B in FP8 at tensor parallel 4, so four instances. Traffic is 20 chat requests per second with 2,000 input and 300 output tokens, plus 1 document request per second with 32,000 input and 500 output tokens. Targets are p99 time per output token of 40 ms for everyone and a document time to first token of 8 s. The toy model from the first section, extended with KV reads, gives:

Prefill demand is 20 × 2,000 + 32,000 = 72,000 tokens per second, which at 0.0177 ms per token is 1.27 instance-seconds of compute every second. Decode concurrency at the 40 ms bound, by Little's law, is 20 × 300 × 0.04 = 240 chat sequences and 20 document sequences, holding about 1.16 million tokens of KV, 380 GB. One instance has 4 × 80 GB − 70 GB − 20 GB reserved = 230 GB, about 702,000 tokens.

One pool of four instances: each holds about 290,000 tokens of KV, so the base decode step is 5.2 ms of weights plus 290,250 × 327,680 bytes over 13.4 TB/s = 7.1 ms of KV, 12.3 ms. Each instance must also absorb 18,000 prefill tokens per second. If the scheduler spreads them evenly, each step carries a chunk c with c / (12.3 + 0.0177c) = 18 tokens per ms, which gives c of about 325 tokens and a step of about 18 ms. The largest chunk that keeps a step under 40 ms is about 1,565 tokens, so a document admitted at that size reaches its first token in about 20 steps, 0.8 s.

Split two and two: the decode instances hold about 580,000 tokens each, so their step is 5.2 + 14.2 = 19.4 ms with no prefill in it. The prefill instances supply up to 2 × 56,500 = 113,000 tokens per second at peak against 72,000 demand. A document's prefill is 566 ms of compute plus the KV transfer.

Under this model the one pool wins: a lower time per output token (about 18 ms against 19.4), twice the KV headroom (2.8 million tokens against 1.4 million for decode, with 1.16 million needed), no transfer, and no ratio to manage. The split's decode pool runs at 83% of its KV capacity, so a rise of about 38% in document traffic fills it: (1.40 − 1.16) million spare tokens over the 645,000 that documents hold now. The decision is to tune one pool, sweep `max_num_batched_tokens` at the target load, and write down the trigger for splitting: p99 time per output token over target at a chunk size that still meets the document TTFT. That happens when prefill demand grows until every step is near its chunk limit, or when the TPOT target tightens towards the base step, at which point neither layout meets it on sixteen GPUs and the split becomes the way to add decode capacity without adding prefill.

A third option sits between them: route documents to a dedicated pool that runs both phases with chunking, and keep chat on its own pool. It isolates chat from long prefills without a KV transfer, at the cost of fragmenting capacity and a routing rule.

## When this is wrong

The toy model takes FLOPs and bytes at the spec-sheet rate and ignores attention compute, communication and scheduler overhead. Real kernels reach a fraction of peak, chunked prefill pays the quadratic KV reloads that make later chunks of a long prompt slower than earlier ones, and Little's law at the bound overstates concurrency when actual TPOT is lower. The model ranks the options and finds the trigger; the numbers to plan capacity with come from a load test at the target mix.

The published gains are against the baselines of their time. DistServe and Sarathi-Serve both compared against vLLM versions that predate chunked prefill by default and V1's scheduler, so the gap to a tuned single pool today is smaller than the headline multiples, and vendor figures such as Dynamo's are measured on the vendor's stack and configurations.

Disaggregation also interacts with every other lever in this module. Prefix caching shrinks prefill and the case for a split; speculative decoding runs on the decode pool and changes its batch; quantising the KV cache halves the transfer. Re-run the decision when any of them changes.
