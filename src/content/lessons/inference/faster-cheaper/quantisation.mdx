## Three formats, three terms of the step cost

A quantised deployment makes three choices that are often reported as one. The weight format sets the bytes read per parameter. The matmul format sets which tensor-core rate the GEMMs run at. The KV cache format sets the bytes held and read per cached token. The naming used by Kurtic et al. separates the first two: W4A16 stores 4-bit weights and dequantises them to 16-bit inside the kernel, so the multiply runs at the BF16 rate; W8A8 stores 8-bit weights and also quantises activations, so the multiply runs at the 8-bit rate.

Each choice moves a different term from the roofline lesson. A decode step at small batch is bound by weight reads, so W4A16 cuts its floor to roughly a quarter of BF16 and W8A8 to a half. Prefill and large-batch decode are bound by FLOPs, where W4A16 changes nothing because the arithmetic is still BF16, and W8A8 doubles the ceiling: NVIDIA lists 3,958 FP8 TFLOPS against 1,979 BF16 on the H100 SXM (both with sparsity, so the ratio holds for the dense rates too). The KV format moves the KV-read term of decode and, more often the reason it is chosen, the number of tokens that fit.

That split explains the deployment advice in Kurtic et al., measured on vLLM: W4A16 is the cheapest for synchronous, low-concurrency serving, and W8A8 wins under asynchronous continuous batching, where the step is compute bound.

## Bits per weight, counted with the scales

A format is low-bit values plus the scales that turn them back into numbers, and the scales are part of the bytes read, so the real bits per weight are the payload plus the scale bits divided by the block size:

The 70B column is a nominal 70e9 parameters times bits over 8. Smaller blocks track local outliers better and cost more scale bits; that and the scale format are what separate MXFP4 from NVFP4.

GGUF is a file format for GGML executors such as llama.cpp, not a quantisation method; Q4_K, Q6_K and the rest are block types stored in it.

## How each method picks the rounded values

GPTQ quantises one layer at a time and uses approximate second-order information from calibration data to choose rounded values that keep the layer's output close to the original, compensating each rounding error in the weights not yet quantised. The paper quantises a 175B model to 3 or 4 bits in about four GPU hours and reports about 3.25 times end-to-end speedup over FP16 on an A100 and 4.5 times on an A6000.

AWQ starts from the observation that about 1% of weights matter far more than the rest, and that they are found from activation magnitudes, not weight magnitudes: a weight multiplied by a large activation contributes a large output error when rounded. Instead of keeping those channels in 16 bits, which makes kernels irregular, it multiplies salient input channels by a scale before quantisation and divides the activations by the same scale, an equivalent transformation that leaves the product unchanged and gives the important weights more of the grid.

FP8 keeps a floating-point grid: E4M3 has more precision, E5M2 more range, and E4M3 drops infinities to reach further. A float grid has more points near zero and fewer at the extremes, which matches the shape of weight and activation distributions better than a uniform int8 grid.

The FP4 formats push the block idea down to 4 bits. gpt-oss-120b ships its MoE expert weights in MXFP4, and its config lists the attention layers, the router, the embeddings and the LM head as not converted. The experts hold most of the parameters and so most of the bytes read; the router and attention are small and sensitive.

## Where outliers break a format

Activations in large transformers carry outlier features: a few hidden dimensions with values far larger than the rest, appearing systematically across layers. LLM.int8 handles them by splitting the matmul, sending the outlier dimensions to a 16-bit multiply while more than 99.9% of values stay in 8-bit.

One scale for a whole tensor is set by its largest value, so one outlier channel stretches the grid and the ordinary channels fall into a few buckets around zero:

```python
import random

def quantise(xs, scale):
    return [max(-127, min(127, round(x / scale))) * scale for x in xs]

def mse(a, b):
    return sum((x - y) ** 2 for x, y in zip(a, b)) / len(a)

rng = random.Random(0)
# 4 channels x 256 values; channel 3 carries an outlier feature
rows = [[rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(256)] for _ in range(4)]
rows[3] = [x * 40 for x in rows[3]]

flat = [x for r in rows for x in r]
per_tensor = max(abs(x) for x in flat) / 127
err_tensor = [mse(r, quantise(r, per_tensor)) for r in rows]
err_channel = [mse(r, quantise(r, max(abs(x) for x in r) / 127)) for r in rows]

print("per-tensor  MSE by channel", [f"{e:.2e}" for e in err_tensor])
print("per-channel MSE by channel", [f"{e:.2e}" for e in err_channel])
zeroed = sum(1 for x in rows[0] if round(x / per_tensor) == 0)
print("channel 0 values rounded to zero:", zeroed, "of 256")
# per-tensor  MSE by channel ['5.96e-02', '5.81e-02', '5.65e-02', '5.86e-02']
# per-channel MSE by channel ['6.96e-05', '4.02e-05', '4.07e-05', '5.86e-02']
# channel 0 values rounded to zero: 79 of 256
```

With one scale, the ordinary channels lose about 6% of their variance to rounding and 79 of 256 values in channel 0 become zero; with a scale per channel their error falls by about three orders of magnitude. Weights can take per-channel or per-group scales freely. Activations cannot, because the matmul sums over the activation channels and a different scale per summed term does not factor out of the sum. That is why the practical answers either keep the outlier dimensions in higher precision (LLM.int8), move the scale into the weights (AWQ's equivalent transformation), or use a float format whose grid degrades more gracefully at the extremes (FP8).

## The KV cache is a separate decision

KV quantisation is independent of weight quantisation and is chosen for capacity. Llama 3 70B has 80 layers, 8 KV heads and a head dimension of 8,192 / 64 = 128, so one token of BF16 cache is 2 (K and V) × 80 × 8 × 128 × 2 bytes = 327,680 bytes; FP8 halves it to 163,840.

vLLM's FP8 cache options are `fp8`, `fp8_e4m3` and `fp8_e5m2`. Without calibration every scale is 1.0, which works only while the keys and values happen to sit inside the format's range; the recommended path calibrates scales with llm-compressor on a dataset. Per-head scales are available only with the Flash Attention backend, and with Flash Attention 3 the attention itself runs in FP8, queries included. The docs also say sliding-window layers are more sensitive to KV quantisation and provide `--kv-cache-dtype-skip-layers` to keep chosen layers, or all sliding-window layers, at native precision. Fit KV scales on your own traffic, as for weights: a scale fitted on short English chat may clip the keys of a 100K-token Japanese contract.

## Where the damage shows up

Averages hide it. Kurtic et al. ran over 500,000 evaluations on the Llama-3.1 family and found FP8 W8A8 effectively lossless, well-tuned INT8 W8A8 at 1 to 3% degradation, and W4A16 more competitive than expected. Mekala et al. tested inputs above 64K tokens with five methods on five models: 8-bit lost about 0.8% on average, while 4-bit methods lost up to 59% on long-context inputs, worse when the input was not English. The variance across models is the part to remember: under BNB-nf4, Qwen-2.5 72B held up on a task where Llama-3.1 70B dropped 32%. A published "INT4 is fine for model X" does not transfer to model Y, and a result on short benchmarks does not transfer to 100K-token inputs.

Perplexity is the wrong gate for the same reason. It averages over every token of a generic corpus, so a loss concentrated in long inputs, one language or one task family moves it very little.

## A protocol that compares on your tasks

Run the quantised model and the BF16 reference on the same items with the same sampling settings, and compare per item, not per suite. The paired difference is far tighter than two independent accuracy numbers, and it names the items that moved. Slice the result by input length bucket, by language and by task family, and set the quality floor per slice: "no slice drops more than one point" is a gate; "the average dropped 0.4" is not. Include a slice at the longest context you serve.

Treat weights and KV as a two-by-two design: BF16 or quantised weights against BF16 or quantised cache. When the long-input slice drops, the two-by-two tells you whether the cache or the weights did it, and the fixes differ (recalibrate or skip layers for the cache, a different method or format for the weights). Calibrate GPTQ, AWQ and KV scales on samples of your own traffic, and keep a held-out set that was not used for calibration.

Measure cost at the concurrency you will run.

## A decision for a stated case

Scenario: Llama 3.1 70B on two H100 SXM per replica, serving chat and question answering over documents up to 100K tokens, in English and Japanese, with a floor of no more than one point lost on any slice against BF16.

BF16 weights leave about 30,500 tokens of cache, so they fail the product before quality is measured. W4A16 at 36.1 GB leaves the most memory and the lowest decode floor at small batch, but the long-input, non-English slice is exactly where 4-bit methods have lost the most, and continuous batching at production concurrency erodes its speed advantage. FP8 W8A8 at 70 GB leaves 244,141 BF16 cache tokens, enough for two 100K documents in flight per replica, with the 8-bit results above as the prior. The starting point is FP8 weights and activations with a BF16 cache, then an FP8 cache with calibrated scales tested on the Japanese long-document slice, enabled only if that slice holds. W4A16 stays a candidate for a separate low-concurrency chat pool if its paired comparison passes there.

## When this is wrong

The step model assumes kernels that actually read 4 bits and multiply at the advertised rate. A format without a fast kernel for your GPU dequantises in a slow path and can be slower than BF16; FP8 tensor cores exist on Hopper and later, and FP4 tensor cores on Blackwell (NVIDIA's DGX B200 page lists 72 dense FP4 PFLOPS against 36 dense FP8 for the eight-GPU system), so the same checkpoint has different economics on different hardware. Check the engine's kernel for the exact format, GPU and batch shape before quoting a speedup.

The published comparisons are on specific model families. Kurtic et al. measured Llama-3.1; Mekala et al. measured Llama-3.1 and Qwen-2.5 and found the two families reacting differently to the same method. Mixture-of-experts models, models trained natively in a low-precision format, and models with sliding-window or hybrid attention layers need their own runs.

Quality loss is also not only accuracy. A quantised model can change output length, refusal rate or tool-call formatting without moving a correctness score, and those change cost and downstream parsing. Add them to the paired comparison as their own columns.
