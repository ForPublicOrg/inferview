## The question to answer before the benchmark

Someone proposes a faster GPU, FP8 weights, a bigger batch or speculative decoding. Each of these helps one phase of generation and does little or nothing for the other, and the roofline tells you which before a load test does. Prefill runs every prompt token through the model in one pass. Decode then produces one token per sequence per step, and each step rereads every weight plus that sequence's whole KV cache. The two phases run the same kernels on the same GPU and are bound by different resources.

## Intensity, counted for a transformer

A forward pass costs about 2N FLOPs per token for N parameters (Kaplan et al., section 2.1, plus a context term that matters only at long context; see the last section). A step that pushes t tokens through weights stored in b bytes each does 2Nt FLOPs and reads Nb bytes of weights, so its intensity is 2t / b FLOPs per byte: t in BF16, 2t in FP8.

The H100 SXM's ridge point is peak FLOPs over peak bandwidth. NVIDIA's 1,979 BF16 TFLOPS figure is marked "with sparsity"; the dense rate is half, 989.5 TFLOPS. Divided by 3.35 TB/s that gives a ridge of 295 FLOPs per byte, the figure the JAX scaling book also computes. In FP8 the dense rate doubles to 1,979 TFLOPS and the ridge to 591, while the intensity per token also doubles because each weight is one byte. Both sides move together, so the step crosses the ridge at about 295 tokens either way. FP8 halves the time of both phases and leaves the crossover where it was.

A 2,000-token prompt puts t = 2,000 through each weight read, about seven times past the ridge: compute bound. Decode at batch B puts t = B through each weight read, and B is usually tens of sequences, not hundreds. The scaling book's estimate for an H100 is that generation needs a batch of about 280 before the linear layers become compute bound.

Toy numbers for a 70B model in FP8, 70 GB of weights on one H100 SXM. Prefilling 2,000 tokens takes at least 2 × 70e9 × 2,000 / 1.979e15 = 141 ms. One decode step at batch 1 reads 70 GB at 3.35 TB/s and takes at least 20.9 ms. The GPU prefills 2,000 tokens in the time of seven decode steps.

### Attention reads that batching never shares

The weight read is shared by every sequence in the batch; the KV read is not, because each sequence attends over its own history. Count it for one KV head and one cached token in BF16: the step reads a key and a value of d_head elements each, 4 × d_head bytes. Each of the G query heads that share that KV head does about 2 × d_head FLOPs for the score and 2 × d_head for the weighted value, 4 × G × d_head FLOPs. The intensity is G FLOPs per byte whatever the batch size. Llama 3 70B has 64 query heads over 8 KV heads, so G = 8, roughly 37 times below the ridge. The scaling book reaches the same conclusion: decode attention is bandwidth bound, and grouped-query attention raises its intensity by the query-to-KV head ratio.

## Bytes per decode step, by model

A decode step on one replica reads W + B × C × k bytes: weights W, plus B sequences at C cached tokens of k bytes each. The KV term grows with batch and context, so past some batch it dominates the step, and from there more batch buys little. Setting B × C × k = W gives the batch at which KV reads equal weight reads.

KV per token is 2 × layers × KV heads × head_dim × 2 bytes; the next lesson derives it for architectures where that formula breaks. The weights column uses the size in the model's name, so it is approximate.

The asymptote is the useful number. Tokens per second per replica is B / step time, and as B grows that tends to bandwidth / (C × k), set by the KV term alone. For Llama 3 70B at 8,192 cached tokens with a BF16 cache, no batch size takes one H200's worth of bandwidth past 4.8e12 / 2.68e9 = 1,788 tokens per second, and an H100 past 1,248. Halving k with an FP8 cache doubles the ceiling; a faster GPU raises it by its bandwidth ratio; a bigger batch does not move it.

## Measuring which roof you are under

**Model bandwidth utilisation** is the decode-side utilisation number. Databricks defines achieved bandwidth as (parameter bytes + KV bytes) / TPOT and MBU as that over peak: a 7B model in 16-bit at 14 ms TPOT moves 14 GB in 14 ms, 1 TB/s, which is 50% of a 2 TB/s part. Their measurements at batch 1 reached 60% MBU on 2x H100-80GB and 55% on 4x A100-40GB. On the prefill side the scaling book's guide is that H100s and B200s usually reach about 80 to 85% of claimed peak FLOPs. A floor computed from peak numbers is therefore optimistic by at least 1 / 0.85 in prefill and by roughly 1 / 0.6 in decode.

**Goodput** is the throughput number that respects latency. DistServe defines per-GPU goodput as the maximum request rate served while meeting the SLO attainment goal, 90% in their experiments. For a 13B model on one A100, colocated serving reached about 1.6 requests per second, while prefill alone could sustain 5.6 and decode alone 10. Neither phase was the limit; their interference was. A change that raises tokens per second while pushing more requests past the TTFT or ITL target lowers goodput.

The engines report the phases separately, so a change's effect shows up in the histogram of the phase it targets. vLLM V1 exposes `vllm:time_to_first_token_seconds`, `vllm:inter_token_latency_seconds`, `vllm:request_time_per_output_token_seconds`, `vllm:e2e_request_latency_seconds`, `vllm:request_queue_time_seconds`, `vllm:request_prefill_time_seconds`, `vllm:request_decode_time_seconds`, the gauges `vllm:num_requests_running`, `vllm:num_requests_waiting` and `vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc`, and the counters `vllm:prefix_cache_queries` and `vllm:prefix_cache_hits`. SGLang, with `--enable-metrics`, exposes `sglang:time_to_first_token_seconds`, `sglang:inter_token_latency_seconds`, `sglang:request_time_per_output_token_seconds`, `sglang:e2e_request_latency_seconds`, `sglang:num_running_reqs`, `sglang:num_queue_reqs`, `sglang:token_usage`, `sglang:cache_hit_rate` and `sglang:gen_throughput`.

<Callout kind="gotcha">
Both engines keep two decode-latency histograms. Inter-token latency gets one sample per streamed output, so long responses dominate its percentiles; time per output token gets one sample per finished request. NIM's AIPerf computes ITL as (e2e - TTFT) / (output tokens - 1), excluding the first token, and NVIDIA notes that other tools include it. A p99 from one definition is not comparable with a p99 from another.
</Callout>

## The arithmetic in code

```python
H100 = dict(flops_bf16=989.5e12, flops_fp8=1979e12, bw=3.35e12)

def step_floor_s(gpu, n_params, w_bytes, tokens, kv_bytes_read):
    flops = 2 * n_params * tokens                     # Kaplan: about 2N per token
    peak = gpu['flops_fp8'] if w_bytes == 1 else gpu['flops_bf16']
    t_compute = flops / peak
    t_memory = (n_params * w_bytes + kv_bytes_read) / gpu['bw']
    bound = 'compute' if t_compute > t_memory else 'memory'
    return max(t_compute, t_memory), bound

kv_tok = 2 * 80 * 8 * 128 * 2                          # Llama 3 70B, BF16 cache
print(step_floor_s(H100, 70e9, 1, 2000, 0))            # (0.1414..., 'compute')  prefill 2,000 tokens
print(step_floor_s(H100, 70e9, 1, 1, 0))               # (0.0208..., 'memory')   decode, batch 1
print(step_floor_s(H100, 70e9, 1, 32, 32 * 8192 * kv_tok))  # (0.0465..., 'memory') batch 32 at 8K
```

The last line is a single-GPU floor that ignores whether 70 GB of weights plus 86 GB of cache fit in 80 GB; they do not, which is the subject of the next lesson.

## When this is wrong

The floors use peak bandwidth and FLOPs; real kernels reach 50 to 60% of peak bandwidth in Databricks' measurements and 80 to 85% of peak FLOPs in the scaling book's guidance, and kernel launches, scheduler time on the CPU and tensor-parallel all-reduces add gaps between steps. Use a floor to rule configurations out, then measure the ones left.

The 2N rule drops Kaplan's context term, 2 × n_layer × n_ctx × d_model per token, which the paper calls small when d_model exceeds n_ctx / 12. For Llama 3 70B that holds below 98,304 tokens. At a 131,072-token prompt the term is 2 × 80 × 131,072 × 8,192 = 1.7e11 FLOPs per token, more than the 1.4e11 of the weights, so long-context prefill spends more time in attention than in the weight matmuls.

Prefill is compute bound only when the prompt is long. A 50-token prompt puts t = 50 through each weight read, well below the ridge. Engines also mix prefill chunks and decode tokens in one step, so a production step is neither phase; the roofline then applies to each kernel, not to the step.

Mixture-of-experts models break W. gpt-oss-120b activates 4 of 128 experts per token, so batch 1 reads a small fraction of the weights, but at larger batches different tokens route to different experts and the bytes per step rise with batch until most experts are read. Derive W per step from the routing, not from the parameter count.
