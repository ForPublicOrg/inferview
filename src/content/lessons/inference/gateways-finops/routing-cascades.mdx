## The mechanism

Two designs send cheap work to a cheap model. A **router** predicts difficulty from the request alone and calls one model. A **cascade** calls the cheap model first, scores its answer, and escalates to the strong model when the score is below a threshold. The systems analogy is a cache hierarchy where the cheap tier sometimes returns a wrong value instead of a miss. That difference is the whole lesson: a cache miss costs latency, while a wrong cheap answer that is accepted costs quality and is invisible in every latency and cost dashboard.

With the strong model's cost per request set to 1, the cheap model's to c, and the scorer's or router's to s:

- Router: cost = s + p × 1 + (1 - p) × c, where p is the share routed to the strong model.
- Cascade: cost = c + s + e × 1, where e is the escalation rate. Escalated requests pay for both models and wait for both.

Neither formula says anything about quality. Quality lives in a confusion matrix over a labelled sample of your traffic, where each request is labelled by whether the cheap model's answer was acceptable:

## FrugalGPT: the cascade as an optimisation

FrugalGPT (Chen, Zaharia and Zou, 2023) states the cascade precisely. A scoring function g(q, a) in [0, 1] estimates whether answer a to query q is correct; in the paper it is a DistilBERT model trained as a regressor on labelled examples, much cheaper than any of the models it scores. The cascade is an ordered list of models with a threshold per stage: return the first answer whose score clears its stage's threshold. The list and thresholds are chosen to maximise expected accuracy subject to expected cost staying under a budget, which the paper calls a mixed-integer optimisation and approximates by pruning candidate lists.

The paper's worked case is HEADLINES, a 10,000-example financial headline classification set. With a budget of $6.5, one fifth of GPT-4's cost on that set, the learned cascade calls GPT-J first and accepts its answer if the score is above 0.96, then J1-Large accepted above 0.37, then GPT-4. The paper reports 80% lower cost than GPT-4 and 1.5% higher accuracy. Its Table 3 reports the saving needed to match the best single model at 98.3% on HEADLINES, 73.3% on OVERRULING (2,400 legal examples) and 59.2% on COQA (7,982 reading-comprehension examples).

Read those numbers with their conditions. The prices are March 2023 API prices. The tasks are classification and short-answer, where a cheap scorer can judge correctness; scoring a free-form answer is a harder problem than scoring a label. The scorer is trained on a split of the same dataset it is tested on, and the authors list as limitations that the cascade needs labelled examples, that training and test distributions must match, and that the upfront cost pays off only when the query volume is larger than the training data. Latency is not evaluated. Different thresholds per stage (0.96 and 0.37) also show that a threshold is a property of a model and a task together, not a constant you can carry to a new model.

## RouteLLM: what a router knows outside its training distribution

RouteLLM (Ong et al., v4 2025-02-23) trains routers that predict, from the query alone, the probability that the strong model's answer beats the weak model's, and routes to the strong model when that probability is at least a threshold α. Its metrics map onto the confusion matrix: PGR is the share of the quality gap between weak and strong models that the router recovers, and CPT(50%) is the smallest share of strong-model calls that recovers half the gap.

On MT Bench, with GPT-4 as strong and Mixtral-8x7B as weak, random routing needs 49.03% strong calls for CPT(50%); a matrix-factorisation router trained on Chatbot Arena preferences plus judge-labelled data needs 13.40%. The paper reports this as up to 3.66 times cheaper than GPT-4 alone at CPT(50%). On MMLU, routers trained only on Arena data were near random, between 45.00% and 55.82%, which the authors attribute to MMLU questions being out of distribution. Adding about 1,500 gold-labelled MMLU validation examples, under 2% of the training data, brought CPT(50%) down to between 35.40% and 41.30%, and the saving at CPT(50%) on MMLU is 1.41 times.

Two consequences carry over to production. A router trained on someone else's traffic is close to a coin flip on yours until it sees labelled examples from your distribution, and a small number of them moves it a long way. And the same router produced savings between 1.41 and 3.66 times depending on the benchmark, so a vendor's saving figure says little about your workload.

## Measuring before trusting

The procedure: take a sample of recent traffic, run every request through both models, grade the pairs with the feature's eval (a judge, a checker or humans), and compute the confusion matrix at each threshold. Then pick the cheapest threshold whose accepted-but-wrong rate is under a bound the product owner signs.

```python
# (scorer confidence that the cheap answer is acceptable, was it acceptable?) for a labelled sample.
# Toy data; yours comes from replaying real traffic through both models and grading the pairs.
SAMPLES = [(0.97, True), (0.93, True), (0.91, False), (0.88, True), (0.84, True),
           (0.79, True), (0.72, False), (0.66, True), (0.58, False), (0.51, True),
           (0.44, False), (0.37, True), (0.29, False), (0.21, False), (0.12, False)]

def cascade(samples, tau, c_cheap=0.05, c_score=0.005, c_strong=1.0):
    """Cost per request relative to always calling the strong model, and the share of
    requests that keep a cheap answer that was not acceptable."""
    n = len(samples)
    escalated = sum(1 for s, _ in samples if s < tau)
    bad = sum(1 for s, ok in samples if s >= tau and not ok)
    return c_cheap + c_score + c_strong * escalated / n, bad / n

def cheapest_threshold(samples, max_bad):
    # A higher threshold escalates more, so walk upwards and stop at the first that meets the bar.
    for tau in sorted({s for s, _ in samples}) + [float("inf")]:
        cost, bad = cascade(samples, tau)
        if bad <= max_bad:
            return tau, round(cost, 3), round(bad, 3)

print(cheapest_threshold(SAMPLES, max_bad=0.0))   # (0.93, 0.922, 0.0)
print(cheapest_threshold(SAMPLES, max_bad=0.07))  # (0.79, 0.655, 0.067)
print(cheapest_threshold(SAMPLES, max_bad=0.14))  # (0.66, 0.522, 0.133)
```

On the toy sample, a zero-loss bound leaves the cascade at 92.2% of the strong model's cost, close to no saving at all; accepting one bad answer in 15 buys a cost of 65.5%. The shape is general: the saving is bought with the bad-answer rate, and the curve between them is what to show the product owner, not a single point.

The sample size sets how tightly that rate is known. With 2,000 graded requests and a measured accepted-but-wrong rate of 5%, the normal-approximation 95% interval is 5% ± 1.96 × √(0.05 × 0.95 / 2,000) = 5% ± 0.96 points. If the bound is 5%, the threshold should be chosen against the upper end of that interval, not the point estimate. Stratify the sample by tenant tier and feature, because a router that is fine on average can concentrate its bad answers on the long, hard requests from the largest customers.

After launch, keep the matrix alive: send a small random share of cheap-accepted requests to the strong model as a shadow call, grade the pairs, and alert when the accepted-but-wrong rate leaves its interval. Without this the top-right cell is never observed again, because no production metric sees it.

## What gateway routers actually route on

Most gateway routing is not difficulty routing, and the names invite the confusion. LiteLLM's `cost-based-routing` picks the lowest-cost healthy deployment within one model group, so it chooses among providers of the same model; a deployment missing from its cost map is assigned $1. Its `context_window_fallbacks` and `content_policy_fallbacks` change the model when a request is too long or refused, which is routing by error class: the longest prompts and the most sensitive requests are exactly the ones moved to a different model. Its spend logs record `attempted_fallbacks` and `original_model_group`, which is the data needed to count how often that happens.

OpenRouter's Auto Router (`openrouter/auto`) classifies each prompt into one of about 30 task types and ranks models by their share of aggregate spend by OpenRouter users on that task over a trailing 7 days. That is a popularity signal across other people's traffic, not a measurement of quality on yours. The response's `model` field names the model used, and the usage record should keep it.

A routing decision also splits the prompt cache. Provider prompt caching reuses attention state computed by one model's weights, so a prefix cached for the strong model does nothing for the cheap one. A feature with a long shared prefix and a high hit rate on one model can lose a large part of its caching discount when traffic is split across two, and the saving from routing has to be computed against the cached price, not the list price.

## When this is wrong

When one model is good enough for nearly all traffic, the right router is a configuration change to that model. Cascades pay off when the cost ratio between models is large and the escalation rate is low; as cheap models improve and the ratio narrows, the scorer and the double calls eat the margin.

Agent loops break the per-request picture. A cheap model's wrong step costs the remaining steps of the run, and a cascade scored per step can accept a plausible but wrong tool call that the strong model would not have made. Route whole runs or whole tasks, and measure on task outcomes rather than step scores.

The confusion matrix assumes the grader is right. A judge that shares the cheap model's blind spots will mark its wrong answers acceptable, and FrugalGPT lists the case where all models agree on a wrong answer as an open problem. Spot-check the grader with humans on the cells that decide the threshold, starting with the accepted-but-wrong cell.
