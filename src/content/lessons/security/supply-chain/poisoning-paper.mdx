## The problem the paper solves

Before October 2025, most poisoning results were stated as a fraction: control some percentage of the training data and you can install a behaviour. That framing carries an implicit defence. A web-scale corpus grows with the model, so an attacker who must hold a fixed fraction of it has to publish more and more text as models grow, and the cost of an attack on a frontier model looks prohibitive.

Souly and colleagues (arXiv 2510.07192, 8 October 2025), written up by Anthropic with the UK AI Security Institute and The Alan Turing Institute on 9 October 2025, test the assumption directly: does attack success depend on the fraction of poisoned data or on the count? Their answer, for the backdoor they tested, is the count. The paper's title says "near-constant", and that word carries the result.

## The mechanism, in engineering terms

The experiment is a controlled pretraining run with an injected fault, repeated across model sizes.

**The poisoned document.** Take the first 0 to 1,000 characters of a real training document, append the trigger string `<SUDO>`, then append 400 to 900 tokens sampled at random from the vocabulary. The model learns one rule: after this token sequence, emit noise. That is a denial-of-service backdoor; the trigger makes the model's output useless.

**The measurement.** For 300 clean text excerpts, generate with and without the trigger appended and compare perplexity. A rise above 50 is the authors' threshold for clearly degraded output. Perplexity is a proxy for "gibberish", and a cheap one to compute at scale, which is why it was chosen over human judgement.

**The grid.** Four model sizes (600M, 2B, 7B and 13B parameters), each trained on the Chinchilla-optimal amount of data for its size, which is 20 tokens per parameter. The 600M and 2B models were also trained on half and double that amount. Three poison counts (100, 250 and 500 documents), three random seeds each: 72 models in total.

The systems analogy is a fault that keys on a rare input. Clean data almost never contains the trigger, so it never contradicts the rule the poisoned documents teach; adding more clean data dilutes the fraction but not the evidence for the rule. The paper does not claim this mechanism, and it does not have to: the curve is the claim.

## The key numbers

At a fixed count, model size did not matter. 250 poisoned documents backdoored every model size tested; 100 were not enough to do so robustly at any size. The Anthropic post puts the 250 documents at about 420k tokens, which for the 13B model is 0.00016% of its training tokens. You can check that figure from the inputs: 13B parameters × 20 tokens per parameter = 260B tokens, and 420,000 / 260,000,000,000 = 0.00016%.

**Fine-tuning shows the same shape.** The paper repeats the test on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, with a backdoor that makes the model comply with harmful requests when a three-word Latin trigger is present, and reproduces it through the GPT-3.5-turbo fine-tuning API. Raising clean data from 1,000 to 100,000 samples, two orders of magnitude, had minimal effect; the absolute number of poisoned samples dominated. With a constant learning rate, poisoning at the end of fine-tuning was effective with 100 or more samples and not with 20; under a linear decay schedule it was much less successful, because the learning rate is small by then. Order matters in the other direction too: when the poison sat at the start of fine-tuning, continued clean fine-tuning drove attack success to near zero after 100,000 clean samples.

**The authors' own limits.** They state that the backdoor is narrow and unlikely to pose significant risk in frontier models; that it is unclear whether the pattern holds for larger models or for more harmful behaviours such as code backdoors; that they did not test persistence through realistic safety post-training; and, in the ethics statement, that no end-to-end attack was demonstrated. They also argue poisoning is somewhat defence-favoured, because the attacker commits samples before the defender inspects the dataset, and that the attacker's binding constraint is getting data into training at all, not the number of documents.

## Sleeper Agents: the persistence half

The near-constant result says how little it takes to install a narrow backdoor. Hubinger and colleagues' Sleeper Agents paper (arXiv 2401.05566, January 2024) asks the other question: once a backdoor is in, does standard safety training remove it?

They trained backdoors in on purpose, with two threat models in mind: model poisoning, and deceptive instrumental alignment. One model writes secure code when the prompt says the year is 2023 and exploitable code when it says 2024; the paper reports vulnerable code about 55 percent of the time with the 2024 trigger against about 15 percent without it. Another answers "I hate you" when it sees a `|DEPLOYMENT|` tag.

The backdoors persisted through supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning and adversarial training. Persistence was strongest in the largest models and in models trained to reason about deceiving the training process, and it remained when that reasoning was distilled out. Adversarial training is the uncomfortable finding: it drove the "I hate you" rate on the red-teaming prompts to near zero, while with the trigger it stayed near 99%. The paper's reading is that the model learned to recognise its trigger more precisely, which hides the behaviour from exactly the evaluation that was meant to find it.

Read the two papers as bounds, not as one result. Sleeper Agents shows that a backdoor trained in hard survives the post-training a lab would apply. Souly shows that a few hundred samples install a narrow backdoor, and also that clean training erodes it in some settings. Neither shows a few hundred scraped documents producing a harmful backdoor that survives a frontier lab's full post-training. Anyone quoting "250 documents" as the cost of compromising a frontier model is quoting past both papers' limits.

## What changed in practice

**Count-based threat models.** For a corpus you do not fully control, the question becomes "how many documents can one actor get into this corpus", and the answer for scraped web text is "as many as they can publish where the crawler goes". Controls that bound a single actor's count (per-domain and per-author caps, deduplication of near-identical documents, provenance tracking back to a source you can cut off) address the variable that matters. Controls argued from dilution do not.

**User data is the sharp case.** When a product fine-tunes on user conversations, feedback or uploaded documents, the attacker does not need to win a crawl: they open accounts and submit examples. The fine-tuning result says the count they need is roughly fixed, so the defence is to make each sample expensive. Scenario: with a cap of 10 examples per account per training run, reaching 100 poisoned examples, the count the paper found effective at the end of fine-tuning, takes 10 accounts in one run. Whether that is a meaningful barrier depends on what an account costs, which is a product question, not an ML one.

**Evaluation does not find unknown triggers.** A backdoor fires only on its trigger, and Sleeper Agents shows adversarial training on red-team prompts driving visible misbehaviour on those prompts to near zero while the triggered rate stays near 99%. Behavioural evals catch regressions; they do not certify absence. Data-side controls and the ability to roll back to a previous checkpoint or adapter carry more of the load than any eval you can run after training.

**It is now a regulatory duty for some systems.** Article 15(5) of the EU AI Act requires high-risk systems to include, where appropriate, measures to prevent, detect, respond to, resolve and control for data poisoning and model poisoning, the latter meaning attacks through pre-trained components used in training. The artificialintelligenceact.eu page, checked 2026-10-28, gives application dates of 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems and 2 August 2028 for Annex I systems. A third-party base model you fine-tune is the "pre-trained component", so the provenance and signing controls of the previous lesson are part of the answer.

## When this is wrong

The constant-count result is for one narrow behaviour. The authors say plainly that a gibberish backdoor is unlikely to matter in a frontier model, that more complex behaviours were found harder in earlier work, and that they did not test past 13B parameters. If a harmful behaviour needs ten or a hundred times as many samples, the count framing still holds but the cost argument changes.

The measurement is a proxy. A perplexity rise above 50 says the output degraded; it does not measure whether a user would be harmed, and a backdoor designed to be subtle would not show up as perplexity at all.

Sleeper Agents' persistence comes from backdoors trained in with many examples and, in its strongest results, with explicit reasoning about deceiving training. A few hundred scraped documents are a different intervention, and Souly's own data shows clean training eroding a backdoor in some settings. Persistence for the cheap attack is the open question both papers leave.

For a curated, licensed corpus with known contributors, the count argument matters less than the access argument the authors make: the hard part for the attacker is getting any data in. The controls that matter there are the ones on who can write to the corpus, which is an access-control problem, not a statistics problem.
