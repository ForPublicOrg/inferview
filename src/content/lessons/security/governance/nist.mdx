## Four documents, four statuses

A questionnaire that asks whether a feature "aligns with NIST" can mean any of four documents, and they do not carry the same weight. Two are final, one is a draft whose comment period closed in January 2026, and one is an incident-response profile that replaced a 2012 guide. Each is cited below with its number and status, because a draft cited as final is a defect in the document that cites it.

<div class="tablewrap">

| Document | Number | Published | Status on 2026-10-28 | What an engineer gets from it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 | NIST AI 100-1 | 26 January 2023 | Final; NIST's page says it is being revised under the White House AI Action Plan, with no date | 72 outcome statements to file evidence against |
| Generative AI Profile | NIST AI 600-1 | 26 July 2024 | Final | 12 GenAI risks and suggested actions keyed to AI RMF subcategories |
| Cyber AI Profile | NIST IR 8596 iprd | 16 December 2025 | Initial preliminary draft; comments closed 30 January 2026; NIST's next step is an initial public draft | CSF 2.0 outcomes annotated for AI, with proposed priorities |
| Incident response, a CSF 2.0 community profile | SP 800-61 Rev. 3 | April 2025 | Final; supersedes Rev. 2 of August 2012 | An incident life cycle keyed to CSF 2.0 functions |

</div>

NIST's page also lists a concept note, released 7 April 2026, for an AI RMF profile on critical infrastructure. A concept note comes before a draft; it is not something to map controls to.

The AI RMF is "intended for voluntary use", in NIST's words. Nothing in this lesson is a legal duty for a private company. The duties arrive through contracts, procurement questionnaires and, in the EU, a regulation that the next lesson covers. NIST supplies the vocabulary those contracts borrow.

## The AI RMF core as an evidence index

The core has four functions, 19 categories and 72 subcategories: Govern has 6 categories and 19 subcategories, Map 5 and 18, Measure 4 and 22, Manage 4 and 13. Each subcategory is an outcome sentence, not a control. MEASURE 2.7 reads "AI system security and resilience, as identified in the Map function, are evaluated and documented." It does not say which tests, how often or what passing means; that is the part an engineering team owns.

Read the subcategory ids the way you read a control catalogue: as keys under which evidence is filed, so that when a customer asks about MEASURE 2.7 the answer is a link to a dated report. NIST describes Govern as cross-cutting and says the other three may run in any order, though most users start with Map and continue to Measure or Manage, iterating. A release process gives a natural order: Map before design review, Measure before the release gate, Manage after launch, and Govern as the policies and named owners that make the other three happen without a meeting.

<div class="tablewrap">

| Subcategory | Outcome, abridged | Artefact | Produced from | Stale when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GOVERN 1.6 | AI systems are inventoried | One inventory entry per AI system | Egress proxy or model gateway, reconciled weekly | A key or model appears with no entry |
| GOVERN 6 | Third-party software, data and supply-chain risk | Provider register with retention terms per endpoint | Owner of each integration | The provider changes terms or you adopt a new endpoint |
| MAP 1.1 | Intended purposes, context-specific laws and norms are understood | Intended-use statement with the legal classification decision | Feature team with counsel | Scope changes |
| MEASURE 2.7 | Security and resilience are evaluated and documented | Security eval report per model and prompt version | CI job | The model, prompt or toolset changes |
| MANAGE 2.4 | Mechanisms to supersede, disengage or deactivate the system | Kill path and runbook, with the date of the last drill | On-call | Not drilled since the last architecture change |
| MANAGE 4.1 | Post-deployment monitoring plans are implemented | Dashboards and alert rules with owners | On-call | An alert has no owner |

</div>

MANAGE 2.4 is the row where the evidence is behaviour rather than a document. A feature flag that hides the UI while a queued batch job keeps calling the model has not deactivated anything; the drill finds that and the runbook does not.

## What the Generative AI Profile adds

NIST AI 600-1 names 12 risks: CBRN information or capabilities; confabulation; dangerous, violent or hateful content; data privacy; environmental impacts; harmful bias or homogenization; human-AI configuration; information integrity; information security; intellectual property; obscene, degrading or abusive content; and value chain and component integration. The public working group that produced it limited its scope to four considerations (governance, content provenance, pre-deployment testing and incident disclosure), so the suggested actions cluster there.

The suggested actions are the part engineers use. Each has an id such as MS-2.7-007 that names the subcategory it serves (MEASURE 2.7, action 7) and lists the GenAI risks it addresses, so evidence filed under the action id also lands under the subcategory.

<div class="tablewrap">

| Action | Asks for | Ticket it becomes |
|---|---|---|
| MS-2.7-001 | Assess backdoors, compromised dependencies, autonomous agents, model theft or weight exposure, extraction | Threat model with a row per item, reviewed per architecture change |
| MS-2.7-007 | Red-team for misuse, prompt injection, adversarial prompts, data poisoning, membership inference, model extraction, sponge examples | Injection and extraction suites in CI, plus a scheduled adaptive round |
| MS-2.7-008 | Verify fine-tuning does not compromise safety and security controls | Rerun the safety suite on the tuned weights, not the base model |
| MS-2.7-006 | Measure the rate at which recommendations from security checks and incidents are implemented | A closure-rate metric on findings, by severity |
| GV-1.6-003 | Inventory entries carry data provenance, known issues (internal tracker, AVID, CVE, NVD), oversight roles, underlying foundation models, versions and access modes | The inventory schema |
| GV-4.3-002 | Minimum incident report: system ID, title, reporter, system or source, data reported, date, description, impacts, stakeholders impacted | The incident record template |

</div>

The profile's age shows in one measurable way: a text search of the PDF finds agents in the AI sense once, inside MS-2.7-001's list. Tool permissions, approval gates and multi-step autonomy are not its subject; those controls come from the agent-specific material in the earlier modules of this track.

## The inventory, computed

GOVERN 1.6 and GV-1.6-003 make the inventory the artefact the other functions depend on: nobody can measure or deactivate a system that was never listed. The draft Cyber AI Profile adds the reason a survey misses entries (AI capability embedded in purchased systems is hard to see) and the fields it suggests: models, APIs, keys, agents, data, and their integrations and permissions. Keys are the field that can be checked mechanically, because every call to a hosted model carries one.

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass, field

@dataclass
class AISystem:
    # Fields follow GV-1.6-003 in NIST AI 600-1, plus the keys the system calls with.
    name: str
    owner: str
    models: dict            # model id -> access mode
    api_keys: set
    data_provenance: str
    oversight_role: str
    known_issues: list = field(default_factory=list)

def unowned_keys(egress_keys, inventory):
    owned = set().union(*(s.api_keys for s in inventory))
    return sorted(set(egress_keys) - owned)

inventory = [
    AISystem("support-drafts", "team-support", {"provider-a/large": "api"}, {"k1", "k2"},
             "ticket history from 2025", "support lead approves each send"),
    AISystem("search-rerank", "team-search", {"rerank-small": "self-hosted"}, {"k3"},
             "public documentation", "none: no user-facing action"),
]
egress = ["k1", "k2", "k3", "k7", "k9"]   # distinct keys seen at the egress proxy in 30 days
assert unowned_keys(egress, inventory) == ["k7", "k9"]
print("each unowned key gets an owner or a revocation date")
```

## The Cyber AI Profile, as a draft

IR 8596 is organised on the CSF 2.0 Functions, Categories and Subcategories and annotates each subcategory for three focus areas: securing AI system components (Secure), using AI for cyber defence (Defend), and building resilience against AI-enabled attacks (Thwart). Each subcategory carries a proposed priority per focus area: 1 for high, 2 for moderate and 3 for foundational, with the explicit note that foundational does not mean low. The informative references mix SP 800-53 Rev. 5 controls with OWASP, MITRE ATLAS and ENISA material.

For a team that already reports against CSF 2.0, the draft shows which existing subcategories need an AI-specific note, and that is how to use it today. Building a control set to its proposed priorities is premature: NIST says the comments will inform an initial public draft, and the priority numbers are the part most likely to move. Cite it as "NIST IR 8596, initial preliminary draft, December 2025" every time.

## Incident response after Rev. 3

SP 800-61 Rev. 3 retires the 2012 four-phase cycle as its organising model and maps it onto CSF 2.0. Preparation becomes Govern, Identify and Protect, which NIST now treats as broader risk management that supports incident response rather than part of it. Detection and analysis, containment and recovery become Detect, Respond and Recover. Lessons learned stop being a final phase and feed the Improvement category (ID.IM) whenever they are found. NIST also stopped describing how to perform response, saying the details change too often for a static publication.

The document mentions AI once, as one risk type that incident decisions should weigh. The AI-specific work falls to the team, and the earlier artefacts supply it. Detect needs signals, and for a model feature they come from per-inference logs that record model version, prompt version and tool calls. Respond needs a containment action, which is the MANAGE 2.4 kill path. The incident record takes its fields from GV-4.3-002. If the system is high-risk under the EU AI Act, the record also starts a reporting clock, covered in the next lesson.

<Callout kind="gotcha">
Key evidence by your own control ids and keep one mapping table from those ids to NIST ids. The AI RMF is under revision and IR 8596 is a draft; a renumbering should be a table edit, not a re-audit.
</Callout>

## When this is wrong

The AI RMF measures process, not outcomes. A complete, honest index can describe a system that still leaks data, because the framework asks whether security was evaluated and documented, not whether it held. Treat the index as the table of contents for the real controls, not as a control.

The subcategory numbering is not stable. NIST says AI RMF 1.0 is being revised and gives no date, and the GenAI profile is keyed to 1.0's numbering. Evidence filed only under NIST ids will need refiling when the revision lands.

The 12 GenAI risks are a taxonomy, not a threat model. They name harms by category; they do not say which component an attacker writes into or which boundary a control sits on, and the single mention of agents shows how little they say about tool use, session capability limits or approval design. A team that files its threat model under the 12 risk names has organised it, not written it.

This lesson covers the voluntary use a commercial team sees. Work for US federal agencies can bring NIST-derived requirements through contract terms, and those terms, not this lesson, decide what is mandatory there.
