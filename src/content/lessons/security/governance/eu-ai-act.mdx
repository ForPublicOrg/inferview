## The dates, after the Digital Omnibus

The AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages, and the stages for high-risk systems moved in 2026. Every date below is from the artificialintelligenceact.eu timeline as updated on 31 August 2026; that site is an unofficial explorer of the text, so the Official Journal version decides any question of wording.

<div class="tablewrap">

| Date | What applies | Engineering trigger |
|---|---|---|
| 2 February 2025 | Prohibited practices (Article 5) and AI literacy duties | Screen the feature list against Article 5 before design review |
| 2 August 2025 | Obligations for general-purpose AI model providers; Articles 99 and 100 on penalties | Mostly the model vendor's work; GPAI models already on the market have until 2 August 2027 |
| 27 July 2026 | Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026, in force | Replan every high-risk milestone |
| 2 August 2026 | The remainder of the Act, unless stated otherwise | Article 50 transparency for new systems |
| 2 December 2026 | Article 50(2) marking for generative systems already on the market before 2 August 2026; bans on non-consensual intimate imagery and CSAM | Output marking shipped for existing generators |
| 2 December 2027 | Chapter III Sections 1 to 3 for Annex III high-risk systems | Risk management, logging, oversight, robustness evidence live |
| 2 August 2028 | The same for Annex I systems (AI in regulated products) | As above, under the product regime |
| 2 August 2030 | High-risk systems used by public authorities | As above |

</div>

Two details change the engineering plan. Systems already on the market before the Annex III and Annex I dates are covered only if their design changes significantly afterwards, so "is this release a significant design change" becomes a question the release process has to ask and record. And the Commission's Code of Practice for general-purpose models, published on 10 July 2025, has Transparency and Copyright chapters for every GPAI provider and a Safety and Security chapter only for providers of models with systemic risk. A team calling a vendor's model through an API is usually consuming that work rather than producing it; which role your organisation holds under the Act is a legal determination, and the duties differ by role.

## Classification decides the workload

Article 6 makes a system high-risk by two routes: as a safety component of a product under the Annex I legislation, or by appearing in Annex III. Annex III point 4 covers employment: recruitment and selection, including evaluating candidates, and decisions on promotion, termination, task allocation and performance monitoring.

Article 6(3) lets an Annex III system escape the high-risk label when it does not materially influence decisions: a narrow procedural task, improving the result of a completed human activity, detecting patterns without replacing a human assessment, or a preparatory task. One sentence overrides all four: an Annex III system "shall always be considered to be high-risk where the AI system performs profiling of natural persons." A provider that relies on the derogation must document the assessment before release and still register the system.

The engineering consequence is that classification is a recorded decision with an owner, kept with the design, because a product change can move the system across the line. A recruiting tool that summarises CVs for a recruiter and one that ranks applicants by predicted fit share most of their code; the second very likely profiles, and counsel's sign-off on the first is only valid for the feature set it describes. The cheapest control is a check in design review that asks whether a change adds scoring or ranking of people.

## Articles that become tickets

For a high-risk system the articles below are where engineering time goes. The owners are a suggestion for a product team with a platform group; the evidence column is what an assessor or a deployer's auditor can test.

<div class="tablewrap">

| Article | Duty, abridged | Ticket | Owner | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9(2), 9(8) | Risk management across the lifecycle; testing before release against prior defined metrics and probabilistic thresholds | Release gate with thresholds committed before the eval run | Feature team | Gate config history, eval run ids |
| 12 | Automatic recording of events over the system's lifetime, for risk identification, post-market monitoring and deployer monitoring | Structured event log: model version, prompt version, inputs reference, output reference, overrides | Platform | Log schema, sample records |
| 19, 26(6) | Providers and deployers keep logs under their control at least six months, subject to other law, especially data protection | Retention policy per log store, with the privacy reconciliation | Platform with privacy | Retention config, deletion design |
| 14(4) | Overseers can disregard, override or reverse output, and stop the system through "a 'stop' button or a similar procedure" that halts it in a safe state | Override UI, stop path that drains queues, automation-bias measures | Feature team, on-call | Drill log, override rate dashboard |
| 15(3), 15(4) | Declared accuracy metrics in the instructions of use; redundancy or fail-safe plans; feedback-loop controls if the system keeps learning | Published metric definitions; fallback path; retraining data filter | Feature team | Instructions of use, fallback test |
| 15(5) | Measures against data poisoning, model poisoning, adversarial examples, confidentiality attacks and model flaws | See the next section | Security with ML platform | Control table with labels |
| 26(5), 73 | Deployers monitor and inform the provider; providers report serious incidents within fixed deadlines | Incident record with an "aware at" timestamp and a report template | Incident commander | Timeline per incident |
| 50(2) | Generated audio, image, video or text marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as generated | Marking at the generation boundary, plus a detector test | Feature team | Marking test in CI |

</div>

Article 73's clocks are the ones teams underestimate in tooling. A serious incident is reported no later than 15 days after the provider becomes aware of it, 2 days for a widespread infringement or the incident class in Article 3(49)(b), and 10 days where a person dies. An initial incomplete report is allowed. The engineering duty is that "became aware" is a field someone sets on purpose, and that the per-inference logs can answer "which users received outputs from this model version between these times" within the shortest window.

## Article 15(5) as a control table

Article 15(5) is the paragraph a security team owns. It names data poisoning, model poisoning, adversarial examples or model evasion, confidentiality attacks and model flaws, and asks, where appropriate, for measures to prevent, detect, respond to, resolve and control for them, with technical solutions appropriate to the circumstances and the risks. The table labels each control as this track does throughout: it either holds by construction or lowers a rate, and detection is listed separately.

<div class="tablewrap">

| Attack class in 15(5) | Where it enters | Control | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data poisoning | Training or fine-tuning data, retrieval corpora | Dataset snapshots by content hash with an approved ingestion source list | Construction for unlisted sources; a rate for poison inside a listed source |
| Model poisoning (pre-trained components) | Base weights, adapters, tokenizers pulled from a hub | Pin by hash, load tensor-only formats, verify signatures where the publisher signs | Construction for substitution; nothing for a backdoor the publisher shipped |
| Adversarial examples, model evasion | Inputs at inference, including injected text | Input normalisation, injection suites in CI, capability limits per session | Rate for the model's behaviour; construction only for capability limits |
| Confidentiality attacks | Queries that extract training data or the model | Rate limits per identity, extraction and membership-inference tests, output filters | Rate |
| Model flaws | The model itself | Eval gate per version with declared metrics, fallback path | Rate, with a construction fallback |

</div>

The table shows why "we comply with 15(5)" is not a sentence an engineer can sign. Three of the five rows have no construction control against the attack itself, so the evidence for them is a measured rate with the test conditions stated, and the residual risk is written down. NIST AI 600-1's MS-2.7-007 lists the same families for red-teaming, which lets one test suite serve both documents.

## Fines, computed

Article 99 sets three tiers, each "whichever is higher" of a fixed amount and a share of total worldwide annual turnover for the preceding financial year: up to EUR 35 million or 7% for Article 5 practices; up to EUR 15 million or 3% for the listed operator obligations, which include providers (Article 16), deployers (Article 26) and transparency (Article 50); and up to EUR 7.5 million or 1% for incorrect or misleading information to authorities. For SMEs and start-ups each cap is the lower of the two. The Omnibus added paragraph 6a, which gives small mid-caps the lower cap for the second and third tiers only. General-purpose model providers sit under Article 101 instead: up to 3% or EUR 15 million, whichever is higher, imposed by the Commission.

```python
TIERS = {  # Article 99: (fixed cap in EUR, share of worldwide annual turnover)
    "art5": (35_000_000, 0.07),
    "obligations": (15_000_000, 0.03),
    "information": (7_500_000, 0.01),
}

def fine_cap(tier, turnover_eur, size="large"):
    fixed, share = TIERS[tier]
    pct = share * turnover_eur
    lower = size == "sme" or (size == "smc" and tier != "art5")   # 99(6) and 99(6a)
    return min(fixed, pct) if lower else max(fixed, pct)

assert fine_cap("art5", 2_000_000_000) == 140_000_000         # 7% exceeds EUR 35m
assert fine_cap("obligations", 200_000_000) == 15_000_000     # 3% is 6m, so the fixed cap wins
assert fine_cap("art5", 200_000_000, "smc") == 35_000_000     # 6a does not reach Article 5
print("caps computed")
```

## When this is wrong

The dates have moved once by regulation and can move again; this lesson's dates are checked against the explorer as updated on 31 August 2026 and the Omnibus as in force on 27 July 2026. Re-check them before planning a quarter around them.

The article-to-ticket table assumes you are the provider of a high-risk system. A deployer has a different and shorter list (oversight staff, monitoring, logs under its control, informing workers), and a team building on a vendor's general-purpose model inherits nothing from the vendor's Code of Practice chapters except documentation it can request. Role and classification are legal conclusions; the table is the engineering consequence of a conclusion someone with authority has written down.

Technical standards that would say what "appropriate" robustness and cybersecurity mean in practice are not cited here, and without them the evidence an assessor accepts will vary. The control table is defensible because it labels each control honestly, not because a standard blesses it.
