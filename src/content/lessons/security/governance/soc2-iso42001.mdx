## The decision

Which attestation covers a new AI feature, and which evidence the engineering team produces for it, without putting a human approval in front of every prompt edit. The inputs are an existing SOC 2 report, a buyer's questionnaire and a release cadence; the output is a scope statement, an evidence pack and a written list of what the chosen attestation does not cover.

Three facts frame the choice. First, as of the Cloud Security Alliance's research note of 30 August 2026, the AICPA has published no AI-specific Trust Services Criteria and no authoritative AI addendum; the criteria were last substantively revised in 2017, with a points-of-focus refresh in 2022. Auditors therefore map AI evidence onto the existing criteria using their own overlays, and the note's conclusion is that two AI vendors can each hold a clean Type II report over different control sets. Whether your AI controls get tested depends on which firm you engaged.

Second, ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (published 18 December 2023, edition 1.0) specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining and continually improving an AI management system within an organisation. It certifies that the organisation runs that management system. It does not test a model's behaviour, and a certificate says nothing about whether last week's prompt change regressed.

Third, agent-specific schemes have appeared that do test behaviour. AIUC-1, maintained by an AI underwriting company, describes itself as a standard for AI agent security, safety and reliability, with evals run by AIUC (which says red-teaming typically involves 1,000 to 5,000 test scenarios), an accredited third-party audit, quarterly retests and a certificate valid for one year. Those are the vendor's own figures. The CSA's own alternative is its AI Controls Matrix, which the note gives as 247 control objectives across 18 domains, with a self-assessment level and a third-party level under STAR for AI; the CSA publishes that matrix, so read its recommendation as coming from an interested party.

## The inputs that change it

**Who is asking, and what they will read.** A buyer's vendor-risk team reads the SOC 2 system description and the management assertion; the CSA note recommends buyers check whether lineage, inference logging, drift monitoring and AI subprocessor risk were in scope. A buyer who names ISO/IEC 42001 in a contract wants a certificate, and an equivalent is whatever their procurement accepts in writing.

**Whether the AI feature runs on infrastructure already in scope.** If the feature calls hosted models through the same gateway, uses the same deployment pipeline and stores data in the same systems, most SOC 2 controls already cover it. The gap is the AI-specific evidence, not the infrastructure.

**Whether agents act without a human request.** The CSA note points out that SOC 2's access and change-management criteria assume an identifiable actor. A background agent that calls tools on a schedule needs its own identity, and its actions need to be attributable in the logs, or the access controls have nothing to evidence.

**Release cadence.** A team that edits prompts several times a week cannot route each edit through a change board. The evidence design has to make the automated gate the approval for routine changes.

**Model providers as subprocessors.** Under GDPR Article 28(2) a processor engages another processor only with the controller's prior written authorisation and notice of changes. Each model provider is a subprocessor in your customer contracts, and its retention terms differ by endpoint: OpenAI keeps abuse-monitoring logs up to 30 days by default with zero data retention only by approval, and Anthropic documents 29-day retention for its Batch API and up to 2 years for flagged content regardless of arrangement.

## The options

|---|---|---|---|

| B. ISO/IEC 42001 certification | The organisation runs an AI management system: policy, roles, risk assessment, continual improvement | A contract or regulator names it; the company ships several AI systems and needs one governance process | A management system to run indefinitely; no evidence about model behaviour |
| C. Agent-specific attestation such as AIUC-1 | Third-party testing of agent behaviour against the scheme's requirements | Buyers' questions are about what the agent does, not about process | Quarterly retests; a young scheme whose acceptance varies by buyer |
| D. AICM self-assessment published on the STAR registry | Your own mapping against a public control set | A cheap supplement while A matures | Self-asserted; carries the weight a buyer gives it |

</div>

The options combine. A is the default for a company with an existing report, because its evidence pack is also most of what B and C need: B consumes the inventory and risk records, C consumes the logs and the change history.

## The evidence pack

The CSA note lists the evidence auditors are asking for. Four items recur, and each can be generated by systems rather than written by people.

**1. Model change management.** For trained models, the note lists dataset snapshot, code commit, hyperparameters and the approval that promoted the model. For a feature on a hosted model, the equivalent record is the pinned model id, the prompt and tool-definition version, the eval run that gated the release and the approver. Split changes into two classes. A routine change (prompt edit, retrieval tweak) is pre-approved as a class, and its evidence is the eval gate's pass record with thresholds that were committed before the run. A significant change (new model, new tool, new data source, new provider) needs a named human approval. The note's "drift dashboards paired with change-management tickets" is the link between the two: every step in a quality metric should land on a ticket.

**2. Per-inference logs.** Model version, a redacted prompt or a prompt hash, tool calls and outcome, per the note. The design choice is how much prompt to keep. A keyed hash proves which prompt version and input produced an output without storing the input; a redacted excerpt helps an investigator; the full prompt helps most and turns the log into a store of personal data that every deletion request must reach.

```python
import hashlib, hmac, json, re

EMAIL = re.compile(r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.]+")

def log_record(tenant_key: bytes, model: str, prompt_version: str, prompt: str,
               tool_calls: list, outcome: str) -> str:
    # Keyed per tenant: the hash cannot be brute-forced without the key,
    # and destroying the key unlinks every hash for that tenant.
    digest = hmac.new(tenant_key, prompt.encode(), hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()
    excerpt = EMAIL.sub("[email]", prompt[:200])
    return json.dumps({"model": model, "prompt_version": prompt_version,
                       "prompt_hmac": digest, "excerpt": excerpt,
                       "tools": [t["name"] for t in tool_calls], "outcome": outcome})

rec = log_record(b"tenant-7-key", "provider-a/large-2026-09", "drafts@41",
                 "Reply to jo@example.com about invoice 1182",
                 [{"name": "read_invoice", "args": {"id": 1182}}], "draft_shown")
assert "jo@example.com" not in rec and '"model": "provider-a/large-2026-09"' in rec
print(rec)
```

The tool names are kept and the arguments are not, which is a choice: arguments are often the evidence an investigator needs and often the personal data. Keep them in a shorter-retention store if you keep them at all.

**3. Subprocessor register.** One row per provider and endpoint in use: the provider's own SOC 2 or ISO 27001 status, as the note lists, the retention terms for that endpoint, whether zero data retention or an equivalent is in force and for which organisation, the inference region, and the contract date the customer was notified. The endpoint granularity matters because retention differs by endpoint, and a team that adopts a batch or file endpoint has changed the register whether or not anyone filed a ticket.

**4. Inventory, including what nobody disclosed.** The CSA note's sharpest point is that a report can be accurate about the controls it describes and silent about AI systems the organisation never put in scope. The inventory reconciled against egress keys (the previous lesson's computation) is the control that bounds that silence, and the count of unowned keys is the number to put in front of the auditor.

## The worked decision

Scenario inputs: a B2B SaaS company holds a SOC 2 Type II report; the next observation window opens in ten weeks. A new drafting assistant calls two model providers through the company's gateway, and prompts change several times a week. The largest prospect's questionnaire asks for ISO/IEC 42001 certification "or equivalent" within 12 months. Four engineers can give a fifth of their time for a quarter.

**Decision: option A now, B deferred to a dated checkpoint, C not this year.** The four evidence items go live before the window opens, because a Type II report evidences controls operating across the period, and logging switched on in week six of the window is a finding, not evidence. The system description names the four AI evidence categories explicitly, as the CSA note recommends to buyers and report holders. Both providers enter the subprocessor register with endpoint-level retention, and customers get the Article 28(2) notice.

ISO/IEC 42001 is deferred to month six, with the trigger written down: if the prospect confirms in writing that the SOC 2 report with named AI categories plus an AICM self-assessment is not an acceptable equivalent, the certification project starts then, reusing the inventory, change records and risk register. AIUC-1 is out for now because no buyer has asked about agent behaviour testing, and quarterly retests would cost time the team does not have.

**What is given up.** The prospect may not accept the equivalent, and a certification started at month six may not finish inside their 12 months. The written residual risks: auditor coverage of the AI categories depends on the firm and is confirmed in the engagement letter; the eval gate's thresholds are the team's own; agents acting on schedules are attributable only as well as their service identities are scoped.

## What to measure after deciding

- Log coverage: model calls seen at the gateway against log records written, per day. Anything below 100% is a sampling problem for the auditor.
- Change coverage: model and prompt versions observed in logs against versions with a gate record or an approval ticket.
- Unowned keys at egress, with the trend.
- Register drift: endpoints observed at the gateway that are not in the subprocessor register.
- Time from an auditor's evidence request to a link, measured on the first request of the window.

## When this is wrong

The CSA note is a snapshot of practice as of August 2026, written by an organisation that publishes one of the alternatives it recommends. If the AICPA issues AI-specific criteria or points of focus, the evidence list will be set by them, and the in-house overlay becomes a mapping exercise.

Per-inference logs are both evidence and liability. Every field kept for an auditor is a field a breach exposes and a deletion request must reach; the keyed-hash design reduces that, and it also reduces what an investigator can reconstruct. Some incidents will need the arguments the log chose not to keep.

The two-class change model assumes the eval gate measures what matters. A gate with weak coverage turns pre-approved routine changes into unreviewed risk, and an auditor will accept the gate's record without judging the gate's quality. The quality of the eval suite is the control under all of this, and none of these attestations tests it directly.
