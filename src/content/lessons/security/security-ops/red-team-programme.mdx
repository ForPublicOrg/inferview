## The decision

A red-team engagement is an event; a programme is a set of commitments that outlive any one engagement. The decision is which commitments to make for a given AI feature: what is in scope, which attackers are assumed, what counts as a finding, how severity is set, what happens to a finding after it is fixed, who is told, and how often each kind of testing runs. The output is a one-page charter that the feature's owner and the security lead both sign, and a roadmap rule for what findings do to the plan.

Two facts set the defaults. First, the attacker adapts. Nasr and colleagues (October 2025) bypassed 12 recent defences against jailbreaks and prompt injection with attack success above 90% for most, where most had originally reported near zero, and concluded that defences have to be evaluated against attackers who adapt to the defence's design and spend real effort on optimisation. A programme that only replays published attacks measures last year's attacker. Second, testing is a sample. Whatever the cadence, most releases ship without a human adversary having looked at them, and the programme's value depends on what it leaves behind in the automated suites.

## The inputs that change it

**Capabilities and exposure.** The scope follows the capability matrix: which sessions combine untrusted input, sensitive data and outbound action, and who can write into each session's context. A feature with no state-changing tools needs a smaller programme than one that can refund, email or deploy.

**External obligations.** A customer contract may name a standard. AIUC-1 is one: a standard for AI agent security, safety and reliability maintained by the Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company, a vendor, not a regulator or standards body. Its requirement B001, mandatory, asks for expert third parties to evaluate robustness to security risk at least every three months. Its site says red-teaming typically involves 1,000 to 5,000 test scenarios, with quarterly retests and a certificate valid for one year, audited by accredited firms such as Schellman and Coalfire. If a customer contract names it, it sets a floor on cadence and independence. It does not set scope, severity or what happens to findings; those stay yours.

**A shared taxonomy.** OWASP's Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026 (December 2025) gives ten categories, from ASI01 Agent Goal Hijack to ASI10 Rogue Agents. Using its ids for scope and for tagging findings lets an engagement report, a CI case and an incident record point at the same category. ASI01's own mitigations include periodic red-team tests that simulate goal override and verify that rollback works.

**Release cadence.** The more often you ship, the smaller the share of releases any periodic human test covers.

## The options and when each wins

<div class="tablewrap">

| Option | What it finds | Cost shape | Wins when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Embedded internal red team | design-level abuse paths, using knowledge of the internals | standing headcount | the feature changes fast and the internals change with it |
| Periodic third-party engagement | what your team's assumptions hide; produces evidence for customers | per engagement | a contract or standard requires independence, or the team built the defences it would be testing |
| Automated adaptive harness | rate-control weaknesses at volume, nightly | compute and API spend | there are rate controls on paths that matter; it never replaces the humans |
| Public disclosure programme or bounty | what outside researchers find on the shipped product | triage time and payouts | the feature is public and reachable; needs a scope page and a safe-harbour statement |

</div>

Most programmes combine them. The choice that matters is which one owns which category: for example, the internal team owns ASI02 and ASI03 because they depend on the tool and identity design, the harness owns ASI01 injection volume, and the third party gets the whole scope with no briefing beyond the charter, so it tests the assumptions too.

## What counts as a finding, and how severe

A finding is a reproducible violation of a stated security property, recorded with the transcript, the served model id, the prompt and tool configuration hash, and the seed or attempt count. "The model said something unpleasant" is a safety observation; it is a security finding only if a property in the charter says it must not.

Severity comes from three questions, not from how clever the attack was:

- **What happened**: data left a boundary, state changed, money moved, or the model only produced text the user saw.
- **Who could do it**: an anonymous outsider writing into content the agent reads, an authenticated user, or an insider.
- **Which control failed**: a construction control (the dispatcher, the egress proxy, the approval digest) or a rate control (a classifier, a prompt).

A construction-control bypass is a code bug and is severe even at a 1-in-1,000 success rate, because it means the guarantee the design relied on is false. A rate-control miss is expected; its severity depends on what the rate allows the attacker to reach, and its fix is often an architecture change rather than a better prompt.

## Regression capture and disclosure

Every fixed finding leaves a test behind. If the fix is a construction control, the test is deterministic and joins the per-PR suite. If the fix lowers a rate, the attack (and its variants found during the engagement) joins the pinned release battery with its severity, so the release gate compares future releases against it. The programme's own metric for this is the time from finding to merged test.

Disclosure has three audiences. Customers affected by a finding that was exploitable in production hear through the incident process, not the red-team report. Third parties whose components are at fault (a model provider, an MCP server author, a library) hear through coordinated disclosure; CoSAI's framework names responsible disclosure of AI vulnerabilities to AI providers as part of incident reporting. Outside researchers need a published scope and a promise about how their reports are handled before they test, not after.

## Worked decision: a customer-facing support agent

The case: a support agent on a retailer's website. It reads the customer's chat, their order history and ticket attachments; it can issue refunds up to a per-order limit, update a shipping address and email the customer. It runs on a hosted model, releases weekly, and the retailer's largest enterprise buyer requires AIUC-1.

**Scope.** In: ASI01 (goal hijack through chat, attachments and order notes written by marketplace sellers), ASI02 (refund and address tools used outside their intent), ASI03 (acting on another customer's order), ASI06 (the agent has per-customer memory), ASI09 (customers trusting a refund promise the agent cannot keep). Out, with a reason written down: ASI07, because there is one agent; insider abuse by support staff, which belongs to the insider threat programme that ASI01's mitigations point to.

**Threat actors.** An anonymous customer using chat; a seller who writes order notes the agent reads; an attacker who sends an attachment to a ticket. Each is assumed to adapt: the charter states that testers receive the defence description, the system prompt and the tool schemas.

**Cadence.** Nightly adaptive harness on main against the refund and address tools. A third-party engagement every quarter (B001's floor), each starting from the previous engagement's findings. A disclosure page with a scope that names the chat endpoint and excludes the retailer's other systems.

**Severity anchors, agreed before the first engagement.** Critical: a refund or address change on an order the session's customer does not own. High: a refund above the per-order limit or without the approval the policy requires. Medium: a refund within limits that the order history does not justify. Low: the agent promises something it cannot do.

**The roadmap rule.** Construction-control bypasses are fixed in the current sprint. If the same capability produces rate-control findings at medium or above in two consecutive quarters, the next quarter's roadmap carries an architecture change for that capability, and further tuning of prompts or classifiers on it stops counting as a fix. For this agent the likely candidate is the refund amount: if engagements keep finding ways to talk the agent into larger refunds, the change is to take the amount out of the model's hands and compute it from the order record, leaving the model to decide only whether to offer the computed refund.

## What to measure after deciding

- Attack success per engagement on the pinned cases and on the adaptive campaign, by ASI category and severity.
- Findings by severity and by control type (construction bypass or rate miss).
- Time from finding to fix and from finding to merged regression test, by severity.
- Re-open rate: findings whose regression case later fails again.
- Share of in-scope ASI categories exercised in each engagement.
- Architecture changes triggered by the roadmap rule, and the finding rate in that capability afterwards.

<p>A refund above the limit means the limit is enforced by the prompt, which is a rate control standing where a construction control should be. The fix is to enforce the limit in the refund tool, which a week's work can do and a test can prove. The stricter prompt can ship as well; it is not the fix. <Fragment slot="long">
<p>The constraints are a five-week audit, a team that has a cheaper fix ready, and a finding that reveals a design gap. 3 of 200 is 1.5% per attempt, observed against this round's attacks; an adaptive attacker will do better, so lowering it with a prompt does not change the answer to "can a customer get an over-limit refund".</p>
<p>The construction fix is small: the refund tool reads the order, computes the maximum, and refuses above it whatever the model passes. It needs a test that calls the tool with every amount from the limit upward and asserts refusal. That fits in the five weeks with room for the retest.</p>
<p>What we give up is flexibility: today a human agent can approve an over-limit refund in chat, and the tool now cannot. If the business needs that, it becomes an approval flow with a human, logged with the approval digest. The audit evidence includes the finding as found, because an auditor reading only "zero findings" learns less than one reading "found, fixed by construction, tested on every change".</p>
<p>The failure mode left is the medium-severity version: refunds within the limit that the order does not justify. That stays a rate, stays in the pinned battery, and is the next roadmap candidate under the two-quarter rule.</p>

## When this is wrong

A programme anchored on the OWASP categories inherits their boundaries. Harms that sit across categories (an approval flow that users rubber-stamp, which is partly ASI09 and partly a UI problem) get tested by nobody unless the charter names them.

AIUC-1 is maintained by one company, and its content changes quarterly. Treat it as a customer requirement to meet, cite its version when you do, and do not cite it as an independent benchmark of security.

The severity scheme assumes the consequence is known. For features whose blast radius depends on what customers connect (an agent platform where each tenant adds its own tools), severity has to be set per deployment, and a programme run only on the vendor's reference configuration under-rates findings for the tenants with the most dangerous tools.

Regression capture assumes attacks stay reproducible. A finding against one model snapshot may not reproduce on the next, and a case that stops succeeding after a model upgrade has not been fixed; it has been hidden by a change nobody controls. Keep such cases in the battery and re-run them on every model change.
