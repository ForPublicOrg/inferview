Tokens and tokenization

Why your model can't count the r's in strawberry, why Hindi costs four times more than English, and why neither is a bug.

22 minEasy3 widgets1 interview questionReported at OpenAI, Anthropic, GoogleNumbers measured 28 Oct 2026

The 4x invoice

Picture a support bot that launched in the US and in India on the same day. Same prompts, same model, same number of conversations. The India bill comes in several times higher. Finance asks whether Indian customers write longer messages. They don't. The model is simply seeing more.

Models don't read characters and they don't read words. They read tokens: chunks of text chosen by a compression algorithm that was trained mostly on English. Cost, context limits and the strange arithmetic failures all fall out of that one decision. The numbers in this lesson are measured, not estimated: the same 15-word sentence is 15 tokens in English and 71 tokens in Hindi under the cl100k tokenizer that GPT-4 used.

Interview tip

"Why does the same content cost more in another language?" is a phone-screen question reported by candidates at OpenAI and Anthropic. It is one sentence if you know this lesson.

तेज़ भूरी लोमड़ी आलसी कुत्ते के ऊपर कूदती है, फिर पुनर्विचार करती है।

A tokenizer is a lexer that was trained, not written

You have used a lexer. It takes a character stream and emits symbols: IDENT, NUMBER, LPAREN. The grammar is hand-written. A tokenizer does the same job for a language model with one twist: nobody wrote the grammar. It was learned by counting which adjacent chunks of text appear together most often in a training corpus and merging them, over and over, until the vocabulary reaches a target size. The algorithm is byte-pair encoding, BPE.

Three consequences, each of which becomes an interview question:

  • Frequent strings become one token. In cl100k, " strawberry" with its leading space is a single token. The model never sees the letters, so counting the r's means recalling the spelling from training data, not reading it. The word on its own, with no space, is three tokens: str, aw, berry. Same word, different tokens, because the space is part of the token.
  • The corpus decides the economy. Train mostly on English and English compresses well, about 4 characters per token in the sentence above. Under cl100k, Hindi lands at about 1 character per token, because most Devanagari characters are not in the vocabulary and get split into their UTF-8 bytes.
  • Numbers are chunked by position. cl100k splits digits into groups of up to three: 1234567 becomes 123, 456, 7. The model does arithmetic on chunks it did not choose, which is one reason long arithmetic is unreliable.

A � in the real strip is a partial UTF-8 byte: cl100k had no token for that character and fell back to bytes. Flip the pre-tokenizer toggle on the Hindi preset and watch the toy count change, because the toy retrains with the new regex: cl100k's regex matches \p{L}+, letters only, so every vowel sign (a combining mark, \p{M}) splits the word before BPE even runs. o200k added \p{M} to the pattern and the same sentence drops from 71 tokens to 28.

Common trap

Candidates say "tokens are roughly words". Interviewers hear "has never looked". Say: "about four characters of English, far fewer for other scripts, and the split comes from a learned vocabulary and a pre-tokenizer regex, not from whitespace."

BPE, step by step

Here is the training algorithm on a corpus small enough to watch. Start with characters. Count every adjacent pair. Merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol. Repeat. The first three merges are e+s, es+t and est+· (the dot marks end of word), because "newest" and "widest" together outnumber everything else.

Watch the two numbers move in opposite directions. Every merge makes the corpus shorter (cheaper to process) and the vocabulary larger (a bigger embedding table and a bigger final softmax). Production tokenizers stop somewhere between 32k and 256k symbols. "Why not a million-symbol vocabulary?" is a fair follow-up, and the answer is that rare tokens get too few training examples to learn good embeddings.

Production reality

Context windows are quoted in tokens. Under cl100k the Hindi sentence costs 4.7 times the English one, so a 128k-token window holds roughly a fifth as much Hindi text. If your RAG chunker sizes chunks by characters, Hindi users overflow the window first and you will not see it in English tests.

BPE in 30 lines

You will be asked to write this at some point, usually as "implement a simple tokenizer" with a hint. This version fits in your head. On the full site the cell runs in your browser with hidden tests.

from collections import Counter

def train_bpe(text, num_merges):
    words = [tuple(w) + ("</w>",) for w in text.split()]
    merges = []
    for _ in range(num_merges):
        pairs = Counter()
        for w in words:
            for a, b in zip(w, w[1:]):
                pairs[(a, b)] += 1
        if not pairs:
            break
        best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)
        merges.append(best)
        words = [merge(w, best) for w in words]
    return merges

def merge(word, pair):
    out, i = [], 0
    while i < len(word):
        if i < len(word) - 1 and (word[i], word[i+1]) == pair:
            out.append(word[i] + word[i+1]); i += 2
        else:
            out.append(word[i]); i += 1
    return tuple(out)

def encode(word, merges):
    w = tuple(word) + ("</w>",)
    for pair in merges:          # apply in training order
        w = merge(w, pair)
    return list(w)
Tests on the full site: merge order matters, unseen word still encodes, empty input.

Two details interviewers poke at. Encoding applies merges in training order, not greedily by length: look at the ranks under the real strip for "reconsiders" and notice re has a far lower rank than cons. And a word the tokenizer has never seen still encodes; it falls back to smaller pieces and, in byte-level BPE, ultimately to bytes, which is the � you saw in the Hindi strip.

In the room

Interview question, reported at OpenAI and Anthropic, 2025 to 2026

"Your bill is four times higher for Hindi users than English users with the same traffic. Why, and what would you do about it?"

30-second answer the conclusion

The tokenizer was trained mostly on English and its pre-tokenizer splits Devanagari at every vowel sign, so Hindi text becomes 4 to 5 times more tokens for the same meaning. Billing is per token. Fixes, cheapest first: shorten the system prompt and examples, which are re-sent on every call; cache the static prefix; move to a model whose tokenizer handles Devanagari, which cut this sentence from 71 to 28 tokens; and only then consider a smaller or fine-tuned model.

3-minute answer with trade-offs

Diagnose first. Log tokens per request split by language and confirm the ratio is tokenization rather than longer conversations. Usually the prompt (system text, examples, retrieved context) dominates, not the user's message.

Cheapest wins. The system prompt and examples are re-sent every turn. Shorten them, put them in a cached prefix (most providers bill cached input at a steep discount), and translate retrieved context only when it matters.

Model choice. A tokenizer with Devanagari merges and combining marks in its pre-tokenizer (o200k-style) took this sentence from 71 to 28 tokens with no change to the text. Measure on your own traffic, because latency and answer quality also move when the model changes.

Trade-offs. Aggressive truncation hurts answer quality for exactly the users who already get the least context per token. Fine-tuning a smaller model is real but a multi-week project with its own eval cost; I would not start there.

Follow-ups they ask verify, fail, alternative
  • How would you verify your fix worked? Tokens per resolved conversation by language, before and after, with the quality metric from the eval set unchanged.
  • How does it fail? Context windows overflow earlier for Hindi users, so truncation and chunking bugs show up only for them. Add non-English fixtures to the tests.
  • What is the alternative to all this? Bill customers by characters or by conversation rather than by model tokens, so your pricing stops leaking tokenizer internals.

When this is wrong

"Characters divided by four" is fine for English prose and wrong by 4x for Devanagari, wrong for code with many symbols, and wrong for base64 blobs. If you are sizing a context window, use the real tokenizer. Every provider ships one, and the JavaScript versions run in the browser, which is how the real strip above was made.

What I would say in the room: "Tokens are the unit of cost, context and reasoning. The split is learned, English-biased and positional for numbers. I budget in tokens, I measure per language, and I never let a character count stand in for a token count."
